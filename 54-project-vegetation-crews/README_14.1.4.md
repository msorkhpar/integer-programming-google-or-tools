# 14.1.4 Risk Grows While a Circuit Waits: The Objective Changes

The crew schedule of the lesson *A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals* reached GridCap's reliability engineer, who asked a question the
model had never been asked:

> Your plan trims C13 in the first five weeks and leaves C12 for weeks 17 to 23. C12 is the one I
> worry about: its trees are growing into the lines, and every week we wait its risk goes up. A
> trim in September does not take out a year of risk; it takes out what is left of the year, and the
> circuit carried the rest. Order the work so that we carry the least risk over the season. And
> a circuit we skip waits half a year more for next season's crews; count that too.

Until now the objective was "risk removed a year", the same for a trim in week 0 and in week 23. The
engineer's rule makes *when* matter, and it changes which plan is best. (GridCap and its seasons are
fictional, generated by the project's seeded generator.) This lesson takes the project from `steps/03`
to `steps/04`.

## The Old Objective Is Blind to Order, So It Cannot Prefer Early Work

In `steps/03` the objective is $`\sum_c \text{risk}_c \sum_k p_{c,k}`$. No start week appears in it.
Any two schedules that trim the same circuits score the same, so the solver returns whichever order it
finds first, and swapping C12 to the front of the season would change nothing it can see. The model
cannot answer the engineer because the engineer's quantity is not in it: nothing a test or a tweak to
the rows can fix. The objective itself has to change.

## Risk Carried Is a Table Indexed by the Week a Trim Ends

The rule, stated as data. Each circuit's weekly risk starts at its yearly risk divided by 52 and grows
by $`g_c`$ dollars every week the circuit waits (a new inventory column, `growth`, in $k a week). A trim
that ends at week $`e`$ leaves the circuit carrying risk for weeks $`0, \dots, e - 1`$:

```math
R_c(e) = \operatorname{round}\Big( \sum_{w=0}^{e-1} \big( \tfrac{\text{risk}_c}{52} + g_c\, w \big) \Big)
       = \operatorname{round}\Big( \tfrac{\text{risk}_c}{52}\, e + g_c \tfrac{e(e-1)}{2} \Big)
```

A circuit left for next season waits until week $`H + D`$, with $`D = 26`$ weeks until next
season's crews can reach it, and carries $`R_c(H + D)`$. `risk_plan.py` prints C17's table:

```text
C17: $405k a year ($7.79k a week at week 0), growing $0.63k a week
  trim ends at week        4     8    12    16    20    24    50
  risk carried ($k)       35    80   135   200   275   361  1161
```

Finishing C17 in week 8 instead of week 24 saves $281k; not trimming it this season costs $800k more
than finishing in week 24. $`R_c`$ is not linear in $`e`$ (the growth term is quadratic), so how
should a model hold it? The options:

| Option | What it gives | Why not, or why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approximate $`R_c`$ by a straight line, a weight times the end week | a linear objective, the weighted-completion objective of lesson 4.5.4 | wrong for fast growers: it undercounts late weeks, which is the engineer's whole point |
| Piecewise-linear segments with extra variables (lesson 6.6.2) | exact at the breakpoints | many variables per circuit to state a table that is already exact |
| The exact table, and CP-SAT's element constraint: $`\rho_c = R_c[e_c]`$ | exact, one constraint per circuit | chosen: the end week is an integer from 0 to 50, so the whole function is a list of 51 integers (lesson 4.5.3) |

The **element constraint** says `expressions[index] == target`, in the words of `add_element`'s
docstring: the target equals the entry of a list at a position given by a variable. With the end week
as the index and the risk table as the list, the target is the risk carried, exactly.

## The Model

Before this lesson the objective was $`\max \sum_c \text{risk}_c \sum_k p_{c,k}`$, with the once,
crew and budget rows of the lesson *A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals*. After, in full:

**Sets.** $`C`$, circuits; $`K`$, crews; weeks $`0, \dots, H - 1`$, $`H = 24`$.

**Parameters.** $`\text{cost}_c`$, $`B`$ ($k); $`w_c`$ (weeks); $`R_c(e)`$ for
$`e = 0, \dots, H + D`$, the risk carried if the trim ends at $`e`$ ($k, integers); $`D = 26`$.
← *this lesson: $`R_c`$, from $`\text{risk}_c`$ and $`g_c`$*

**Variables.**
$`t_c \in \{0, 1\}`$, 1 if $`c`$ is trimmed this season; ← *this lesson*
$`p_{c,k} \in \{0,1\}`$, $`s_{c,k} \in \{0, \dots, H - w_c\}`$ and the optional interval $`I_{c,k}`$, as
before;
$`e_c \in \{w_c, \dots, H\} \cup \{H + D\}`$, the week the trim ends, or $`H + D`$; ← *this lesson*
$`\rho_c \in \{0, \dots, R_c(H + D)\}`$, the risk $`c`$ carries. ← *this lesson*

**Objective.** ← *this lesson*

```math
\min \; \sum_{c \in C} \rho_c
```

**Constraints.**

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k \in K} p_{c,k} &= t_c && \forall c \in C && \text{(once}_c) \quad \leftarrow \text{now} = t_c \\
p_{c,k} = 1 \;&\Rightarrow\; e_c = s_{c,k} + w_c && \forall c \in C,\ k \in K && \text{(ends}_{c,k}) \quad \leftarrow \text{this lesson} \\
t_c = 0 \;&\Rightarrow\; e_c = H + D && \forall c \in C && \text{(waits}_c) \quad \leftarrow \text{this lesson} \\
\rho_c &= R_c[e_c] && \forall c \in C && \text{(risk}_c) \quad \leftarrow \text{this lesson} \\
\text{NoOverlap}\big(\{ I_{c,k} : c \in C \}\big) & && \forall k \in K && \text{(crew}_k) \\
\sum_{c \in C} \text{cost}_c\, t_c &\le B && && \text{(budget)}
\end{aligned}
```

The two implications are **enforced constraints** (lesson 4.4.2, `only_enforce_if`): a linear equality
that must hold only when its literal is true. A circuit is either trimmed by exactly one crew, which
fixes its end week, or not trimmed, which puts its end at $`H + D`$; the domain of $`e_c`$ allows
nothing in between.

## The Change, File by File

**`vegcrew/generate.py`.** A `growth` column, from its own stream, so no older column moves:

**`vegcrew/data.py`.** `Circuit` gains `growth` and the rule itself, as a method next to the data it
reads, and the module gains the deferral constant:

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/vegcrew/data.py -->
```python
DEFER_WEEKS = 26        # an untrimmed circuit waits this many weeks past the season for the next one
```

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/vegcrew/data.py -->
```python
    growth: float       # $k a week by which the weekly risk grows while the circuit waits

    def carried(self, end: int) -> int:
        """$k of risk carried from week 0 until a trim that ends at `end` (weeks 0 to end - 1)."""
        weekly = self.risk / 52
        return round(weekly * end + self.growth * end * (end - 1) / 2)
```

The table entries are rounded to whole $k because CP-SAT works in integers (lesson 4.4.3 covers
scaling). A dollar of rounding per circuit is far below what the risk estimate can claim.

**`vegcrew/planner.py`.** `Plan` gains `risk_carried` (a stub in `steps/03`, now filled from the
objective). `TrimModel` gains three variable families, `trimmed`, `end` and `carried`, and the old
`trimmed` method becomes a dict of Booleans, because the waits row needs a literal to negate, and a
sum of presences is not one. All three are keyed by circuit id:

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/vegcrew/planner.py -->
```python
@dataclass
class TrimModel:
    model: cp_model.CpModel
    trimmed: dict[str, cp_model.IntVar] = field(default_factory=dict)                 # circuit
    present: dict[tuple[str, str], cp_model.IntVar] = field(default_factory=dict)    # (circuit, crew)
    start: dict[tuple[str, str], cp_model.IntVar] = field(default_factory=dict)
    work: dict[tuple[str, str], cp_model.IntervalVar] = field(default_factory=dict)
    end: dict[str, cp_model.IntVar] = field(default_factory=dict)                     # circuit
    carried: dict[str, cp_model.IntVar] = field(default_factory=dict)                 # circuit
```

`trimmed[c]` is $`t_c`$, `end[c]` is $`e_c`$ and `carried[c]` is $`\rho_c`$; `present`, `start` and
`work` are as before. `build` before (the lesson *A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals*) had one block per circuit for its crew
options; after, each circuit also gets its end week, its two enforced rows and its table. The
variables come first, with `deferred` set to `h + DEFER_WEEKS`, which is $`H + D`$:

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/vegcrew/planner.py -->
```python
    for c in season.circuits:
        t = tm.trimmed[c.cid] = tm.model.new_bool_var(f"t_{c.cid}")
        e = tm.end[c.cid] = tm.model.new_int_var_from_domain(
            cp_model.Domain.from_intervals([[c.weeks, h], [deferred, deferred]]), f"e_{c.cid}")
        options = crew_ids(season) if c.weeks <= h else []          # too long for the season: no options
        for k in options:
            p = tm.present[c.cid, k] = tm.model.new_bool_var(f"p_{c.cid}_{k}")
            s = tm.start[c.cid, k] = tm.model.new_int_var(0, h - c.weeks, f"s_{c.cid}_{k}")
            tm.work[c.cid, k] = tm.model.new_optional_fixed_size_interval_var(s, c.weeks, p, f"w_{c.cid}_{k}")
```

The rows that tie the end week to the schedule and the risk to the end week are the rest of the loop,
and the page leaves them to the practice. They are four rows of *The Model* above, each marked with
its label in a comment:

- (ends$`_{c,k}`$), inside the crew loop, right after the interval: $`e_c = s_{c,k} + w_c`$, enforced
  only when $`p_{c,k}`$ is true (`only_enforce_if`, lesson 4.4.2);
- (once$`_c`$), after the crew loop: the circuit's presence literals over its options sum to
  $`t_c`$, no longer "at most one";
- (waits$`_c`$): $`e_c = H + D`$, enforced only when $`t_c`$ is false (the negated literal `~t`);
- (risk$`_c`$): the table $`R_c`$ as a list of `c.carried(week)` for every week from 0 to
  $`H + D`$, the variable $`\rho_c`$ in `tm.carried` from 0 to the table's last entry (named
  `r_<cid>`), and CP-SAT's element constraint `add_element(index, table, target)` (lesson 4.5.3),
  which makes $`\rho_c`$ the table's entry at $`e_c`$.

The once row could not stay `add_at_most_one`: with an equality to $`t_c`$, the literal the waits row
negates is true exactly when one crew trims the circuit.

`Domain.from_intervals([[w, H], [H + D, H + D]])` gives $`e_c`$ its two-piece domain directly, so
"ends in the season or waits" is a fact of the variable, not a row. A circuit too long for the
season gets no options; `once` then reads $`0 = t_c`$, and the circuit waits.

For such a circuit the first piece of the domain is inverted: $`w_c > H`$, so $`[w_c, H]`$ holds no
week. `Domain.from_intervals` drops an empty piece, so the domain is the one value $`H + D`$, and the
model is valid. `risk_plan.py` builds the model of a one-month season with one 6-week circuit:

```text
a 6-week trim in a 4-week season: Domain.from_intervals([[6, 4], [30, 30]]) is [30]
  e_L1 in the model: [30, 30]; validate() reports nothing
  OPTIMAL: 0 trims, risk carried $30k
```

The end week's only value is week 30, `validate()` (which returns a description of what is wrong
with a model, or an empty string) finds nothing, and the circuit waits, carrying its risk until
next season ($1k a week for 30 weeks). The objective line
becomes `tm.model.minimize(sum(tm.carried.values()))`, and the budget row reads $`t_c`$.

`make_plan` changes one line: the plan records `risk_carried=round(solver.objective_value)`.

**`vegcrew/report.py`.** The status line shows risk carried instead of risk removed.

## The Small Season Gets a Better Plan, and a Harder Proof

On the small season the change is larger, and so is a new problem. The planner's 10 seconds are no
longer enough to prove the best plan: the objective now depends on the order of the work, and orders
are many. So that this page's numbers repeat, `risk_plan.py` solves the small season with one
worker, a fixed seed and a limit of 20 units of deterministic time (lesson 4.6.3), rather than with
the planner's own settings:

```text
season small-54: 20 circuits, budget $962k, crews 2, weeks 24
status FEASIBLE: 9 trims, cost $956k, risk carried $3,989k
  K1 111114444444222555500...  C01:0-4 C04:5-11 C02:12-14 C05:15-18 C10:19-20
  K2 7777777999922222226666..  C17:0-6 C09:7-10 C12:11-17 C06:18-21
proved: no plan carries less than $3,510k (gap 12.0%); 341,720 branches
the knapsack-era plan of steps/03 for small carries $4,297k
```

The new plan carries $3,989k against the old plan's $4,297k, $308k less, and it changes the set:
C02 is in and C13 is out. C17, the fastest-growing large risk, now starts in week 0. But the status is
FEASIBLE, not OPTIMAL. The search proved only that no plan carries less than $3,510k, so the plan may
be up to 12.0% from the best. That is the first time in this project that the planner cannot prove
its answer, and it has two consequences the next lessons deal with: the tests can no longer ask for
OPTIMAL on the small season, and a run stopped by a clock may stop at a different plan each time
(the lesson *The same plan every run: determinism and regression tests*).

## Three New Tests and One Rewritten

The tests state the rule without trusting the code under test. `tests/rules.py`, new, writes the risk
rule again as the engineer said it, a sum of weekly risks, read from the inventory rows:

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/tests/rules.py -->
```python
def carried(row: dict, end: int) -> int:
    """$k of risk a circuit (an inventory row) carries over weeks 0 to end - 1: its weekly share of the
    yearly risk, plus `growth` more every week it waits (a row with no growth column does not grow)."""
    weekly = [row["risk"] / 52 + row.get("growth", 0) * week for week in range(end)]
    return round(sum(weekly))
```

The new `tests/test_risk.py` holds two cases built by hand, with their arithmetic in the comments:

<!-- snapshot: steps/04/tests/test_risk.py -->
```python
def test_the_fast_growing_circuit_goes_first():
    # One crew, two 4-week trims. A carries $10k a week, flat; B starts at $2k and grows by $3k a week.
    # A first: 40 + (16 + 84) = 140. B first: (8 + 18) + 80 = 106.
    plan = make_plan(season(1000, ("A", 10, 520, 4, 0), ("B", 10, 104, 4, 3), crews=1, months=2))
    assert plan.risk_carried == 106
    assert (plan.trims["B"].start, plan.trims["A"].start) == (0, 4)


def test_waiting_risk_can_change_which_circuit_is_trimmed():
    # Room for one 4-week trim. A removes more risk in a year; B's risk grows so fast that leaving it
    # for next season costs more than leaving A.
    plan = make_plan(season(1000, ("A", 10, 520, 4, 0), ("B", 10, 260, 4, 3), crews=1, months=1))
    assert sorted(plan.trims) == ["B"]
```

The second case is the requirement in miniature: the old objective picks A (a bigger yearly risk), the
new one B. A third test recomputes the risk carried of a whole plan on the two-crew tiny season.
And the brute-force test of the lesson *The first request: which circuits to trim this year* is rewritten for the new objective: the tiny season has
one crew, so every plan is a subset of circuits within the budget, done back to back, and the test
tries every subset in every order ($`\sum_k \binom{6}{k} k! = 1{,}957`$ orders) for the least risk.

## The Step Added Twenty Lines and Changed the Model's Character

```text
code, steps/03 -> steps/04 (lines, functions; blanks and comments left out):
  __init__.py     4   0  ->    4   0
  __main__.py    16   1  ->   16   1
  data.py        28   2  ->   34   3  changed
  generate.py    33   2  ->   36   2  changed
  planner.py     65   5  ->   76   4  changed
  report.py      16   1  ->   16   1
  package       162  11  ->  182  11
  tests          89  13  ->  132  16  (lines, tests)
```

Twenty lines of code, and the tests grew by 43: the rule is written twice, once in `data.py` for the
model and once in `tests/rules.py` for the checks, on purpose. The code's size hardly moved; its
character did. Before, the model was a packing problem that CP-SAT proved optimal in well under a
second on the small season. After, it is a scheduling problem with an order-dependent objective, and
the proof does not finish. That is a property of the problem, not of the code, and it changes how the
code must be run and tested from now on.

## Summary

- **The old objective is blind to order.** No start week appears in it, so it cannot prefer early
  work; only a new objective can.
- **Risk carried is a table indexed by the week a trim ends.** CP-SAT's `add_element` holds the exact
  table; enforced equalities tie the end week to the chosen crew's interval or to next season.
- **Order now matters,** and on the small season the plan carries $308k less than the knapsack-era
  plan, with C17 first.
- **The proof got harder:** FEASIBLE with a 12.0% gap. Tests move to seasons small enough to prove.

## Reference

| Formulation | CP-SAT call |
| --- | --- |
| $`t_c`$ | `new_bool_var(f"t_{c.cid}")` |
| $`e_c \in [w_c, H] \cup \{H+D\}`$ | `new_int_var_from_domain(Domain.from_intervals([[c.weeks, h], [deferred, deferred]]), ...)` |
| ends$`_{c,k}`$ | `add(e == s + c.weeks).only_enforce_if(p)` |
| waits$`_c`$ | `add(e == deferred).only_enforce_if(~t)` |
| risk$`_c`$ | `add_element(e, table, carried)` |
| objective | `minimize(sum(tm.carried.values()))` |

## Design Note: When the Objective Changes, the Old Tests Change Too

Two tests of the lesson *The first request: which circuits to trim this year* encoded the old objective: the brute force compared against the best
*risk removed*, and its numbers were right only for that rule. Leaving it in place would have failed
for a correct reason and taught the team to ignore failures. Rewriting it to the new rule, as a
brute force over every order, kept its purpose (an answer computed without a solver) while changing
its content. When the definition of "best" changes, review every test that knows what "best" means.
