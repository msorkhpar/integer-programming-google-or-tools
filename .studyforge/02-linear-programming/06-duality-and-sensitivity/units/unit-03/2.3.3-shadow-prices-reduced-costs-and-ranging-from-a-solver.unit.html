The rate case is being negotiated, and GridCap's finance team will be asked, on short notice, what a different 2028 budget would do to the plan's value. They also want to know why vegetation work gets only what the floor forces, and what it would take for it to get more. Lessons The dual LP and what its variables price and Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness built the dual by hand to understand it. In practice you read the numbers from the solver after one solve. This lesson reads them from MathOpt, checks what each one means by re-solving, and finds how far each one holds, in four rules. (GridCap is fictional; its numbers come from the course's generator.)

The model is the GridCap budget LP of lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness, unchanged: spend $s_{g,y} \ge 0$ per program and year, carry $r_y \ge 0$ out of 2027 and 2028, rows $\text{cash}_y$, $\text{crew}_{g,y}$ and $\text{veg\_floor}$, maximizing discounted NPV. Its full formulation is the "The Model" section of lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness; the reference table at the end of this lesson repeats it.

MathOpt Reports Shadow Prices as dual_values and Reduced Costs as reduced_costs

After an LP solve that ends OPTIMAL, the result holds a price for every row and a reduced cost for every variable:

  • result.dual_values(row) is the row's shadow price: the change in the optimal objective per unit increase of the row's right-hand side (its bound).
  • result.reduced_costs(var) is the variable's reduced cost: its objective coefficient minus the prices of the rows it uses, $d_j = c_j - \sum_i a_{ij} y_i$ (lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness). For a variable sitting at its bound, it is the change in the optimal objective per unit increase of that bound.

Both are read the same way as variable values. Here they are for the budget LP, from GLOP and from HiGHS:

The two solvers agree, and the numbers are the ones lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness derived by complementary slackness. Check the status first: a solve that is not OPTIMAL has no prices to read, and on a MILP MathOpt has none at all (lesson Explaining a dual value to a finance partner). clean rounds for printing and turns the -0.0 a solver sometimes returns into 0.0.

Units. A price is in units of the objective per unit of the row. The cash rows are in $k of budget and the objective in $k of NPV, so 0.4019 means $0.4019k of NPV per $1k of 2028 budget. The crew rows are written in $k of crew capacity, so their prices are per $k of capacity, not per crew-month; lesson Explaining a dual value to a finance partner converts them.

Signs. MathOpt's price is the derivative of the objective as you wrote it. The same LP written as minimizing the negative value gives the same numbers with the opposite sign:

So read a price as "the objective goes up by this much per unit of right-hand side", and know which way your objective points. Both OR-Tools interfaces, MathOpt and pywraplp, follow this convention (lesson The dual LP and what its variables price printed both), but it is a convention: a text whose theory is written for a minimization, as MO-book's standard form and the HiGHS documentation are, states its prices for the minimization. That is a reason to check one price by re-solving the first time you use a new tool, as the next rules do.

OR-Tools' MathOpt sample advanced_linear_programming.py reads these two arrays after a GLOP solve, and one more thing: the basis (lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)). Before reading anything it asks the result whether it holds a dual solution and a basis at all, because MathOpt does not require a solver to return them; most LP solvers, GLOP among them, do. Adapted here, on the sample's own LP (the one lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs solved):

The statuses are lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)'s basis, as the solver reports it. A row whose status is BASIC has its slack in the basis: c2 has room, and its price is 0. The rows AT_UPPER_BOUND are the tight ones, c1 and c3, and they carry the prices. The basic variables, $x_0$ and $x_1$, have reduced cost 0, and $x_2$ sits AT_LOWER_BOUND with reduced cost $4 - (5 \cdot 0.6667 + 6 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 3.3333) = -2.6667$. Three basic entries (two variables and one slack) for three rows, as at every vertex.

A Reduced Cost Is the Price of Forcing an Idle Variable Into the Plan

Drop the vegetation floor, and vegetation gets nothing: at $0.31k per $k in 2027, it loses to every other use of the money. Its reduced cost says by how much. The program forces some vegetation work into the plan by raising the variable's lower bound, then raises its value per $k instead:

Two readings of the same number, $-0.12$:

  • The cost of forcing it. Each $k of vegetation forced into 2027 lowers the value by $0.12k, because it displaces transmission work worth 0.43 (the 2027 cash price) to earn 0.31.
  • The improvement it needs. Vegetation's value per $k would have to rise by more than 0.12, to above $0.31 + 0.12 = 0.43$, before the plan uses it. At $+0.11$ it stays at 0; at $+0.13$ it is worth more than transmission and takes all its crews can do, $680k.

So for an idle variable in a maximization, the reduced cost is $\le 0$, and $c_j - d_j$ is the coefficient at which it starts to pay. (In a minimization the signs flip: an idle variable's reduced cost is $\ge 0$, and it starts to pay when its cost falls below $c_j - d_j$. The formula is the same.) A variable the plan uses has reduced cost 0, by complementary slackness. This is how a planner answers "what would it take for this project type to make the cut?" from one solve.

Problem statement

A school district's facilities office solves an LP each spring to split its repair money. Every year some kinds of work get nothing, and their sponsors ask the same question: "how much better would our work have to be to get funded?" The office wants the answer for every unfunded kind of work, from one solve.

For an LP solved to optimality, a variable $x_j$ that the plan leaves at 0 has reduced cost $d_j$ (MathOpt's result.reduced_costs(var)). Its entry value, the objective coefficient at which it starts to pay, is

where $c_j$ is its coefficient in the objective (model.objective.get_linear_coefficient(var)). The formula holds for a maximization ($d_j \le 0$, so the entry value is above $c_j$) and for a minimization ($d_j \ge 0$, so it is below).

Write entry_values(model, tol=1e-9) in entry.py. Solve model with GLOP. If the solve does not end OPTIMAL, return {}. Otherwise return a dict mapping the name of every variable whose value is within tol of 0 to its entry value; variables in use do not appear.

A Shadow Price Holds Only Over a Range


The 2028 cash price is 0.4019. That is a slope: the value rises by 0.4019 per $k for small changes. Re-solving at several 2028 budgets shows how small:






The price stays at 0.4019 from a 2028 budget of 2,980 up to 8,140, and changes at each end. Each change has a reason you can read off the plan:


  • Between 2,980 and 8,140 the marginal $k of 2028 money goes to 2028 transmission, worth $0.43\,\delta_{2028} = 0.4019$. Distribution (2,640) and vegetation (340) are at their crew limits.
  • Below 2,980 there is no 2028 transmission left to cut ($2640 + 340 = 2980$). A cut now comes out of 2028 vegetation, whose floor share must move to 2027, where it displaces transmission. Each $k cut loses the vegetation's 0.2897 plus the floor's 0.12: the price is 0.4097.
  • Above 8,140 2028 transmission is at its crew limit ($2640 + 340 + 5160 = 8140$). The next $k is carried into 2029 and spent on 2029 transmission, so its price is the 2029 cash price, 0.3756. The carry variable is now in use, and complementary slackness says its reduced cost is 0: the 2028 and 2029 cash prices are equal.
  • At 9,750 2029 is full too: it carries 1,500, all the room its crews have above its own budget. Money beyond that has no use, the cash row has slack, and its price is 0.


So the optimal value, as a function of one right-hand side, is a chain of straight segments. Each segment's slope is a shadow price, and the points where the slope changes are breakpoints. In a maximization the slopes only fall as the right-hand side grows (each extra $k goes to the best use still open; lesson Explaining a dual value to a finance partner proves it), so the value has diminishing returns. A shadow price, then, comes with a range: the interval of the right-hand side over which it holds, all other data unchanged.


Look at 2,979 and 2,980. At exactly 2,980 the value has two slopes, 0.4097 to the left and 0.4019 to the right, and the solver returned one of them. At a breakpoint the optimal plan is degenerate (a basic variable, here 2028 transmission, sits at 0), and more than one price system is optimal. Do not read the price at a breakpoint as "the" slope; look a little to each side.

Problem statement


A bus depot's planners solve a small LP to split weekly overtime hours between engine and brake work. Their solver interface gives prices but no ranges, and the union is negotiating the overtime cap. They want to know, for the current cap, how far up or down it can move before its price changes, found by re-solving.


The row's price, as a function of its right-hand side $r$, is constant on intervals and jumps at breakpoints. You are given build(r), which returns a fresh (model, row) with the row's right-hand side set to r. Solving that model with GLOP and reading result.dual_values(row) gives the price at r; a solve that does not end OPTIMAL has no price there.


Write price_range(build, rhs, low, high, tol=1e-3) in ranging.py. It returns (lo, hi), the interval around rhs, inside [low, high], on which the price equals the price at rhs (compare prices within 1e-9):


  • If the price at high is still the same, hi is high itself (and likewise lo is low).
  • Otherwise there is a change somewhere between rhs and high: narrow it down by bisection, keeping a point with the same price and a point without, until they are within tol, and return the point with the same price. Do the same toward low.
  • A right-hand side with no optimal plan does not have the same price.


At a breakpoint itself the solver may report either neighbouring price, so the tests accept any answer within 0.01 of a breakpoint. About twenty lines.

HiGHS Reports the Ranges Directly


Sweeping is slow and only finds breakpoints near the points you try. An LP solver can compute each range exactly from its final simplex basis, in one step after the solve. This is ranging (or sensitivity analysis). MathOpt does not expose it, but HiGHS does through its own Python package, highspy, which is in the course image. to_highs hands the same model to HiGHS, row by row:






getRanging() returns a status and a record with one entry per column and per row, in the order they were added (here, MathOpt's order). The program reads two parts of it: ranging.row_bound_dn.value_[i] and ranging.row_bound_up.value_[i], the lowest and highest right-hand side for row i at which its price still holds, and ranging.col_cost_dn.value_[j] and ranging.col_cost_up.value_[j], the lowest and highest objective coefficient for column j at which the current plan stays optimal. Read the other lines with the same care:


  • The floor holds its price, $-0.12$, from 680 to 1,360. Below 680 the 2028 and 2029 vegetation crews ($340 + 340$) can meet the floor alone, and it stops displacing 2027 transmission. Above 1,360 no plan meets it (lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness's infeasible case).
  • A cost range keeps the plan, not just the prices. While 2028 transmission's value per $k stays between 0.3756 and 0.4097, the same plan stays optimal, though its value changes. Below 0.3756 it pays to carry the money into 2029 transmission instead. An infinite end means no change in that direction alters the plan: making 2029 vegetation more valuable only confirms that the plan uses all of it.


2027 vegetation is in use, at 220, yet its range starts at $-\infty$. The floor pins it. The 2028 and 2029 vegetation crews can do at most 340 each, and the plan uses both in full, so the floor's 900 leaves exactly 220 that only 2027 can supply. No allowed plan has less 2027 vegetation, so making it worth less, even negative, cannot move the plan: the loss lands on the floor's price instead. The program checks this by re-solving with the coefficient lowered:






The plan is the same at every value; only the floor's price moves, to the coefficient minus 0.43, the 2027 cash price that each forced $k of vegetation takes from transmission.


Two limits apply to every range. A range is for one change at a time: the cash_2028 range assumes every other budget, crew and value is fixed, and two changes together can cross a breakpoint that neither crosses alone. And a range is about the LP: lesson Explaining a dual value to a finance partner shows that a model with yes/no decisions has no such slopes.


Summary


  • MathOpt reports shadow prices as dual_values and reduced costs as reduced_costs. Each is the change in the objective as written, per unit increase in a row's bound or a variable's bound, in the objective's units per the row's units.
  • A reduced cost is the price of forcing an idle variable into the plan. It is also how much the variable's objective coefficient must improve before the plan uses it: the threshold is $c_j - d_j$.
  • A shadow price holds only over a range. The optimal value is piecewise linear in a right-hand side, prices fall as a limit grows (in a maximization), and at a breakpoint more than one price is optimal.
  • HiGHS reports the ranges directly. getRanging() gives, from one solve, each binding row's right-hand side range and each variable's cost range; a re-solve on each side checks them.

Design Note: Ranging Without a Ranging API


Not every solver interface reports ranges; MathOpt does not. The fallback is the re-solve of the sweep: solve at the right-hand side plus and minus a step, compare the prices, and narrow down the breakpoint by bisection. It costs a few solves per row, so it suits the handful of rows a decision depends on (the budgets, a floor), not a report on every row. When a solver does report ranges, check one by re-solving before trusting the rest, as the program does: it is the cheapest test that you are reading the right array in the right order.
