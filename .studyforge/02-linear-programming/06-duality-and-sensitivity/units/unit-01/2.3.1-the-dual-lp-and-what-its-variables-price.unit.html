GridCap's 2027 budget is $2,300k, and the planners split it between two programs: distribution work, which returns $0.66k of NPV for every $1k spent, and transmission work, which returns $0.43k. Each program can only absorb what its crews can do. An LP gives the best split. Then the finance partner asks two different questions: "what would one more $1k of 2027 budget be worth?" and "should we hire more transmission crews?" Neither is a question about the plan. They ask what each limit is worth. Every LP has a second LP attached to it, the dual, whose variables are exactly those values. This lesson builds it by hand, in four rules, and then lets code build it for any LP. (GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers come from the course's generator, not from a real company.)

The Model

We start from the smallest model that has the two questions in it: one year, two programs. In this module an LP is called the primal when we want to tell it apart from its dual.

Sets. Two programs: distribution ($d$) and transmission ($t$). One year, 2027.

Parameters. All money is in $k (thousands of dollars).

  • $v_d = 0.66$ and $v_t = 0.43$: NPV returned per $k spent, in each program. They are computed from the tiny GridCap portfolio: each program's projects' total NPV divided by their total cost, rounded to two decimals.
  • $B = 2300$: the 2027 budget.
  • $D = 880$ and $T = 1720$: the most distribution and transmission work the 2027 crews can do, in $k. GridCap gives crew-months per program and year (2 for distribution, 4 for transmission), and a crew-month delivers about $440k of distribution work or $430k of transmission work (each program's total cost over its total crew-months, rounded to tens).

Variables. $d \ge 0$ and $t \ge 0$: the $k spent on distribution and on transmission in 2027. They are continuous: money can be split to the dollar.

Objective.

Constraints.

The code is the five parts of every MathOpt program (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick): a model, variables with their bounds, named rows, an objective, a solve.

The plan is what intuition says: fill distribution, the better program, up to its crews (880), and put the rest of the budget into transmission (1,420). Its value is $0.66 \cdot 880 + 0.43 \cdot 1420 = 580.8 + 610.6 = 1191.4$.

Two words for what the plan does to each row. A row is binding (or tight) when the plan meets it with equality: the budget is all spent, and distribution uses all its crews. A row has slack when there is room left: transmission uses $1,420k of its $1,720k of crews, a slack of 300. Lesson Standard form, slack and surplus introduces slack in detail; here it is just "limit minus what is used".

Any Nonnegative Prices on the Rows Give an Upper Bound

Before asking what a limit is worth, ask a simpler question: without solving, can we prove that no plan is worth more than some number? Here is a way. Pick a number $y \ge 0$ for each row, call it the row's price, multiply each row by its price, and add the rows up. With prices $y_b$ (budget), $y_d$ (crew_d) and $y_t$ (crew_t):

This holds for every plan that satisfies the three rows, because each row's left side is at most its right side, and multiplying both sides by a number $\ge 0$ keeps the direction of $\le$. (Multiplying by a negative number would flip it, which is why the prices must be $\ge 0$.) Now regroup the left side by variable:

If the prices are chosen so that $y_b + y_d \ge 0.66$ and $y_b + y_t \ge 0.43$, then, because $d \ge 0$ and $t \ge 0$, the plan's value $0.66\, d + 0.43\, t$ is at most the left side, and so at most the right side. We say the prices cover the objective: for every variable, the prices of the rows it appears in, weighted by its coefficients there, add up to at least its value per unit. Covering prices turn the right side, $2300\, y_b + 880\, y_d + 1720\, y_t$, into a number no plan can beat: an upper bound (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick met bounds from an LP relaxation; this one needs no relaxation and no solver).

In code, a price system either covers the objective and proves a bound, or proves nothing:

Read the lines one at a time:

  • Pricing the budget at 0.66 and nothing else covers both programs (0.66 ≥ 0.66 and 0.66 ≥ 0.43), and proves that no plan is worth more than $2300 \cdot 0.66 = 1518$. True, but loose.
  • Moving some of the price from the budget onto the distribution crews still covers both, and proves a smaller bound, 1,290.8.
  • Prices (0.43, 0.23, 0) prove 1,191.4, which is the plan's own value. No plan can be worth more than 1,191.4, and our plan is worth exactly that, so the plan is proven optimal, without trusting the solver.
  • Prices (0.43, 0, 0) do not cover distribution ($0.43 + 0 < 0.66$). A plan that only buys distribution could beat them, so they prove nothing.

Every covering price system gives a valid bound; some are better than others. That raises the obvious question of how to find the best one.

Problem statement

A city transit agency splits its maintenance money between buses, rail and ferries. Its analyst has no solver on the laptop she brings to the board meeting, but she wants to say, with proof, "no plan can be worth more than this". She has candidate prices for the rows and needs a function that says what bound they prove, if any.

The agency's LP, with $A$ the activities, $R$ the rows, $v_j$ the value per unit of activity $j$, $a_{ij}$ its coefficient in row $i$ (0 if it does not appear) and $b_i$ the row's right-hand side:

Prices $y_i$ prove the bound $\sum_{i \in R} b_i\, y_i$ when every price is $y_i \ge 0$ and they cover every activity: $\sum_{i \in R} a_{ij}\, y_i \ge v_j$ for every $j \in A$.

Write price_bound(value, rows, prices) in bound.py. value maps each activity to $v_j$; rows maps each row's name to a pair (coefficients, rhs), where coefficients maps the activities in that row to $a_{ij}$; prices maps every row's name to $y_i$. Return the bound the prices prove, or None when they prove nothing. Compare the cover sums with a tolerance of 1e-9, since prices are floats.

About eight lines.

The Best Bound Is Itself an LP: the Dual


"Find nonnegative prices that cover the objective and make the bound as small as possible" is a linear program. Its variables are the prices, its objective is the bound, and its constraints are the cover conditions, one per primal variable. It is called the dual LP of the primal:



Look at how the numbers moved. The primal's right-hand sides (2300, 880, 1720) became the dual's objective. The primal's objective coefficients (0.66, 0.43) became the dual's right-hand sides. Each primal row became a dual variable, and each primal variable became a dual row, whose coefficients are that variable's column: $d$ appears in budget and in crew_d, so cover_d is $y_b + y_d$. In matrix form (lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy introduces the notation), the primal $\max\, c^\top x$ subject to $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$ has the dual



where $A^\top$, the transpose of $A$, is $A$ with its rows written as columns. That is all "taking the dual" means: transpose the data, swap max for min, and turn each $\le$ into a sign condition on a price. The dual of the dual is the primal again.

Each Dual Variable Prices One Unit of Its Row's Limit


The dual was built to prove bounds, but its optimal prices answer the finance partner's questions. Raise the budget by 1 and by 100 and re-solve the primal:






Each extra $1k of budget adds $0.43k of NPV, exactly the budget's dual price. Here is why. The dual's constraints, cover_d and cover_t, do not contain the primal's right-hand sides; only the dual's objective does. So when the budget changes from 2300 to 2301, the prices (0.43, 0.23, 0) are still covering prices, and as long as they stay the best ones, the optimal value is $2301 \cdot 0.43 + 880 \cdot 0.23 + 1720 \cdot 0$: it moves by 0.43 per unit of budget. A dual price is therefore called a shadow price: the change in the optimal objective per unit increase in a row's right-hand side, other data held fixed. "As long as they stay the best ones" is a real condition, and lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver measures how far it reaches.


The prices also make sense on their own:


  • Budget: 0.43. An extra $1k goes to transmission, the program that still has crews free, and returns $0.43k.
  • Distribution crews: 0.23. Another $1k of distribution crew capacity lets $1k move from transmission to distribution, gaining $0.66 - 0.43 = 0.23$.
  • Transmission crews: 0. Transmission has 300 of slack. More transmission crews would sit idle, so they are worth nothing to this plan. That answers the second question: no, do not hire transmission crews for 2027.


A Row's Direction Sets the Sign of Its Price


GridCap's wildfire plan adds a rule: at least $300k of vegetation work in 2027, a program that returns $0.31k per $k and whose crews can do $680k. (The floor is this module's own assumption, not part of the GridCap data.) The new row points the other way: $v \ge 300$. What price does a $\ge$ row get?


Rewrite it as a $\le$ row by multiplying by $-1$: $-v \le -300$. That row takes a price $z \ge 0$ like any other, and contributes $-z$ to the vegetation column and $-300\, z$ to the bound. Writing $y = -z$ for the original row gives the rule: in a maximization, a $\ge$ row's price is $\le 0$. An equality row is a $\le$ row and a $\ge$ row at once, so its price is the difference of a nonnegative and a nonpositive price, and may have either sign: it is free. The whole recipe, for a maximization primal:


Primal (max)Dual (min)
row $i$: $a_i^\top x \le b_i$price $y_i \ge 0$
row $i$: $a_i^\top x \ge b_i$price $y_i \le 0$
row $i$: $a_i^\top x = b_i$price $y_i$ free
variable $x_j \ge 0$dual row $\sum_i a_{ij}\, y_i \ge c_j$
variable $x_j$ freedual row $\sum_i a_{ij}\, y_i = c_j$
objective coefficients $c$, right-hand sides $b$right-hand sides $c$, objective $\min\, b^\top y$


The price's sign also has a plain meaning. A $\le$ row is a limit: raising it can only allow more plans, so the value cannot fall, and the price is $\ge 0$. A $\ge$ row is a requirement: raising it can only remove plans, so the value cannot rise, and the price is $\le 0$. An equality can hurt in either direction.


The table is for a maximization. In a minimization every sign in the price column flips: a $\le$ row's price is $\le 0$ and a $\ge$ row's is $\ge 0$. The reason is the same: raising a limit can only allow more plans, so the lowest cost cannot rise. The OR-Tools guide "Advanced LP Solving" has the smallest example there is, a primal with one row and its dual, which the guide writes with the price $y \le 0$:



Solved with GLOP through MathOpt, and through pywraplp, the older OR-Tools linear solver wrapper that the guide's first examples use:






The price is $-2$, the guide's $y = -2$, and negative as the flipped rule says: one more unit of the limit lets $x_1$ grow by 1 and lowers the cost by 2. The two reduced costs (lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness defines them; here, the objective coefficient minus the price of the row the variable uses) are the left sides of the guide's two dual rows at $y = -2$: $-2 - (-2) = 0$ for $x_1$, which the plan uses, and $-1 - (-2) = 1$ for $x_2$, which it does not. Both OR-Tools interfaces report the same numbers with the same signs.


The recipe is mechanical, so code can apply it to any MathOpt model. dualize reads each row's bounds to choose its price's sign, and each variable's column to write its cover row (column_nonzeros(var) lists the rows a variable appears in, and row.get_coefficient(var) its coefficient there):




MathOpt stores $d + t \le 2300$ as a row with lower bound $-\infty$ and upper bound 2300, and $v \ge 300$ as lower bound 300 and upper bound $+\infty$, which is how dualize tells the directions apart. A row bounded on both sides, such as $100 \le v \le 300$, is two rows for the recipe, so dualize refuses it; so does a variable with a bound other than $\ge 0$, which you would write as a row first.


Run on the one-year model with vegetation and its floor, the dual's prices sit next to the prices MathOpt itself reports for the primal, through result.dual_values(row):



The floor's price is $-0.12$: each extra $1k of required vegetation work costs $0.12k of NPV, because that $1k comes out of transmission ($0.43 - 0.31 = 0.12$). MathOpt's dual_values are the same numbers, so from now on we read prices from the solver instead of building the dual. Building it once by hand is what makes those numbers readable: each is a row's price, with a sign fixed by the row's direction. Problem statement


A facilities team plans a week of inspection, repair and painting hours. Some of its rules are limits, some are minimums, and one is a contract that fixes an amount exactly. The team lead wants the price of every rule, and the proof that the plan is the best one, from a single function that writes the dual LP and solves it.


The team's LP, with $A$ the activities, $R$ the rows, $v_j$ the value per unit of activity $j$, $a_{ij}$ its coefficient in row $i$ (0 if it does not appear), $b_i$ the row's right-hand side, and each row a limit, a minimum or an equality:



Its dual has one price $y_i$ per row and one cover row per activity:



with $y_i \ge 0$ for a $\le$ row, $y_i \le 0$ for a $\ge$ row, and $y_i$ free for an $=$ row.


Write solve_dual(value, rows) in dual.py. value maps each activity to $v_j$; rows maps each row's name to (coefficients, sense, rhs), with sense one of "<=", ">=", "==" and coefficients mapping the activities in that row to $a_{ij}$. Build the dual in MathOpt, solve it with GLOP, and return (value, prices): the dual's optimal value and a dict of each row's price. If the solve does not end OPTIMAL, return (None, {}).


An activity may appear in no row (every $a_{ij} = 0$). Its cover row is then $0 \ge v_j$: if $v_j > 0$ no prices satisfy it, so the dual is infeasible (the LP itself is unbounded, since that activity can grow forever) and the function returns (None, {}); if $v_j \le 0$ the row always holds, and the dual is solved as usual. Either way the function must not raise. A Python sum over no terms is the number 0, and 0 >= v_j is a bool, which add_linear_constraint refuses with a TypeError; start such a sum from mathopt.LinearExpression(), or keep a zero term for each row, so the cover row is always a MathOpt expression.


A free variable is add_variable(lb=-math.inf, ub=math.inf); MathOpt's default lower bound is also $-\infty$, so write each bound you mean. About fifteen lines.

Summary


  • Any nonnegative prices on the rows give an upper bound. Multiply each $\le$ row by a price $\ge 0$ and add; if the prices cover every variable's objective coefficient, the combined right side bounds every plan's value.
  • The best bound is itself an LP: the dual. Primal rows become dual variables, primal variables become dual rows, right-hand sides and objective coefficients swap places, and max becomes min.
  • Each dual variable prices one unit of its row's limit. At the optimum, the dual price of a row is its shadow price: the change in the best value per unit increase of the row's right-hand side. A row with slack has price 0.
  • A row's direction sets the sign of its price. In a maximization, $\le$ rows get prices $\ge 0$, $\ge$ rows get prices $\le 0$, and equality rows get free prices.

Design Note: Why Learn the Dual When the Solver Reports Prices


A solver hands you dual_values for free, so building the dual by hand looks like a detour. It is the only way to know what those numbers mean: which sign to expect, which unit they are in, and why a row with slack has price 0. Without that, a negative price on a floor looks like a bug and a zero price on a crew limit looks like missing data. The second reason is that the dual is a tool in its own right: bounds from prices are how MILP solvers prune their search (section 3), and later decomposition methods (section 11) are built on them.
