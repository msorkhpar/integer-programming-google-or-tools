GridCap's planners now split the budget over three years, 2027 to 2029, across all three programs, with crews, carry-over and the vegetation floor. A colleague built a plan in a spreadsheet: meet the $900k vegetation floor evenly, $300k a year, fill distribution, and give the rest to transmission. It is worth $4,108.7k. The solver's plan is worth $4,109.6k. Is the colleague's plan "basically optimal"? How would you prove that the solver's plan is the best, to someone who does not trust the solver, or that a plan is not, and what to change? Duality answers all three, with three facts: weak duality, strong duality and complementary slackness. (GridCap is fictional; its numbers come from the course's generator.)

The Model

This is the module's running model, the GridCap budget LP on the tiny portfolio. It is the one-year model of lesson The dual LP and what its variables price repeated for three years and three programs, with two links between the years: money may be carried from one year to the next (the carry-over pattern of lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over), and the vegetation floor counts all three years together.

Parameters. Money in $k.

  • $\delta_y = 1.07^{-(y - y_0)}$: GridCap's 7% discount factor. A $k spent in 2028 returns $v_g \delta_{2028}$, worth less than the same work in 2027.

Weak Duality: No Plan Is Worth More Than Any Covering Prices' Bound

Lesson The dual LP and what its variables price showed, on the one-year model, that covering prices prove an upper bound. The same holds for every LP, with every kind of row. Write the primal as maximizing $\sum_j c_j x_j$ with $x_j \ge 0$, rows $a_i^\top x \le b_i$, $\ge b_i$ or $= b_i$, and prices $y_i$ with the signs of lesson The dual LP and what its variables price. Dual feasible prices are prices with those signs that cover every variable: $\sum_i a_{ij}\, y_i \ge c_j$ for every $j$. Then for every feasible plan $x$:

The first $\le$ is the cover condition times $x_j \ge 0$. The middle $=$ only regroups the same products by row instead of by column. The last $\le$ holds row by row: a $\le$ row has $a_i^\top x \le b_i$ and $y_i \ge 0$; a $\ge$ row has $a_i^\top x \ge b_i$ and $y_i \le 0$, and multiplying by a nonpositive number flips the direction back; an equality row has $a_i^\top x = b_i$. This is weak duality: every feasible plan's value is at most every dual feasible price system's bound $\sum_i b_i\, y_i$.

Three small functions check it on GridCap without solving anything. reduced_cost is the variable's objective coefficient minus what its column costs at the given prices (the next sections use it again); a price system is dual feasible when every price has its row's sign and no reduced cost is positive, which is the cover condition rearranged:

TOL is $10^{-6}$: solvers compute in floating point, so "zero" means "within a tolerance". The program checks three plans (the solver's, the colleague's, and one that funds transmission first) and four price systems:

Every plan is below every bound, as weak duality says. Two consequences are worth keeping:

  • A gap you can quote. The colleague's plan is worth 4,108.7 and the solver's prices prove no plan exceeds 4,109.6, so the colleague's plan is at most $0.9k short of the best possible, out of $4,109.6k. You know this from one plan and one price system, without knowing the optimum. The same idea, a proven bound against the best plan found, is the gap every MILP solver reports (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap).
  • A certificate of optimality. If a plan's value equals some dual feasible bound, the plan is optimal: nothing can beat the bound, and the plan reaches it. The solver's plan and the solver's duals form such a pair.

The last price line is a warning. Keeping only the solver's cash prices and zeroing the crew prices leaves distribution uncovered: its value 0.66 is more than the cash price 0.43. Prices that do not cover every variable prove nothing, however reasonable they look.

Strong Duality: At the Optimum the Two Values Are Equal

Weak duality says plan values sit below bounds. Strong duality says that the gap between them closes: if the primal LP has an optimal plan, the dual has optimal prices, and the two optimal values are equal. Its proof is longer than weak duality's (it follows from the simplex method of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation), or from a separation argument), and LP textbooks give it in full. What you need is the statement, and to see it hold on GridCap: the program solves the budget LP and the dual that dualize writes for it.

The same number from two different LPs, and the dual LP's optimal prices are the prices MathOpt reports for the primal. That is why a solver can return dual_values "for free": the simplex method finds optimal prices together with the optimal plan, and they certify each other.

Strong duality has a statement for the cases where there is no optimum, too. Weak duality already rules some combinations out (an unbounded primal would exceed every bound, so no bound can exist):

Primal (max)Dual (min)
has an optimal planhas optimal prices, with the same value
unbounded (value can grow without limit)infeasible (no covering prices exist)
infeasible (no plan satisfies the rows)unbounded or infeasible

Here is the third line on GridCap. A vegetation floor of $2,000k is more than the vegetation crews can do in three years ($680 + 340 + 340 = 1360$), so no plan exists:

The dual's constraints (the cover rows) do not depend on the primal's right-hand sides, so prices that were dual feasible with a floor of 900 still are with a floor of 2,000: the dual is feasible. A feasible dual whose primal has no plan must be unbounded: there is a direction in which the prices can move, staying feasible, and push the bound down without end. GLOP only reports that the dual is infeasible or unbounded; HiGHS says which. That direction, called a dual ray, is a proof that the primal is infeasible, and section 9 uses it to find which rows conflict.

Complementary Slackness: Slack Rows Have Price Zero, Used Variables Have Reduced Cost Zero

Strong duality says the optimal plan and prices have equal values. Look at how the gap between a plan's value and a bound is made up, and you get a condition you can check row by row. Take any feasible plan $x$ and any dual feasible prices $y$, and let $d_j = c_j - \sum_i a_{ij} y_i$ be the reduced cost of variable $j$ (the function above). Then

(substitute $c_j = d_j + \sum_i a_{ij} y_i$ and regroup). Every term on the right is $\ge 0$: a $\le$ row has $y_i \ge 0$ and $b_i - a_i^\top x \ge 0$; a $\ge$ row has both $\le 0$; an equality row has $b_i - a_i^\top x = 0$; and dual feasibility gives $d_j \le 0$ with $x_j \ge 0$. A sum of nonnegative terms is zero only if every term is zero. So the gap is zero (the plan and prices are both optimal) exactly when:

  • every row: slack $\times$ price $= 0$. A row with slack has price 0; a row with a nonzero price is binding.
  • every variable: value $\times$ reduced cost $= 0$. A variable the plan uses has reduced cost 0; a variable with a nonzero reduced cost is at 0.

This is complementary slackness. "Complementary" because in each pair at least one side is zero. Note the direction: a binding row may also have price 0, and a variable at 0 may have reduced cost 0. The rule forbids both sides being nonzero, nothing else.

The program prints both products for the solver's plan and prices. slack is the room a row has left, $\ge 0$ for every row the plan satisfies:

Every product is zero. The rows with slack (transmission crews in every year, and vegetation crews in 2027) have price 0: more of those crews would not change the plan. Every program-year that is funded has reduced cost 0: at these prices, each funded $k returns exactly what the budget, crews and floor it uses are worth.

The carry variables are not used, and their reduced costs say why. Carrying $1k out of 2027 frees $1k of 2027 cash, priced 0.43, and spends it in 2028, priced 0.4019. Its reduced cost is $0 - (0.43 - 0.4019) = -0.0281$: every $1k carried would lose $0.0281k of value. A negative reduced cost on a variable at 0 is the LP saying "using this would cost you this much per unit".

Problem statement

A bakery's planning tool solves its daily LP with one solver and stores the plan and the row prices. The owner wants an independent check, run on every saved result, that the pair really is optimal: a list of every place where it breaks complementary slackness, empty when the pair is fine.

Complementary Slackness Certifies a Plan Without a Solver


Complementary slackness can also be run backwards: from a plan alone, it tells you what the prices would have to be if the plan were optimal. Then one check decides whether it is:


  1. Every row with slack gets price 0.
  2. Every variable the plan uses gets reduced cost 0: $\sum_i a_{ij}\, y_i = c_j$. That is one linear equation per used variable, in the prices of the binding rows.
  3. Solve those equations. If the prices that come out are dual feasible (right signs, no positive reduced cost), the plan and the prices satisfy complementary slackness, so the gap is zero and the plan is optimal. If not, the plan is not optimal, and the violation shows what to change.



np.linalg.solve(A, c) solves the square system $A y = c$: here one row of A per used variable, one column per binding row. Both plans below have as many binding rows as used variables (9 and 8), so the system is square. The program applies it to the solver's plan, and to a plan that meets the vegetation floor early: $680k of vegetation in 2027 (all its crews), $220k in 2028 and none in 2029.



The solver's plan is certified: the prices complementary slackness forces are the solver's own duals, and they are dual feasible. Anyone with the model and these 13 numbers can check the proof with a calculator.


The second plan is feasible but not optimal, and the two violations say why. The vegetation crew limit in 2027 gets a negative price: the plan treats more 2027 vegetation capacity as a loss, so it uses more than it should. And vegetation in 2029 has a positive reduced cost: at the prices this plan implies, each $1k of vegetation work moved to 2029 would gain value. Both point to the same move, the one the solver made: do the floor's vegetation work in 2028 and 2029, where it displaces less valuable transmission work, and only the remainder in 2027.


The square-system shortcut needs the plan to have as many binding rows as used variables, which is true of the corner-point plans the simplex method returns (lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)). When a plan has more binding rows than used variables (a degenerate plan, lesson Infeasible, unbounded and degenerate LPs), several price systems fit and you search among them with a small LP instead; the idea is unchanged.

Summary


  • Weak duality: no plan is worth more than any covering prices' bound. A plan and a dual feasible price system bracket the optimum, so the difference of their values is a proven gap.
  • Strong duality: at the optimum the two values are equal. The optimal plan and the optimal prices certify each other; an infeasible primal has an infeasible or unbounded dual, and an unbounded primal has an infeasible dual.
  • Complementary slackness: slack rows have price zero, used variables have reduced cost zero. The gap between bound and value is a sum of slack $\times$ price and value $\times$ reduced cost terms, all $\ge 0$, so it is zero exactly when each product is.
  • Complementary slackness certifies a plan without a solver. Price slack rows at 0, solve "reduced cost = 0" for the used variables, and check the signs.

Design Note: A Plan Without Its Prices Is Unverified


A solver's "OPTIMAL" is a claim. The prices are the evidence: with them, anyone can check optimality in a few lines that do not depend on the solver's code, as prices_are_feasible and slack do above. Production planning code that saves the plan should save the dual values with it, and its tests can check complementary slackness on every solve. That catches a model that changed under the plan (a row added, a sign flipped) much earlier than a planner will.
