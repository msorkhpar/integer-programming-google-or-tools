GridCap's finance partner has the LP's output in front of her and three questions for tomorrow's rate-case meeting. "The model says the 2028 budget is worth 0.4019. If the regulator gives us $6M more for 2028, is that $2.4M more NPV?" "The vegetation floor shows −0.12. Is the wildfire commitment costing us $108k?" "And distribution crews show 0.215: should we pay a contractor $60k a month for one more crew?" The numbers are right; each question reads one of them wrongly. This lesson turns a dual value into a sentence a partner can act on and check, in four rules. (GridCap is fictional; its numbers come from the course's generator.)

The model is the GridCap budget LP of lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness (its formulation is in "The Model" there, and repeated in lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver's reference). The prices and ranges below are the ones lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver read from MathOpt and HiGHS.

Say a Price's Unit, Its Direction and Its Range

A shadow price is three facts, and a partner needs all three:

  1. The unit: objective units per unit of the row. The cash row is in $k of budget and the objective in $k of NPV, so 0.4019 is "$0.40 of NPV per extra dollar of 2028 budget".
  2. The direction: what happens when the limit goes up, other data fixed. For a budget, more value; for a requirement such as the floor, less.
  3. The range: how far the slope holds. For the 2028 budget, from $2.98M to $8.14M (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver), that is from $0.3M below today's $3.28M to $4.86M above it.

So the sentence for the first number is: "Each extra $1 of 2028 budget adds about $0.40 of NPV, for increases up to $4.86M; beyond that, each dollar adds less, and we re-solve to say how much." It states the unit, the direction and the range, and the partner can check it.

The unit is the part most often lost. The crew rows are written in $k of crew capacity, so their prices are per $k of capacity. A partner buys crew-months. Multiply by the $k of work one crew-month delivers ($k_g$ in the model) to convert:

One more distribution crew-month in 2028 adds $94.6k of NPV to the plan. That answers the third question: a contractor at $60k a month is worth hiring, if the $60k does not come out of the same 2028 capital budget (if it does, it also uses 2028 cash, priced 0.4019 per $k). And it is worth hiring for one month at least; for several, check the crew row's range first, because the price holds only as far as its range reaches.

A Price Answers a Small Change; a Large One Needs a Re-Solve

The first question multiplies the price by $6M. The program does the same, for a small and a large increase, and compares each with a re-solve:

$500k is inside the range, and price × amount is exact. $6M is not: the range ends $4.86M above today's budget. The first $4,860k earn 0.4019 each; past 8,140 the money is carried into 2029 and earns that year's 0.3756 (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver's sweep). Adding the two segments, $4860 \cdot 0.4019 + 1140 \cdot 0.3756 \approx 1953.2 + 428.2 = 2381.4$, gives the re-solve's 2,381.2 up to the rounding of the printed prices. Price × amount overstates the gain by $30k. In a maximization it can only overstate: past each breakpoint the slope falls, never rises, as the sweep of lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver showed (0.4097, then 0.4019, then 0.3756, then 0).

Why the price can only fall. Write $V(b)$ for the best value when the row's right-hand side is $b$, everything else fixed. Take two right-hand sides $b_1 < b_2$ and a best plan at each, $x_1$ and $x_2$. Now look at the halfway point $\tfrac12(b_1 + b_2)$ and the halfway plan $\tfrac12(x_1 + x_2)$:

  • the halfway plan is allowed there: every row is a sum of numbers times variables, so averaging two plans averages each row's left side. The changed row's left side is at most $\tfrac12(b_1 + b_2)$, and every other row still holds, since both plans met it;
  • its value is $\tfrac12(V(b_1) + V(b_2))$, because the objective is linear too.

The best plan at the halfway point is at least as good as this one, so $V(\tfrac12(b_1 + b_2)) \ge \tfrac12(V(b_1) + V(b_2))$: the value curve never sits below the straight line (the chord) between two of its points. A curve with that property is called concave. Now suppose a segment of the curve were steeper than the one before it. Take one point on each segment, on either side of the breakpoint between them: the curve bends upward there, so the chord between the two points passes above the breakpoint, and the curve sits below its chord, which cannot happen. So each segment's slope, the shadow price, is at most the previous one's. In a minimization the same argument with the inequality reversed shows the opposite: the value curve is convex, and prices can only rise.

So the answer to the first question is: "About $2.38M, not $2.41M: the first $4.86M earn $0.40 per dollar, and the rest only $0.38, because 2028's transmission crews are full and the money has to wait for 2029." For any change that leaves the range, or for two changes at once, the honest tool is a re-solve.

Problem statement

A hospital's finance office swept its maintenance LP over the equipment budget once, re-solving at many budgets, and stored the result as segments: on each interval of the budget the optimal value rises at a constant shadow price. Department heads now send requests like "+$30k" or "cut $20k", and the office wants the exact change in value for each, without re-solving every time.

The sweep is a list of segments (start, end, price), sorted and contiguous: each segment's end is the next one's start. Between start and end the optimal value $z(r)$ has slope price, so the change from $r$ to $r + \Delta$ is the sum, over the segments the move passes through, of the price times the length covered:

with the sign reversed for a cut ($\Delta < 0$): the value falls by the same sum taken over $[r + \Delta, r]$.

Write value_change(segments, rhs, delta) in change.py. Return the change in the optimal value when the budget moves from rhs to rhs + delta, or None when rhs + delta lies outside the swept interval (before the first start or after the last end). rhs itself is always inside it.

A Negative Price Is the Marginal Cost of a Commitment


The floor's price is $-0.12$ per $k. The second question multiplies it by the whole floor, $900k, and calls $108k the cost of the commitment. The program compares that with what the commitment really costs, the value gained by dropping the floor and re-solving:






The floor costs $100.2k, not $108k. The price is the cost of the last $1k of floor: that $1k has to be done in 2027, where it displaces transmission worth 0.43 and earns 0.31. The first $680k of the floor are cheaper, because they go into 2028 and 2029, where the transmission they displace is worth less (it is discounted) and the floor's range from lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver ends at 680. A price is a marginal value (the effect of one more unit), never an average over the whole amount. Multiplying a marginal value by a whole quantity is the commonest misreading of a dual value.


The sentence for the partner: "The wildfire floor costs about $100k of NPV over the plan. Each extra $1k of required vegetation work, up to $1.36M of floor, costs another $0.12k; each $1k less saves $0.12k, down to a $680k floor."


The negative price also explains two small positive prices that would otherwise look odd: the vegetation crews in 2028 and 2029 are worth $1.3k and $2.6k per crew-month. Vegetation work is the least valuable program, so its crews matter only because the floor forces the work. One more crew in 2029 lets $170k of the floor move out of 2027, where it costs the most. Without the floor, vegetation is idle (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver), its crew rows have slack, and complementary slackness sets their prices to 0.

Problem statement

Suppliers and the regulator offer ways to relax some of its rows: extra crane-months, more budget, a waiver that lowers a dredging requirement. Each offer has a cost. A Yes/No Model Has No Shadow Prices, Only Re-Solves


The partner's last question: "What is the shadow price of the budget in the project selection model?" That is the tiny portfolio's selection model of lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick: binary $x_i \in \{0,1\}$ per project, a budget row, must-do, parent and alternative rows. Ask MathOpt for the budget row's dual value after solving it as a MILP:






There is none. Duality, as these four lessons built it, needs the plans to form a region where small changes are possible; with yes/no decisions a project is in or out, and the value does not have a slope. The program shows what it has instead, solving the MILP and its LP relaxation (the same model with $0 \le x_i \le 1$) at budgets from 7,000 to 7,800:






The MILP's value moves in steps: +123 from 7,000 to 7,100, nothing for the next $300k, +14, then +123 again. "Per $1k of budget" has no fixed answer: the next $100k is worth $123k at one budget and $0 at the next. The LP relaxation has a smooth price, 0.4929, but it prices fractions of projects that the utility cannot build, so it is not the answer either.


For a model with yes/no decisions, answer a "what is X worth" question with scenarios: re-solve at the values the partner cares about and report the differences, as the table does. Section 9 treats sensitivity for integer programs, and section 12 builds the scenario workflow. A dual value is an LP tool; using it on a MILP is a mistake.


Summary


  • Say a price's unit, its direction and its range. And convert the unit to the partner's: a price per $k of crew capacity times $k per crew-month is a price per crew-month.
  • A price answers a small change; a large one needs a re-solve. Inside its range, price × amount is exact; beyond it, in a maximization, it overstates the gain.
  • A negative price is the marginal cost of a commitment. It prices the last unit of a requirement; the commitment's total cost comes from re-solving without it.
  • A yes/no model has no shadow prices, only re-solves. MathOpt raised an error when asked for the MILP's dual values; report scenarios instead.

Design Note: Report Re-Solves, Explain With Prices


A deck for a rate-case meeting should show scenario values computed by re-solving, because they are exact and survive any change in the model. The prices are for the conversation: they say why a scenario came out as it did ("2028 transmission is the marginal use of money") and which limit to ask about next (the one with the largest price per dollar of relief). Use each for what it is good at, and never let a price × amount product stand in for a scenario the meeting will rely on.
