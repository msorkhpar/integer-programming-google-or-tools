  15. LP patterns

Three requests reach GridCap's planning team in the same week. Procurement must buy 20,000 hours of tree trimming from three contractors, and the regulator wants the season's average rework rate at 4% or below. Stores must move spare transformers from two depots to four regions, some through a central hub, at the least shipping cost. And finance asks for lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs's program split over all five years of the plan, with money a year leaves unspent allowed to roll into the next.

Each is a new LP, and each is an instance of a pattern: a shape of model that recurs across industries, with its own kind of variable and its own kind of row. Blending bounds an average. Flows balance what enters and leaves each place. Multi-period models carry a stock from one period to the next. Recognizing the pattern is most of the modeling work, and it is what interviewers ask about. GridCap, its contractors, depots and numbers are all fictional. Three rules, one per heading.

A Blend Rule Bounds an Average, and the Average Is Linear Once Multiplied by the Total

The three contractors (invented for this course):

A contractor's rework rate is the share of its work that must be redone; the cheaper contractors redo more. The decisions are amounts: $h_c \ge 0$, the hours bought from contractor $c$, continuous and at most its capacity $u_c$. Hours must add up to the need, $\sum_c h_c = H$, and the cost $\sum_c p_c h_c$ is minimized, with $p_c$ the price per hour.

The new rule is the average. The season's rework rate is the hours-weighted average of the contractors' rates:

with $q_c$ contractor $c$'s rate and $\bar q = 4.0$ the target. Like the share rule of lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs, it divides by variables, so it is not linear. Multiply both sides by the total hours (never negative), and move everything to one side:

The second form reads well. Each hour from a contractor better than the target earns a credit ($q_c - \bar q < 0$): Northline's earns $-2.0$. Each hour from a worse one spends credit: Birch & Sons $+1.5$, Canopy Co $+3.0$. The blend is allowed when the credits cover the spending.

The program solves three versions: no rework rule, the blend rule, and a common misreading of it, "every contractor must be at or under 4%", which excludes the two cheaper contractors:

With no rule, GridCap buys the two cheapest contractors to capacity, and the season's rework is 6.25%. With the blend rule, the answer is a mix: all of Northline's 10,000 hours, which earn $10{,}000 \times 2.0 = 20{,}000$ points of credit, and the other 10,000 hours split so that they spend exactly that credit. The mix can be checked by hand from the two binding rows:

Canopy Co is cheaper per hour, but each of its hours spends twice the credit of a Birch & Sons hour, so the LP buys Birch & Sons first. The average is exactly 4.00%: the rework row is binding. The rule cost $1{,}703{,}333 - 1{,}480{,}000 = 223{,}333$ dollars.

The misreading is infeasible: only Northline meets 4% on its own, and its 10,000 hours cannot cover 20,000. An average limits the mix, not each ingredient; a blend is valuable precisely because good ingredients make room for cheap ones.

The same pattern is everywhere: the sulfur content of a fuel blend, the protein in animal feed, the average age of a vehicle fleet, the average risk score of a portfolio of projects. The recipe is always the same: write the average, multiply by the total, collect the terms.

The oldest model of this family is the diet problem, and OR-Tools ships it as the sample stigler_diet.py (the OR-Tools page on the Stigler diet tells its story). In 1944 the economist George Stigler asked for the cheapest set of foods, at 1939 prices, that meets nine daily nutrient minimums. Its data gives, for each of 77 foods, how much of each nutrient one dollar of that food buys. Adapted to MathOpt, with the sample's own data:

Sets. $F$, the 77 foods; $N$, the 9 nutrients. Parameters. $a_{n,f}$, the amount of nutrient $n$ that $1 of food $f$ buys; $m_n$, the daily minimum of nutrient $n$ (calories in thousands of kcal). Variables. $x_f \ge 0$ for $f \in F$, continuous: the dollars a day spent on food $f$. Units. The minimums are daily, so the model is a one-day model: its objective is the cost of the diet in dollars a day. The yearly figures in the output are not in the model; the program multiplies the daily optimum, and each food's daily $x_f$, by 365 when it prints them, as the OR-Tools sample does:

$39.66 a year, the figure the OR-Tools page reports; Stigler's own diet, found without an LP solver, cost $39.93. The solver's own optimum is the daily cost, $39.66 / 365 \approx 0.109$ dollars, about 11 cents a day at 1939 prices; every "a year" line is that day's spend times 365. Two things to read in it. First, each nutrient row bounds a total, not an average, so it is linear as written and nothing needs multiplying out. A rule such as "at most 30% of the calories from fat" would bound a ratio of two totals, and it would be multiplied out exactly like the rework rule. Second, of 77 foods the diet buys only five, and five rows sit at their minimum. That is the shape of every corner answer. Give each row a surplus amount, so that each of the 9 rows becomes an equation; there are then 86 amounts (77 foods and 9 surpluses), and a corner is a point where so many of them sit at their bound 0 that the 9 equations leave only one choice for the rest. Nine equations can fix at most nine unknowns, so at a corner at most 9 amounts are nonzero. Here the four rows with surplus use four of the nine, which leaves room for at most five foods. Lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) makes this exact: the nonzero amounts of a corner are its basis.

    Blend gravel to a fines window

    • an average-quality rule is multiplied by the total and written as one row per limit

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A county roads department buys road-base gravel from several pits. Each pit has a price per ton, a fines content (the share of very small particles, in % of weight) and the tons it can supply. The specification says the delivered gravel's average fines content must lie between low and high percent: too few fines and the base will not compact, too many and it drains badly.

With $J$ the pits, $p_j$ the price per ton, $q_j$ the fines content (%), $u_j$ the tons pit $j$ has, $T$ the tons needed and $[\ell, h]$ the window, the average rule $\ell \le \sum_j q_j t_j / \sum_j t_j \le h$ is multiplied out by the total tons:

$t_j$ is continuous: the tons bought from pit $j$. Write mix(pits, tons, low, high) in blend.py. It solves this model with GLOP and returns (status, cost, tons_by_pit): the name of the termination reason; when it is OPTIMAL, the cost and a dict from pit name to tons (not rounded, every pit included); otherwise None, None.

About fifteen lines.

A flow model is drawn as a network: nodes (the depots, the hub, the regions) joined by arcs, one-way routes, each with a cost per unit moved and a capacity, the most it can carry. The decision on each arc $(i, j)$ is the flow $f_{ij}$: how many units move from $i$ to $j$, with $0 \le f_{ij} \le u_{ij}$.




The rows come from one idea, applied at every node: conservation of flow. Units do not appear or vanish on the way; what enters a node, less what leaves it, is what the node keeps.



What each node keeps is set by its role:


  • A region keeps exactly its demand: in − out $= d_n$.
  • The hub stores nothing: in − out $= 0$. A node like this is a transshipment node.
  • A depot sends out at most what is on its shelf: out − in $\le$ supply. It is $\le$ because spares may stay on the shelf: the depots hold 80 and the regions need 70. Written as an equation, the depots would have to ship all 80 spares into regions that accept exactly 70 between them, and no flow could satisfy every row: the model would be infeasible.




inflow(n) and outflow(n) are two small helpers that add up the flow variables of the arcs into and out of node n. The answer:



Check the balance rows on the output. The hub takes in $30 + 7 = 37$ and sends out $25 + 12 = 37$. Northgate sends $15 + 30 = 45$, its whole shelf; Southport sends $18 + 7 = 25$ of its 35. Each route choice is a cost comparison the LP made for every region at once: Valley is cheaper through the hub ($2 + 3 = 5$ per unit) than direct from Northgate (6), while Coast is cheaper direct from Southport (3) than through the hub ($2 + 4 = 6$). Every number here is in the arc table above, so you can price the plan yourself: $15 \cdot 4 + 30 \cdot 2 + 18 \cdot 3 + 7 \cdot 2 + 25 \cdot 3 + 12 \cdot 3 = 299$ $k.


The flows came out as whole transformers, although every variable was continuous. For a network model that is not luck. When every supply, demand and capacity is a whole number, a flow LP that has a best solution has one in whole numbers, and every corner answer, the kind GLOP returns, is one of them. That follows from the special structure of its rows (each flow variable appears in exactly two balance rows, with $+1$ in one and $-1$ in the other). So a shipping plan, a crew assignment or a routing of units can often be solved as an LP even though the units are whole. Section 3 explains why this matters: in most models it does not hold, and integer variables cost a search.

Multi-Period Carry-Over Is a Stock Variable That Links Each Year's Cash Row to the Next


Lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs's split left $230.3k of the 2027 budget unspent: no program could absorb it. Finance now asks for the split over all five years of the small portfolio, 2027 to 2031, with each year's own crews and budget. Without anything new, that is five copies of lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs's model side by side, $s_{k,y}$ for each program and year, and each year's budget row on its own. The new rule is carry-over: money a year leaves unspent may roll into the next year.


The amount carried is something the plan chooses, so it is a variable: $r_y \ge 0$, the money carried out of year $y$ into year $y + 1$. Each year may spend its budget plus what came in, less what it sends on:



This is the cash row of year $y$, and $y_0$ is the first year, which has nothing coming in. A variable like $r_y$, which links one period to the next, is a stock variable: it is the amount held between periods. The same shape models inventory (what is in the warehouse at the end of a month), water in a reservoir and money in an account.




Why $\le$ and not $=$? Money a year neither spends nor carries lapses: it goes back to the utility at year end. The gap between the two sides of the row is the lapsed amount. An equation would force every dollar to be spent or carried, and as 2031 below shows, there are years where it cannot be spent. Lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps meets the same row in a project model, with caps on the carry.


The objective adds up value over the years, but a dollar of value earned in 2031 is worth less today than one earned in 2027. So each year's value is discounted: divided by $(1 + \rho)^{y - y_0}$, where $\rho = 0.07$ is GridCap's discount rate. That is the same idea as the NPV of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model: money later is worth less now.






Without carry-over, 2027 spends $8,659.7k, as in lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs, and 2031 spends only $4,195.5k of its $7,850k: its crews are few (distribution and vegetation can do only $1,785.7k and $312.0k of work), and transmission may only match the other two. The other three years spend their whole budget.


With carry-over, 2027's $230.3k moves into 2028, a year whose budget was full, and is spent there: $8{,}120 + 230.3 = 8{,}350.3$. The NPV rises by $18{,}388.8 - 18{,}274.8 = 114.0$ $k. But 2031's $3,654.5k still lapses: carry-over moves money forward, never back, and there is no year after 2031 in the plan to send it to. If finance wanted 2031's money used, the plan would need borrowing ahead (lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps's practice) or more crews.


With every $r_y = 0$, the cash rows are the old budget rows, so the carry-over model allows every plan the yearly model allows and more: its value can only be the same or higher. The module's tests check exactly that.

The Model


The lesson built three models, one per pattern, and adapted OR-Tools' diet model. Each is complete here.


Blending (blend.py). Sets. $J$, the contractors. Parameters. $p_c$, price per crew-hour ($); $q_c$, rework rate (%); $u_c$, hours contractor $c$ can supply; $H = 20{,}000$, hours needed; $\bar q = 4.0$, the target average rework rate (%). Variables. $0 \le h_c \le u_c$ for $c \in J$, continuous: hours bought from $c$.



Diet (stigler_diet.py, adapted from OR-Tools). Sets. $F$, the foods; $N$, the nutrients. Parameters. $a_{n,f}$, nutrient $n$ bought by $1 of food $f$; $m_n$, the daily minimum of nutrient $n$. Variables. $x_f \ge 0$ for $f \in F$, continuous: $ a day on food $f$. The objective is the daily cost in dollars; the program prints $365 \sum_f x_f^*$ as the annual cost.



Flow (flow.py). Sets. $S$, the depots; $h$, the hub; $R$, the regions; $A$, the arcs $(i, j)$. Parameters. $a_n$, the spares on depot $n$'s shelf; $d_n$, region $n$'s demand; $c_{ij}$, the cost per unit on arc $(i,j)$ ($k); $u_{ij}$, its capacity. Variables. $0 \le f_{ij} \le u_{ij}$ for $(i, j) \in A$, continuous: units moved on the arc.



Multi-period split with carry-over (carry_over_lp.py). Sets. $K$, the programs; $Y = \{2027, \dots, 2031\}$, the years, $y_0 = 2027$ the first. Parameters. $v_k$, $m_k$, $f$, $\alpha$ as in lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs; $C_{k,y}$, program $k$'s crew-months in year $y$; $B_y$, year $y$'s budget ($k); $\rho = 0.07$, the discount rate. Variables. $s_{k,y} \ge 0$, the $k spent on program $k$ in year $y$; $r_y \ge 0$, the $k carried out of year $y$; both continuous, with $r_{y_0 - 1} = 0$.



Without carry-over, the cash rows are replaced by $\sum_k s_{k,y} \le B_y$, labelled $\text{(budget}_y)$, and there are no $r_y$.


Summary


  • A blend rule bounds an average. Multiply by the total and collect terms: $\sum_c (q_c - \bar q)\, h_c \le 0$. It limits the mix, not each ingredient.
  • A flow model has one balance row per node: in − out equals what the node keeps; $\le$ at a source that may keep stock, $=$ where nothing is stored. With whole-number data, a flow LP has a whole-number best solution.
  • Carry-over is a stock variable linking each period's row to the next. The cash row is $\le$ because unspent money lapses; carry-over moves money forward only.

Design Note: Patterns Combine


Real models are rarely one pattern. GridCap's capital plan is a multi-period model (stocks of money across years) whose spend is fed by projects (section 3's yes/no decisions), with blend-like rules on regional shares and flow-like rules on crews moving between regions. Name the pattern of each rule when you write it: the row's shape follows, and so do its pitfalls (the ratio that must be multiplied out, the balance row that must be $\le$ where stock may stay, the cash row that must allow money to lapse).
