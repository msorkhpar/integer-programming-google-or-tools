The program split of lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs is in front of GridCap's finance team, and two questions come back. A quantitative analyst wants to check it with her own LP code, which accepts only one shape of problem: maximize, every row a $\le$, every variable $\ge 0$. The budget officer asks a plainer question: "of each limit, how much is left?". The first question is about standard form, the one shape every LP can be rewritten into. The second is about slack and surplus, the room each row has left. Both turn out to be the same idea, and the next two modules (05, the simplex method, and 06, duality) are written in their terms. Four rules, one per heading.

GridCap is fictional; the numbers are the 2027 split of lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs, whose answer was: transmission $4,329.9k, distribution $4,017.9k, vegetation $312.0k, NPV $4,611.5k.

Every LP Can Be Rewritten Into One Standard Form

First, the notation. Put the variables in a list, $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$; here $x = (s_{\text{transmission}}, s_{\text{distribution}}, s_{\text{vegetation}})$. A row $a_1 x_1 + \dots + a_n x_n \le b$ is written $a \cdot x \le b$, where $a \cdot x$ (the dot product) means "multiply entry by entry, then add". Stack the rows' coefficient lists $a$ as the rows of a table $A$ (a matrix, one row per constraint and one column per variable) and their right-hand sides as a list $b$, and the whole system of rows is $A x \le b$: row $i$ of $A x$ is $a_i \cdot x$, and it must be at most $b_i$. The objective's coefficients form one more list $c$, and the objective is $c \cdot x$, also written $c^\top x$.

Every LP can be rewritten into it, by five moves, each of which keeps exactly the same solutions:

The model hasRewrite it asWhy it is the same
$a \cdot x \ge b$$-a \cdot x \le -b$multiplying by $-1$ flips the inequality
$a \cdot x = b$$a \cdot x \le b$ and $-a \cdot x \le -b$"equal" is "at most and at least"

variables on both sidesmove them to the leftordinary algebra

Two more moves handle variables whose domain is not $x_j \ge 0$: a variable with lower bound $\ell_j \ne 0$ is replaced by $x_j' = x_j - \ell_j \ge 0$, and a free variable (any sign, such as a change in spend that may be negative) is written as the difference of two non-negative ones, $x_j = x_j^+ - x_j^-$.

Different books choose different standard forms: some minimize, some use $Ax = b$ (the equality form of the next heading). The book Hands-On Mathematical Optimization with Python (MO-book) writes $\min c^\top x$ s.t. $Ax \ge b$, $x \ge 0$; the HiGHS documentation writes $\min c^\top x$ s.t. $Ax = b$, $x \ge 0$. The moves are the same, so check which form a text means before you use its formulas.

Problem statement

A water utility's analysts keep their LPs as plain data: a list of variable names, an objective (coefficients by name, and "max" or "min") and a list of rows. Each row is a tuple (coefficients, op, rhs): the coefficients by variable name (a variable missing from the dict has coefficient 0), op one of "<=", ">=" or "==", and the right-hand side. Every variable is $\ge 0$. Their in-house LP code accepts only one shape, the standard form:

Write standard_form(variables, objective, sense, rows) in standard_form.py. It returns (c, A, b) as plain lists: c has one entry per variable, in the order of variables; A is a list of rows, each a list with one entry per variable in that order; b has one entry per row of A. Rewrite with these moves:

GivenBecomes
$a \cdot x \le r$the row $a$, right-hand side $r$
$a \cdot x \ge r$the row $-a$, right-hand side $-r$
$a \cdot x = r$two rows, in this order: $a$ with $r$, then $-a$ with $-r$

Rows keep the order they were given in. About twelve lines; no solver needed.

Slack Is the Room Left in a ≤ Row, and Surplus Is the Excess Over a ≥ Row


The budget officer's question has a precise answer for each row:


  • For a $\le$ row $a \cdot x \le b$, the slack is $b - a \cdot x$: how far the row's activity is below its limit. Unspent budget is the budget row's slack.
  • For a $\ge$ row $a \cdot x \ge b$, the surplus is $a \cdot x - b$: how far the activity is above its minimum. Vegetation spend above the floor is the floor row's surplus.


Both are $\ge 0$ for any allowed solution, and both are $0$ exactly when the row is full. A row whose slack or surplus is zero at the solution is called binding (also active or tight); lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs called these the full rows.


Slack is more than a number to report: it can be a variable. Add one new variable per row, $\text{slack}_i \ge 0$, and every inequality becomes an equation:



The whole LP becomes $\max c^\top x$ s.t. $A x + \text{slack} = b$, $x, \text{slack} \ge 0$: the equality form, the shape the simplex method of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) works on. The program builds the split in this form, one extra variable per row:




It then prints, for each row, the value GLOP chose for the extra variable, next to the room computed from lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs's solution ($b - a \cdot x$ for a $\le$ row, $a \cdot x - b$ for a $\ge$ row):



The same NPV, and each slack equals the room. Three rows are binding: crew_distribution, crew_vegetation and share_transmission. Now read each number in the budget officer's units:


  • budget: $230.3k unspent.
  • crew_transmission: 15.3 crew-months of transmission crews idle (26 available, 10.7 used).
  • floor_vegetation: vegetation got $12.0k more than the regulator's minimum.
  • share_distribution: 312.0, in the row's own units. The row is $0.5 s_D - 0.5 s_T - 0.5 s_V \le 0$, so its slack is $0.5 (s_T + s_V - s_D) = 0.5 (4{,}329.9 + 312.0 - 4{,}017.9) = 312.0$: half of how far distribution is below the other two together. A slack is measured in the units of the row as written; multiply a row by 2 and its slack doubles. When you report a slack, say which row it belongs to and in what units.


A slack is never negative in an allowed solution. If your own computation gives $-0.0000001$, that is floating-point noise, not a violated row; compare with a tolerance, as lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs did.


A Row With Room Does Not Shape the Answer; Loosening It Changes Nothing


Slack tells the planner where extra resources pay. The program loosens one row at a time, by more than nothing, and re-solves:






Read the five lines against the slack table.


  • Rows with room. The budget had $230.3k of slack; adding $200k leaves the answer exactly where it was. The floor had $12.0k of surplus; deleting the floor altogether changes nothing. A row with room was not pressing on the solution, so moving it, or removing it, leaves the best split allowed and still best.
  • Binding rows. One more crew-month for distribution or for vegetation, and the NPV rises, by $4{,}733.5 - 4{,}611.5 = 122.0$ and $4{,}746.6 - 4{,}611.5 = 135.1$ $k.


Why must the rows with room not matter? Suppose the solution $x^*$ satisfies a row with room, and you loosen that row. Every split allowed before is still allowed, so the best value can only rise. If some new split $y$ beat $x^*$, then every point on the straight line from $x^*$ toward $y$ would beat $x^*$ too, because the objective is linear. Points close enough to $x^*$ on that line still satisfy the loosened row's old limit (there was room) and every other row (they are the same rows, and $x^*$ and $y$ both satisfy them, so every point between does). Those points were allowed in the original model and beat $x^*$, which contradicts $x^*$ being optimal. So the answer stays. This argument uses linearity twice: in the objective, and in "every point between two allowed points is allowed" (the allowed region is convex, lesson Convex sets, polyhedra and extreme points). For a MILP, where the points between two plans are fractional and not allowed, it fails, and section 3 shows what happens instead.


The distribution line has one more lesson in it. One crew-month bought the same NPV that three did in lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs (4,733.5 both times). With 10 crew-months, distribution's crews allow $10 \times 1000 / 2.24 = 4{,}464.3$ $k, but distribution only grows to $4,133.0k, because a new row binds first: the budget ($4{,}445.0 + 4{,}133.0 + 312.0 = 8{,}890$). So the value of an extra unit of a binding row holds only until another row binds. How far it holds, and the price per unit, are the ranging and dual values of lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver.


MathOpt Stores Every Row as lb ≤ a·x ≤ ub, So You Write Rules as the Business States Them


If every LP must be in standard form, why did lesson Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs write $\ge$ rows, and variables on both sides of a share row? Because MathOpt does the conversion for you. Every row is stored in one shape, a lower bound, a linear expression and an upper bound, $\text{lb} \le a \cdot x \le \text{ub}$, with $\pm\infty$ for a missing side:




  • A $\le$ row has $\text{lb} = -\infty$; a $\ge$ row has $\text{ub} = +\infty$.
  • An equation has $\text{lb} = \text{ub}$. t == 2 * d was stored as $0 \le t - 2d \le 0$: the variables moved left, the constant right.
  • A ranged row, "between 1,000 and 5,000", is one row with two finite bounds, written with lb=, ub= and expr=. In standard form it would be two rows.
  • written_both_sides had variables and a constant on both sides; MathOpt collected them into $0.5t - 0.5d \le 100$.
  • Variables have bounds too. free was created with no lb and no ub, and it is $-\infty \le \text{free} \le +\infty$: the default is a free variable, which is why every money variable in this module says lb=0.


So in MathOpt, write each rule the way the business states it. The rewrite into a solver's own internal form is the solver's job, and it does not change the answer, as the first heading showed. You will still use standard form, and slack, in three places: when you read LP theory (lessons Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) and The dual LP and what its variables price assume one form), when you implement an algorithm yourself (the simplex tableau of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)), and when you explain to a budget officer what is left of each limit.

Summary


  • Every LP can be rewritten into one standard form, $\max c^\top x$, $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$: negate $\ge$ rows and minimizations, split equations into two rows, move variables left, turn bounds into rows, and shift or split variables that are not $\ge 0$. The answer never changes.
  • Slack is the room left in a $\le$ row; surplus is the excess over a $\ge$ row. Both are $\ge 0$, both are zero exactly on a binding row, and adding them as variables gives the equality form. A slack is in the units of the row as written.
  • A row with room does not shape the answer. Loosen or delete it and the LP's answer stays; an extra unit of a binding row pays, until another row binds.
  • MathOpt stores every row as lb ≤ a·x ≤ ub, so write rules as the business states them and let the solver convert. A variable with no bounds is free.

Design Note: Why the Course Uses max and ≤


Textbooks disagree on standard form: many write $\min c^\top x$ s.t. $Ax = b$, $x \ge 0$, which is the equality form with the objective flipped. This course uses $\max$ with $\le$ because GridCap's questions are mostly "most value within limits", so the model as written is already nearly in that shape, and the slack of each $\le$ row is the unused amount of a resource a planner can name.
