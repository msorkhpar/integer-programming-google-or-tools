  16. Simplex, interior point and PDLP

GridCap's planning team has a funding LP: what share of each candidate project to fund, year by year, with unspent money carried forward. It runs on the medium portfolio (400 projects) every week, and the team wants to run it on the large one (10,000 projects) for scenario work. A colleague asks which LP solver to use. OR-Tools offers three through MathOpt: GLOP, HiGHS and PDLP. They are three different algorithms, not three brands of one. This lesson solves the same LP with each one, reads what each returns, and states the rules for choosing between them and for checking that they agree. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data, not any real utility's.

The words used below: a solver is a program that solves models (GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP); an algorithm is the method it uses inside (simplex, interior point, first order). HiGHS has several algorithms; GLOP and PDLP have one family each. An iteration is one step of an algorithm, and every solver counts them.

The Model

The funding LP, stated in full before any code. Every project starts as early as it may; the LP chooses how much of each to fund and how much money to carry from one year into the next.

Sets. $P$ the candidate projects; $Y$ the plan's years, the first being $y_0$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects.

Parameters. $c_{i,y}$, what project $i$ spends in year $y$ if it starts at its earliest start ($k; 0 in a year it is not worked, and spend after the last plan year is outside the plan); $v_i$, the project's NPV at its earliest start ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $0 \le f_i \le 1$ for every $i \in P$: the share of project $i$ funded, a continuous amount (0.4 means 40% of its scope, cost and value). $r_y \ge 0$ for every $y \in Y$: the money carried out of year $y$ into the next, with $r_{y_0 - 1} = 0$.

Objective.

Constraints.

Row (cash$_y$) says a year's spending plus what it carries out is at most its budget plus what came in. Row (must$_i$) funds a must-do project in full. Every variable is continuous and every term is a number times one variable, so this is a linear program (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick). It is also the LP relaxation (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick) of a yes/no selection that starts each project as early as it may: with $f_i \in \{0, 1\}$ instead, it would choose whole projects.

Simplex Walks From Vertex to Vertex and Ends on One

The allowed points of an LP form a polyhedron, and its corners are its vertices (lesson Convex sets, polyhedra and extreme points calls them extreme points). If an LP has a best point, one of its vertices is best. The simplex method uses that fact: it starts at a vertex and moves along an edge of the polyhedron to a neighbouring vertex whose objective is at least as good, one move per iteration, until no neighbour is better (lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) builds a small simplex and walks it by hand). Each move is called a pivot.

It comes in two forms:

  • Primal simplex keeps every row satisfied at every step and improves the objective until no move helps. GLOP's primal simplex took 191 pivots here.
  • Dual simplex keeps the optimality conditions satisfied at every step (no single move could improve the objective) and repairs broken rows one at a time, until none is broken. It reached the same vertex in 14 pivots on this LP, in both GLOP and HiGHS. Dual simplex is HiGHS's default for LPs; its documentation calls it likely faster and more robust than primal simplex.

The two solvers' defaults differ. GLOP's default is primal simplex: its parameter use_dual_simplex is false unless you set it (lesson Re-solving after the data changes relies on this). The end of the program solves the LP once more on each solver with no lp_algorithm, and the pivot counts show which form ran: GLOP's 191 is its primal simplex count, HiGHS's 14 its dual simplex count.

Neither form is faster on every LP; the counts above are for this LP. What both forms share is where they stop: on a vertex. At a vertex every variable but a few sits on one of its bounds, and the few that do not are all basic variables (lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)), of which there is one per row; so at most one variable per row sits in between. Here the 39 must rows only pin $f_i = 1$, so the 10 cash rows decide the vertex, and at most 10 variables can sit in between. Every simplex run above has exactly 10; the program's last line splits GLOP's into 4 projects partly funded and 6 years carrying money. That is why simplex answers are easy to read: most projects are funded fully or not at all.

Interior Point Crosses the Inside in a Few Expensive Steps

An interior point method, also called barrier, does not walk the edges. It keeps every variable strictly inside its bounds, never exactly on one, and moves through the inside toward an optimum. The rows may be broken at first (the log below measures by how much); they are repaired on the way.

What it follows. Complementary slackness (lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness) says that at an optimum, for every variable, its distance from its bound times its reduced cost is 0, and for every row, its slack times its price is 0. An interior point method relaxes each of those products from 0 to the same small positive number $\mu$. For each $\mu > 0$ there is one point (a plan and its prices) where the rows hold, the prices cover the objective, and every product equals $\mu$; since no product is 0, no variable sits on a bound and no slack is 0, so the point is inside. These points form a curve through the interior, the central path, and as $\mu$ shrinks toward 0 the path runs into an optimum. The name "barrier" comes from an equivalent view: add $\mu$ times the logarithm of each variable's distance to its bound to the objective. The logarithm falls to $-\infty$ at a wall, so it is a barrier that keeps the point inside, and its weight $\mu$ is lowered step by step.

What each iteration solves. One iteration takes one Newton step toward the path's point for a smaller $\mu$: it writes the conditions "rows hold, prices cover, every product equals $\mu$" to first order around the current point, which gives one system of linear equations in all the variables and prices at once, and solves it. The system is built from the whole constraint matrix, with weights that change at every iteration, so it is solved anew each time. That is why one iteration costs far more than a pivot. The step then goes as far along that direction as it can while staying inside, and $\mu$ is lowered.

HiGHS's log of its barrier run on the medium LP (its interior point code is called IPX) shows one row per iteration:

The objectives are negative because HiGHS minimizes $-\sum_i v_i f_i$ (lesson Reading an LP solver log reads the same sign in the simplex log). pinf and dinf measure how far the rows and the price conditions are still broken, and gap is the relative difference between the primal and dual objectives. On the path that difference is the sum of the products, so it falls with $\mu$: from 1.08 to $3.58 \times 10^{-12}$ in ten iterations, each iteration from the third on cutting it by a factor of 3 or more. The starred row comes after IPX builds a starting basis for itself; it is still an interior point iteration, the tenth, the count barrier_iterations reported above.

Why it ends near a vertex, not on one. Every product equals $\mu > 0$ on the path, so the method stops with variables that belong on a bound sitting a tiny distance off it, and its objective a tiny bit short of the optimum. Solvers then run crossover, a clean-up that starts from the barrier's point, moves the nearly-at-bound variables onto their bounds and finds the basis of an optimal vertex. HiGHS runs crossover by default. The program solves the LP twice through HiGHS's own Python API, highspy (as lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver does), with HiGHS's option run_crossover off and then on. It also sets presolve off, so that every variable goes through the interior point method; with presolve, the fixed must-do shares would be removed first and put back at exactly 1. For each run it counts the variables that sit strictly inside their bounds by more than a threshold:

Without crossover, all 410 variables are strictly inside their bounds, none exactly on one, and 21 of them by more than $10^{-6}$, more than the 10 a vertex can have; the objective is 0.001 below the simplex value, because the point is allowed but not yet at the optimum. With crossover, exactly 10 variables are inside and every other one is exactly on a bound: a vertex, with the simplex objective. HiGHS's documentation warns that crossover can occasionally be very expensive, and it can be switched off when an optimal point is enough and a vertex is not needed.

Barrier has to solve a large system at every iteration, and when it does so by factoring a matrix, the factor can hold many more nonzeros than the constraint matrix, depending on how the rows share variables. On very large LPs that factorization is what runs out first.

PDLP Takes Many Cheap Steps and Stops Within a Tolerance

PDLP is a first-order method. It starts from duality (lesson The dual LP and what its variables price). For an LP $\max c^\top x$ subject to $Ax \le b$ and bounds on $x$, with prices $y \ge 0$ on the rows, take the Lagrangian

the objective plus each row's slack valued at its price. The best plan and the best prices form a saddle point of $L$: the plan makes $L$ as large as it can, the prices make it as small as they can, and neither can gain by moving alone. "First order" means that each step uses only the gradient of $L$, its first derivatives, the direction in which $L$ changes fastest; it never solves a system of equations. Both gradients are quantities you have met:

  • the gradient in $x$ is $c - A^\top y$: each variable's reduced cost at prices $y$ (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver);
  • the gradient in $y$ is $b - A x$: each row's slack at plan $x$ (lesson Standard form, slack and surplus).

PDLP is built on the primal-dual hybrid gradient method (PDHG). One iteration takes two steps:

  1. Primal step. Move $x$ a step of size $\tau$ up its gradient, then clip each variable back into its bounds: a variable whose value is more than the prices of what it uses grows, one worth less shrinks.
  2. Dual step. Move $y$ a step of size $\sigma$ down its gradient, at the point $2x_\text{new} - x_\text{old}$ (a look ahead; without it the pair can circle the saddle point instead of closing in on it), then clip each price at 0: a broken row ($Ax > b$) gets a higher price, a row with room a lower one.

A bare version of that step, in plain Python, on a 2-variable LP whose optimum is 11 at $(3, 1)$ with prices $(2, 0, 1)$:

Plan and prices find each other together. At iteration 10 the plan overshoots: $x_1 = 4.1$ breaks the row $x_1 \le 3$ by 1.1, and that row's price $y_3$ has risen from 0.25 at iteration 5 to 1.61. By iteration 20 the plan has pulled back to $x_1 = 2.53$, nothing is broken, and the prices have fallen below covering the objective again (uncovered 0.59). The swings shrink until all three measures are below $10^{-6}$, at iteration 957. Each iteration did one product with $A^\top$ and one with $A$, and nothing else expensive.

That is the whole cost of an iteration in PDLP too: a pass over the constraint matrix and its transpose, and no factorization, so it needs no more memory than the matrix itself and can take on LPs too big to factor. The bare step needed 957 iterations even on 2 variables; PDLP adds to it a rescaling of rows and columns, presolve, step sizes that adapt as it goes, and restarts from an average of recent points (the paper behind PDLP describes each); with all of them it took 256 iterations on the 410-variable funding LP. The price is still many iterations and a looser answer: PDLP stops as soon as three measures are below its tolerance ($10^{-6}$ relative by default): how much the rows are broken, how far the prices are from covering the objective, and the gap between the primal and dual objectives.

In one sentence, for an interview: PDLP treats the LP and its dual as one saddle-point problem and alternates cheap gradient steps on the plan and on the prices, each a matrix-vector product followed by a clip to the bounds, until both are feasible and their objectives agree within a tolerance.

That is what the table shows: PDLP's objective, 234898.1297, differs from the simplex value 234898.1206 by 0.009, about 4 parts in 100 million. Both are OPTIMAL; each is optimal within its solver's tolerance. PDLP's value is even above the true maximum, which no allowed point can be: its point breaks some rows by a sliver, within tolerance. On the large portfolio below its value lands below the maximum instead. Which side PDLP lands on is not fixed. PDLP also has no crossover, so its point need not be a vertex. On the large portfolio below it ends with 14 variables in between, more than a vertex can have.

The OR-Tools guide to LP solving puts the trade this way: first-order methods scale to larger problems with less memory, but are more sensitive to numbers and can struggle to reach high accuracy. The HiGHS documentation adds that on a CPU its own PDLP implementations are unlikely to compete with its simplex or interior point solvers; they are built for GPUs. So the order of choice for a model GridCap's size is: simplex first, barrier when simplex is slow, PDLP when the LP is too large for either.

Each Solver Accepts Only Its Own Algorithms

lp_algorithm is a request, and a solver that lacks the algorithm refuses the solve. The full grid, on the small portfolio:

GLOP is simplex only, PDLP is first order only, and HiGHS (as OR-Tools 9.15 drives it) offers both simplex forms and barrier. The refusal arrives as an exception, never as a status. Its message is not the solver's reason: in OR-Tools 9.15 the Python wrapper fails while building the real error and raises this AttributeError instead. So a program that tries an algorithm catches the exception around the solve call, and records the refusal rather than retrying with the default, which would label a simplex result "barrier".

Problem statement

A team benchmarking LP methods wants one helper that runs a model with a named solver and a named LP algorithm and reports how much work the algorithm did, so a table of methods can be built from its results without anyone reading logs.

Write run_method(model, solver, algorithm) in methods.py. It solves model with mathopt.solve(model, solver, params=mathopt.SolveParameters(lp_algorithm=algorithm)) and returns a tuple (status, iterations):

  • status is the termination reason's name ("OPTIMAL", ...);
  • iterations is the count that matches the algorithm's kind, read from result.solve_stats: simplex_iterations for PRIMAL_SIMPLEX and DUAL_SIMPLEX, barrier_iterations for BARRIER, first_order_iterations for FIRST_ORDER.

A solver that does not offer the algorithm raises an exception from mathopt.solve. Then the function returns ("REFUSED", 0). It never falls back to the solver's default algorithm, because a benchmark row labelled "barrier" that was solved by simplex is a wrong row. Catch the exception around the solve call only; in OR-Tools 9.15 the exception's type is an accident of the wrapper, so catch Exception.

The model the tests pass in is their own; the function never builds one.

Same Model, Three Solvers: Compare Objectives With a Relative Tolerance


Switching solver is one argument in MathOpt, so the cheapest test of a model, or of a solver upgrade, is to solve it on several and compare. On the large portfolio, each with its default algorithm:






Three things to read from it.


  • == is the wrong test. PDLP's objective is $1.8k below the simplex value, and both are correct answers within their tolerances. A test that asserts equality fails on a correct model. The two simplex solvers happen to agree to the last digit here; that is not something to rely on either, since two exact methods may stop on different optimal vertices and add their numbers in a different order.
  • The tolerance is relative. A difference of 1.8 on six million is $3 \times 10^{-7}$; the same 1.8 on an objective of 50 would be a real disagreement. So the gap is divided by the size of the values. Dividing by at least 1 keeps two objectives near zero (say 0 and $10^{-9}$) from producing a huge "relative" gap out of noise. $10^{-6}$ is a common choice because LP solvers' default tolerances are around $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-8}$.
  • Rounding is not a fix. 6,052,813.4 and 6,052,811.6 round to different whole numbers, and any two values on either side of a rounding boundary differ after rounding, however close they are.


A comparison also starts from the status: a solver that ended without OPTIMAL has no objective to compare, and that is a disagreement, not a value to skip.

Summary


  • Simplex walks from vertex to vertex and ends on one. Primal simplex keeps the rows satisfied, dual simplex keeps optimality; both stop on a vertex, with at most one in-between variable per row.
  • Interior point crosses the inside in a few expensive steps. It follows the central path, where every complementary slackness product equals $\mu$, with one Newton system per iteration while $\mu$ falls; it stops just off the bounds, and crossover then moves its answer to a vertex.
  • PDLP takes many cheap steps and stops within a tolerance. Gradient steps on the Lagrangian, up for the plan and down for the prices, each a matrix-vector product and a clip; so it fits LPs too large to factor, and its answer is less exact and need not be a vertex.
  • GLOP's default is primal simplex; HiGHS's is dual simplex.
  • Each solver accepts only its own algorithms. GLOP: simplex; HiGHS: simplex and barrier; PDLP: first order. A refusal is an exception.
  • Compare objectives with a relative tolerance. Never with ==, and never by rounding.
