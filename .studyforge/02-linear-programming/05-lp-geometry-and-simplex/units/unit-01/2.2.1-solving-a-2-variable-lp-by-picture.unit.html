GridCap's maintenance planner has $3.0M left for next year and two programs that want it: distribution work (transformers, poles, breakers) and transmission work (lines and towers). Each $100k of distribution work removes 5 points from GridCap's risk score; each $100k of transmission work removes 4. Distribution is the better buy per dollar, so the obvious answer is "all of it to distribution". That answer is wrong, because two more rules apply: crews are limited, and only so much transmission work is designed and ready to start. (GridCap is the course's fictional utility; these numbers describe no real company.)

With only two decisions, the whole problem fits on one sheet of graph paper. This lesson solves it by drawing, in four rules. The picture is worth the trouble: every LP solver in this course, including GLOP and HiGHS (the two that lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick introduced), is doing a higher-dimensional version of what you will do here by eye.

The Model

The planner's question, written as a linear program (LP: continuous variables, a linear objective, linear constraints; lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick introduced the term). Money is counted in units of $100k, so 30 means $3.0M.

Sets. The two programs, $K = \{\text{distribution}, \text{transmission}\}$. With only two, the model names each variable directly instead of indexing by $K$.

Parameters.

SymbolValueMeaning
$v_d, v_t$5, 4risk points removed per $100k of distribution, of transmission
$B$30money available, in $100k
$g_d, g_t$3, 2crew-weeks needed per $100k of distribution, of transmission
$C$72crew-weeks available
$R$20transmission work designed and ready, in $100k

Variables. $d \ge 0$, the money given to distribution, and $t \ge 0$, the money given to transmission, both in $100k and both continuous: $1,234,500 of distribution work is a fine answer.

Objective.

Constraints.

In code, every row is stored the same way, as two coefficients and a right-hand side that read $a_d\, d + a_t\, t \le b$. A bound such as $d \ge 0$ is turned around to fit that shape: $-d \le 0$.

The same rows become a MathOpt model (MathOpt is OR-Tools' modeling layer, met in lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick) with one loop. A solver will confirm the answer at the end of the lesson; until then, the picture does the work.

add_variable(lb=0) gives a continuous variable with lower bound 0 and no upper bound, which is $d \ge 0$. So the two bounds are not added as rows here; the solver keeps them as variable bounds.

Each Constraint Keeps One Side of a Line, and the Feasible Region Is Where All Sides Overlap

A plan is one choice of the two numbers $(d, t)$, so it is a point on a sheet of paper with $d$ across and $t$ up. A plan is feasible when it satisfies every constraint.

Take one constraint, crews: $3d + 2t \le 72$. The points where $3d + 2t = 72$ exactly form a straight line, through $(24, 0)$ and $(0, 36)$. That line splits the sheet in two. On one side, $3d + 2t$ is less than 72 (the origin is there: $3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0 = 0$); on the other side it is more. The constraint keeps the first side and the line itself. A set of that shape, a line and everything on one side of it, is called a half-plane. Every linear inequality in two variables is one.

To test a plan against a row, put its numbers into the left side and compare with the right:

The 1e-9 is a tolerance: a plan that meets a row exactly, but whose float arithmetic lands a hair above $b$, still counts as meeting it. Five plans, tested:

Spending $2.0M on distribution and $1.0M on transmission fits the budget exactly, but needs $3 \cdot 20 + 2 \cdot 10 = 80$ crew-weeks, eight more than GridCap has.

The feasible region is the set of all feasible plans: the points that lie in every half-plane at once. Testing every point of a grid and printing # for feasible and . for not draws it. Each character is one grid point: $d$ goes up by 1 ($100k) per column, $t$ by 2 per row. The letters mark the five corners, which the next section finds exactly:

Read the boundary from the top left. Along the top, from E to D, the region stops at $t = 20$: that is ready_t. From D to C the edge is the budget line $d + t = 30$, and from C down to B it is the steeper crews line $3d + 2t = 72$. The left edge is $d = 0$ and the bottom edge $t = 0$. The program draw_region.py draws the same region with matplotlib, with each boundary line labelled.

Two facts about this region hold for every LP, in any number of variables:

  • It has straight edges. Each edge is part of one constraint's line. In three variables the lines become flat planes and the region is a solid with flat faces; in $n$ variables it is called a polyhedron, and a polyhedron that does not run off to infinity is a polytope.
  • It is convex. A set is convex when the straight segment between any two of its points stays inside it. Each half-plane is convex, and points that lie in every one of several convex sets form a convex set too, so the region has no dents or holes. Lesson Convex sets, polyhedra and extreme points treats convexity in general; here it is all you need.

A Vertex Is Where Two Boundary Lines Cross Inside the Region

A vertex (a corner) of the region is a feasible point where two boundary lines cross. Lesson Convex sets, polyhedra and extreme points calls the same points extreme points: points of the region that are not in the middle of any segment between two other points of it. In two variables a corner needs two lines because one line fixes only one direction; the second pins the point down.

Two lines $a_1 d + b_1 t = r_1$ and $a_2 d + b_2 t = r_2$ meet at one point unless they are parallel. Solving the pair by hand (eliminate one variable, then the other) gives Cramer's rule:

When $\Delta = 0$ the lines have the same slope: they never meet, or they are the same line. In code:

For budget and crews: $\Delta = 1 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 1 = -1$, $d = (30 \cdot 2 - 72 \cdot 1)/(-1) = 12$ and $t = (1 \cdot 72 - 3 \cdot 30)/(-1) = 18$.

A crossing is a vertex only if it is feasible: two lines can cross somewhere every plan is forbidden. The model has five rows (three constraints and two bounds), so there are ten pairs. Every pair, checked:

Five of the ten crossings are corners of the region: A $(0, 0)$, B $(24, 0)$, C $(12, 18)$, D $(10, 20)$ and E $(0, 20)$, the letters on the map. Four crossings fall outside: the budget line meets $d = 0$ at $t = 30$, but only 20 of transmission is ready. One pair, $t = 20$ and $t = 0$, is parallel. The function that keeps only the feasible crossings, and records which rows are tight at each, is:

A row is tight (or active) at a plan when the plan meets it with equality: at C, the budget is spent to the last dollar and every crew-week is used. A row that is not tight has slack, an unused amount: at C, ready transmission work has $20 - 18 = 2$ ($200k) left over. Rounding to 9 decimals merges the same corner reached through different pairs of lines (lesson Infeasible, unbounded and degenerate LPs meets a corner where three lines cross), and + 0.0 turns a -0.0 into 0.0.

Problem statement

A district heating crew plans two kinds of work for a month, $x$ and $y$ (in hundreds of hours), under a handful of limits. Before anyone trusts a solver with it, the team lead wants the corners of the allowed region printed, so the drawing on the whiteboard can be checked against numbers.

Write vertices(rows) in vertices.py. Each row is a tuple (a, b, r) that reads $a\,x + b\,y \le r$; bounds such as $x \ge 0$ arrive as rows too, (-1, 0, 0). The region is

A corner is a point where the boundary lines $a_i x + b_i y = r_i$ of two rows cross and that every row allows. For two rows, with $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$:

and when $|\Delta| < 10^{-12}$ the lines are parallel and have no crossing.

Return the corners as a sorted list of (x, y) tuples, each rounded to 6 decimals (add 0.0 after rounding so that -0.0 becomes 0.0), each corner listed once. Count a row as allowing a point when $a\,x + b\,y \le r + 10^{-9}$ (the constant TOL), so a corner is not lost to float arithmetic. A region with no corners gives [].

The Objective's Level Lines Are Parallel, and the Best Plan Is the Last Point the Line Touches


The objective $5d + 4t$ gives every plan a value. The plans with one particular value, say 100, satisfy $5d + 4t = 100$: that is again a straight line, called a level line (or iso-value line) of the objective. Different values give parallel lines, because only the right-hand side changes, never the slope. Raising the value slides the line away from the origin, in the direction $(5, 4)$: the direction in which the objective grows fastest, made of its coefficients.


So the picture method is: draw one level line through the region, then slide it in the direction $(5, 4)$, keeping it parallel, until one more nudge would leave the region. The last feasible points it touches are the best plans.


The value at each vertex tells you where that happens:




Why the vertices settle whether a level line meets the region: the region is convex and has these five corners, so a line misses it exactly when all five corners lie strictly on one side of the line. The values, and five level lines:



At level 130 the line still cuts through the region: it passes through D, but plans beyond it, near C, are worth more. At 132 the only feasible point left on the line is C. At 140 no plan reaches the line at all. So the best plan is C: $1.2M to distribution and $1.8M to transmission, removing 132 risk points.


That settles the opening puzzle. "All to distribution" is vertex B, $(24, 0)$, and it is stopped by crews before it spends the money: distribution needs 3 crew-weeks per $100k against transmission's 2, so B spends only $2.4M and is worth 120. Moving from B toward C trades distribution for transmission along the crews line. Each step gives up $100k of distribution and buys $150k of transmission with the same crew-weeks, for a net gain of $-5 + 1.5 \cdot 4 = 1$ point, until the budget runs out at C.


If an LP Has a Best Plan, Some Vertex Is a Best Plan


The sliding line always comes to rest on a corner or along an edge between two corners; it cannot come to rest on a point in the middle of the region, because from there it could slide further. Stated as a rule: if an LP has an optimal plan, then at least one vertex of its region is an optimal plan. (Its region needs at least one vertex for this; every region in this module has one, and so does every nonempty region whose variables are all bounded below by 0.)


Why it is true in general, in any number of variables: every point of a bounded region is a weighted average of its vertices, with nonnegative weights that add up to 1. A linear objective's value at a weighted average is the same weighted average of the vertices' values, and an average can never exceed the largest thing being averaged. So no point beats the best vertex.


Sometimes the best plan is not unique. Change the objective to $4d + 4t$, as if both programs removed 4 points per $100k:



Now the level lines are parallel to the budget line $d + t = 30$, and the line comes to rest along the whole edge from D to C. Every plan on that edge, such as $(11, 19)$, is optimal with value 120. The rule still holds: C and D are vertices, and both are optimal. An LP solver returns one optimal plan; when there are several, which one it returns is up to the solver.


A solver agrees with the picture. GLOP, OR-Tools' LP solver, on the model built above:



The rule suggests an algorithm: list every vertex, compute its value, keep the best. That is what this lesson did, and it works for two variables. It does not scale. With $n$ variables, a vertex is a point where $n$ boundary rows are tight, so a model with $n$ variables and $m$ constraints (plus its $n$ bounds) has up to $\binom{n+m}{n}$ candidate crossings to solve and test:



The first line is this lesson's ten pairs. GridCap's real models have thousands of variables. The simplex method, in lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation), keeps the idea that the answer is a vertex but visits only a short path of them, each one better than the last.

Summary


  • Each constraint keeps one side of a line, and the feasible region is where all sides overlap. The region of an LP is convex and has straight edges: a polygon in two variables, a polyhedron in general.
  • A vertex is where two boundary lines cross inside the region. Solve the two rows as equations (Cramer's rule), then keep the crossing only if it breaks no row.
  • The objective's level lines are parallel, and the best plan is the last point the line touches as it slides in the direction of the objective's coefficients.
  • If an LP has a best plan, some vertex is a best plan. Several vertices can tie, and then the whole edge or face between them is optimal. Listing all vertices works for two variables and explodes beyond that.

Design Note: The Picture Is for Understanding, Not for Solving


No one solves a production LP by listing vertices: the table of crossings shows why. The picture earns its place because it makes the vocabulary concrete. Feasible region, vertex, tight row and slack are the words a solver's log, its dual values (lesson The dual LP and what its variables price) and its infeasibility reports (section 9) use, and each one is something you can now point at on a drawing. When a large model misbehaves, shrinking it to two variables and drawing it is still one of the fastest ways to see why.
