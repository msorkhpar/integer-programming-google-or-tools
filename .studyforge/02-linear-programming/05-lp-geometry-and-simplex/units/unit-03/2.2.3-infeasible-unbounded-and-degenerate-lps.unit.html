The budget split of lessons Solving a 2-variable LP by picture and Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) had a best plan, and simplex walked to it in two steps. In one week, three changes reach GridCap's planner (GridCap is the course's fictional utility). Finance adds a commitment the budget cannot pay for. A data load fails and two rows go missing. Operations adds a mix rule that passes through a corner of the region. The first change leaves no plan at all, the second lets the value grow without end, and the third makes simplex take a step that goes nowhere. Each one has a picture, a tableau signature and a solver status, and this lesson shows all three, in four rules.

The Model and Its Three Changes

The budget split, with $d$ and $t$ the money for distribution and transmission in $100k. Each change is applied on its own to the base model, never together:

  • Change 1, committed. Finance has promised a regulator $3.4M of work next year: $d + t \ge 34$.
  • Change 2, lost rows. The budget and crews rows fail to load; only ready_t and the bounds remain.
  • Change 3, mix. Operations wants distribution spending to be at most twice transmission spending: $d \le 2t$.

In code, every row still reads $a_d\, d + a_t\, t \le b$ (lesson Solving a 2-variable LP by picture), so a $\ge$ row is multiplied by $-1$: $d + t \ge 34$ becomes $-d - t \le -34$, and $d \le 2t$ becomes $d - 2t \le 0$.

An Infeasible LP Has an Empty Region: No Plan Satisfies Every Row

An LP is infeasible when no plan satisfies all its constraints: its feasible region is empty. Change 1 does that:

Every vertex of the old region falls short of the commitment, a plan that meets the commitment breaks the budget, no vertex is left, and the course's two LP solvers, GLOP and HiGHS (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick), both report the model infeasible:

In the picture, budget keeps the side of the line $d + t = 30$ toward the origin and committed keeps the side of the parallel line $d + t = 34$ away from it. The two half-planes do not overlap, so no crossing of boundary lines is feasible and vertices finds none. The two rows contradict each other directly: $d + t$ cannot be at most 30 and at least 34.

INFEASIBLE is not a solver failure. It is the solver's proof that the rules, as written, cannot all hold, and it says nothing about which rule is wrong. Here the culprit is plain; in a model with thousands of rows it is not, and section 9 shows how to find a small set of rows that conflict.

Phase 1 Measures How Far the Rows Are From Agreeing, and Finds a First Vertex When They Do

The tableau of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) starts at the origin with the slacks as the basis, and that works only when every $b_i \ge 0$. The committed row has $b = -34$: its slack at the origin would be $-34$, so the origin is not a vertex, and the tableau has nowhere to start. Real models are full of $\ge$ rows like this one. Simplex solvers therefore run in two phases. Phase 1 finds a first vertex, or proves there is none; phase 2 is the walk of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation), started from that vertex.

Phase 1 is itself an LP, and one whose start is easy. Give every row $r$ an elastic variable $v_r \ge 0$, an amount by which the row may be stretched, and minimize the total stretch:

This LP always has a feasible plan: take $d = t = 0$ and stretch each row by what it lacks, $v_r = \max(0, -b_r)$. And its value can never go below 0. So it always has a best plan, and that plan answers the question:

  • Minimum 0. No row needs stretching, so the $d, t$ of that plan satisfy every original row. The model is feasible.
  • Minimum above 0. Every plan breaks some row by at least that total. The model is infeasible, and the number says by how much.

The two-phase method. A simplex solver uses this LP to start. Phase 1 solves the stretch LP with simplex itself, and the stretch LP has a vertex to start from for free: at the origin, each row that the origin breaks takes its stretch $v_r = -b_r$ as its basic variable, and every other row its slack, so every basic variable is $\ge 0$. Simplex then walks, never raising the total stretch at a pivot. If it ends at 0, every $v_r$ is 0, and the vertex it stopped on lies in the original region: it is a vertex of the original LP, found without guessing. Phase 2 drops the stretch variables, puts back the real objective, and walks from that vertex as in lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation). If phase 1 ends above 0, there is no phase 2: the model is infeasible, and phase 1's optimum is the proof.

A worked case. Replace the commitment with a smaller floor, $d + t \ge 20$ ($2.0M), which the budget can pay for. The origin breaks it by 20, so phase 1 starts with $v_\text{floor} = 20$ and total stretch 20:

Phase 1 reaches 0 at $(0, 20)$, and that point is a vertex of the region with the floor: three of its rows are tight there. Phase 2 starts from it and walks to the optimum, $(12, 18)$, with a value of 132. The floor did not change the answer, which was already above it.

Textbooks write phase 1 in more than one way: with one stretch variable per row, as here, only on the rows the origin breaks, or with a single one shared by every row. Every version minimizes how far the rows are from holding, and reaches 0 exactly when the model is feasible.

Without the commitment, the rows agree. With it, the least total stretch is 4, which is $34 - 30$: the commitment exceeds the budget by $400k. Both rows have the same left side, $d + t$, so whatever value it takes is above 30 or below 34 by a total of at least 4. Either finance trims the commitment by $400k, or someone finds $400k more budget. Section 9 builds on this same elastic model to report which rows a solver had to stretch.

Problem statement

A transit agency's maintenance LP came back INFEASIBLE. Before anyone hunts for the culprit row, the planners want one number: how far apart are the rows? Zero would mean the report was wrong; anything above zero is the least total amount by which the rows must be relaxed.

Write total_violation(rows) in violation.py. Each row is (a, sense, b): a list of coefficients, "<=" or ">=", and a right-hand side. The decision variables are $x_j \ge 0$, one per coefficient. Build and solve, with GLOP, the phase-1 LP that gives every row $r$ an elastic variable $v_r$:

and return its optimal value, a float. This LP always has an optimum (any $x$ fits once the $v_r$ are large enough, and the sum cannot go below 0), so there is no other status to handle.

An Unbounded LP Has a Direction Along Which the Objective Improves Forever


An LP is unbounded when plans exist and their values have no upper limit (for a maximization): for any number you name, some feasible plan is worth more. Change 2 does that. Without the budget and crews rows, nothing limits $d$, and each unit of $d$ adds 5 points.




The tableau of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) detects it in the ratio test. $d$ has the most negative objective-row entry, $-5$, so it enters. Its column has a 0 in the only constraint row (ready_t does not involve $d$), so no row has a positive entry, and no basic variable ever reaches zero as $d$ rises. leaving returns None, and solve stops with UNBOUNDED:






The direction is the proof. Start at the current vertex $(0, 0)$ and move along $(1, 0)$, that is, raise $d$ and leave $t$ alone: every point $(\theta, 0)$ with $\theta \ge 0$ is feasible, and its value $5\theta$ grows without limit. solve builds it from the entering column: the entering variable moves by 1, and each basic variable moves by minus its entry in that column. A direction like this is called a ray, or a primal ray; a MathOpt result has a primal_rays field for a solver to put one in.


Two solvers report the same model differently:



INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED means what it says: GLOP stopped knowing that one of the two holds, but not which. To tell them apart, solve the same rows with a zero objective. That LP asks only "is there any plan?", and it cannot be unbounded, since every plan is worth 0:






There is a plan, so the original LP is not infeasible, and it must be unbounded. A program that reads a solver's status should handle all three of INFEASIBLE, UNBOUNDED and INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED.


An unbounded region does not make an unbounded LP. The region of change 2 runs off to the right forever, but whether the value does depends on the objective. With $4t - d$, where distribution work counts against the score, the same region has a best plan:



No real budget is infinite, so in a capital plan UNBOUNDED almost always means a missing row or a row with the wrong sign. The direction tells you where to look: here it says "$d$ can grow forever", so the rows that should limit $d$ are the ones to check.


A Degenerate Vertex Has More Tight Rows Than It Needs, and Simplex Can Pivot Without Moving


In two variables a vertex needs two tight rows. A vertex where more rows are tight than that is degenerate. Change 3 adds the mix row $d - 2t \le 0$, whose line $d = 2t$ passes through the origin, where the two bounds already meet:






The mix rule cuts off the bottom edge of the old region: B $(24, 0)$ is gone, a new vertex $(18, 9)$ appears where the mix line meets crews, and the origin now has three tight rows. C, D and E are unchanged. C still satisfies the mix rule, $12 \le 36$, so the best plan has not moved.


In the tableau, degeneracy shows up as a basic variable with value 0. At the origin the basis is the four slacks, and $s_\text{mix} = 0 - (d - 2t) = 0$. Now run the walk. $d$ enters, as before. The ratio test now includes the mix row, whose entry for $d$ is $+1$ and whose right-hand side is 0, so its ratio is $0 / 1 = 0$, the smallest. $d$ rises by 0:



The first pivot is a degenerate pivot: the basis changes ($d$ is now basic, at value 0, and $s_\text{mix}$ is not) but the point and the value do not. It was not wasted: in the new basis, $t$ has a negative reduced cost and a positive step, and the walk moves up the mix line to $(18, 9)$, then along the crews line to C. Three pivots instead of two, and the same optimum GLOP finds.


Why degeneracy matters. When every step has a positive length, the value rises at every pivot, so simplex never returns to a basis it has left, and there are finitely many bases, so it must stop. A zero step breaks that argument: the value stays put, and in rare cases a sequence of degenerate pivots leads back to a basis already visited, and the walk repeats forever. That is cycling. Bland's rule prevents it: enter the improving variable with the lowest index, and break ties in the ratio test by the basic variable with the lowest index. With it, simplex provably never cycles. The 60-line solve uses Dantzig's rule and has a cruder guard, max_steps, after which it gives up with STEP_LIMIT.


Degenerate vertices are easy to create. A row of the form "at most this share" or "at most the parent's amount" has a right-hand side of 0, so its line passes through the origin, just as the mix row does here.

Problem statement


The pump-scheduling simplex can pivot. Now it must run on its own and say how it ended, including on the two kinds of model that trip up a first implementation: one whose objective can grow without limit, and one with a vertex where several rows are tight.


In simplex.py, pivot and entering (Dantzig's rule, None at an optimum) are given. Write:


  • ratio_row(T, k): the leaving row for entering column k,over the constraint rows (every row but the last), the lowest row index on a tie, and None when no constraint row has an entry above $10^{-9}$ in column k.
  • run_simplex(T, max_steps=100): repeat until done, at most max_steps pivots. Take the entering column; if there is none, return ("OPTIMAL", value) with the value in the objective row's last entry. Take the ratio row; if there is none, the LP is unbounded: return ("UNBOUNDED", None). Otherwise pivot and continue. If the steps run out, return ("STEP_LIMIT", None). The optimum is recognized only at the start of a step, by finding no entering column, and that check costs no pivot. So if the max_steps-th pivot lands on the optimum, there is no step left to see it, and the status is STEP_LIMIT: with max_steps=2, an LP that needs exactly two pivots ends STEP_LIMIT, and with max_steps=3 it ends OPTIMAL.


T is a tableau for $\max c \cdot x$, $Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$ with $b \ge 0$: constraint rows $[A \mid I \mid b]$, then the objective row $[-c \mid 0 \mid 0]$.

Summary


  • An infeasible LP has an empty region. No plan satisfies every row; solvers report INFEASIBLE, which is a proof, not a failure.
  • Phase 1 measures how far the rows are from agreeing. Give each row an elastic $v_r \ge 0$ and minimize $\sum_r v_r$: 0 means feasible (and gives a first vertex), more than 0 means infeasible by that total.
  • An unbounded LP has a direction along which the objective improves forever. In the tableau, the entering column has no positive entry. GLOP may report INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED; solving with a zero objective tells the two apart. An unbounded region alone does not make an LP unbounded.
  • A degenerate vertex has more tight rows than it needs, and simplex can pivot without moving. A basic variable at 0 gives a ratio of 0. Degenerate pivots can, rarely, cycle; Bland's rule prevents it.

Design Note: Read the Status Before the Values, Every Time


Each of the three changes produced a model that solved without an exception: the solver returned a result, and the program had to look at its status to know what the result meant. Asking an infeasible or unbounded result for its objective value gives a number that describes no plan. Lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick made "status first" a rule; this lesson is why. In a service that solves plans every night (section 12), a status other than OPTIMAL is an event to log with the model's name and inputs, and the phase-1 stretch or the unbounded direction is the first thing to put in that log.
