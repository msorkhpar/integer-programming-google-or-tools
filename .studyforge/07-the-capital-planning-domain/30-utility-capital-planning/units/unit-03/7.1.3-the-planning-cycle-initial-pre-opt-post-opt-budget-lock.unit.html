  15. The planning cycle

A capital plan is not solved once. GridCap's planners load the candidate list, check it, optimize it, look at the result, argue about it, change a budget or force a project in, optimize again, and finally freeze one plan that goes to finance and into the rate case. Along the way finance asks "what if 2029 has $2,760k less?", and the answer has to sit beside the base plan, not replace it. A CIPT (lesson How a utility plans capital: programs, portfolios, rate cases) names the stages of this cycle Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt and Budget Lock, and it needs a workflow that can create, copy and optimize scenarios across them, with iterative and ad hoc reruns and manual overrides. This lesson runs one full cycle on GridCap small and states the rules that keep it trustworthy.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course. The four stage names are the ones such a tool uses; what each stage allows below is this course's reading of them, which a real CIPT would write down in its own requirements. The cycle is the domain side of section 12, where lessons Scenarios as data: create, copy, optimize and Planning-cycle states and locking build it as a service.

Two words first. A scenario is one named set of planning inputs: the portfolio, the budgets, the floors, and any overrides. A run is one optimization of one scenario, with its result.

A Plan Moves Through Four Stages, and Each Stage Decides Who May Change What

The stages answer one question each: what may be changed now, and by whom.

StageWhat happensWhat may change
Initialthe candidate list, budgets and commitments arrive from the fleets and finance, and the data is checkedthe inputs, freely
Pre-Optplanners adjust the scenario before optimizing: budgets to compare, projects forced in or outthe inputs, by planners
Post-Optthe run's plan is read, compared with other scenarios and explainednothing in place: a change sends the scenario back to Pre-Opt and needs a rerun
Budget Lockone plan becomes the plan of record, the one finance and the regulator seenothing; a change is a new scenario

planning_cycle.py takes GridCap's base scenario through the first three stages. In Initial it runs two data checks before any solve (each must-do fits the budgets in some start year; no region floor exceeds everything the region asks for), the cheap prechecks of lessons Must-do vs. discretionary planning groups and Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend:

The checks pass, the planners add no override, and the run funds 27 projects for an NPV of $24,260k, the plan of section 6. The word snapshot in the first line is the subject of the next section.

Why make the stages explicit at all, rather than letting anyone edit and rerun at will? Because each group in lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer needs to know which plan it is looking at. A finance analyst who reads the spend of a plan that a planner has since edited is reading a plan that no longer exists.

A Scenario Is a Copy With Its Own Snapshot, Never an Edit of Another Scenario

Finance asks what a smaller 2029 envelope, $6,000k instead of $8,760k, would do. The wrong answer is to edit the base scenario's budget and rerun: the base plan, which planners are still reviewing, would be gone. The right answer is a copy: a new scenario that starts from the base's inputs, records where it came from, and is changed on its own.

To tell scenarios and runs apart with certainty, each scenario has a snapshot id: the sha256 hash of its inputs written out in one canonical form (lesson Versioned snapshots and content hashes covers content hashes). Equal inputs give the same id; any change to a budget, a floor, an override or a solver setting gives a different one. cycle_model.py computes it from everything the model is built from:

Two details make the id trustworthy. The portfolio enters through its own content hash (GridCap's Portfolio.content_hash()), so a change to any project's cost or window changes the snapshot too. And sort_keys=True with fixed separators means the same inputs always produce the same text, whatever order a dictionary happened to be built in. The solver settings are part of the inputs, because the same data solved with a different gap tolerance can give a different plan.

The copy, its edit and its run:

The copy starts with the base's snapshot, because its inputs are identical; the budget edit gives it its own. A copy starts in Initial, like any new scenario, so its changed inputs are checked before it is optimized. The cut costs $1,174k of NPV ($24,260k less $23,086k), and the scenario diff on the last line is what a planner reads first: which projects were dropped, which were added, and which moved year. One change is worth spelling out. P001 and P002 are the replace and the repair option for the same asset, A001 (alternative group G001). With less money in 2029, the plan swaps the $2,640k replacement for the $460k repair. A cut in one year also moves work in other years: six projects change start year, some earlier and some later, because the budget rows of all five years are solved together. Lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted builds this diff into a report.

Problem statement

A water utility (a fictional one) keeps its planning scenarios as JSON-ready dictionaries: budgets, floors, the projects a planner forced in or out, and the solver settings. It wants a snapshot id for each scenario, so that two runs can be compared by their ids, and a short answer to "what is different between these two scenarios?".

Write two functions in snapshot.py:

  • snapshot_id(inputs): the sha256 hex digest of one canonical text of the inputs, encoded as UTF-8. The canonical text is the inputs written as JSON with every dictionary's keys sorted and no spaces (separators "," and ":"), and with the lists under force_in and force_out sorted, because they are sets of project ids. Two scenarios that differ only in the order their dictionaries were built, or the order of those id lists, must get the same id; any other change must give a different one.
  • changed_fields(a, b): the sorted top-level field names whose values differ between two scenarios, by the same canonical rules. A field that only one of them has is a change.

Two details of the canonical text. Only the top-level force_in and force_out lists are sorted; a list of that name nested inside another field (say settings) is written in the order it has. And the text is json.dumps's output with its default ensure_ascii=True, so a character outside ASCII is written as an escape: a region named "Côte" appears as "C\u00f4te". The UTF-8 encoding is then applied to that text.

Before you code, write the canonical text of {"force_in": ["W2", "W1"], "budget": {"2028": 950, "2027": 900}} by hand. The tests compare your id with the digest of texts written out by hand. About ten lines; Python's json and hashlib do the work.

Post-Opt Reads the Plan, and Every Override Sends the Scenario Back for a Rerun


Post-Opt is where planners meet the plan, and they bring knowledge the model does not have. In GridCap small, the Ridge undergrounding job P024 was not funded; a planner knows that Ridge customers were promised it, and forces it in. An override is a change of inputs like any other. It goes into the scenario, gives a new snapshot, sends the scenario back to Pre-Opt, and needs a rerun before anyone may read a plan again:



The override costs $273k of NPV: the same "price it by solving with and without" rule as lesson Manual overrides as fixed bounds. It also changes more than P024. Three projects leave the plan, P037 (the other Ridge undergrounding job) comes in, and three move year. A planner who forced one project in should see all of that before agreeing to it, which is why the diff is printed with the cost.


What the rule protects against is a plan that no run produced. If a planner could add P024 to the Post-Opt plan by hand, without a rerun, the plan would overspend: P024 spends $850k in each of two years, and the base plan leaves at most $150k of any year's budget unspent (lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer prints the headroom). Its NPV would also be a number no solver computed. "Every change goes back through the model" is what keeps a plan feasible and its numbers true.


Budget Lock Freezes the Plan of Record, and a Change Means a New Scenario


When the planners, finance and management agree, one scenario's latest run becomes the plan of record: the plan that sets next year's spending and supports the rate case. Budget Lock freezes it. A locked scenario refuses edits; a change after lock needs a copy, which goes through the cycle again and, if adopted, replaces the plan of record by a decision, not by an edit:






The last line is the property a regulator will test in lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer: run the locked snapshot again and get the same plan. Here it held, on one machine with the same OR-Tools and HiGHS and the settings recorded in the snapshot. That is evidence for this run, not a guarantee. Problem statement


A water utility (a fictional one) keeps its planning scenarios in a book: a dictionary from a scenario's name to a record {"stage", "inputs", "plan", "parent"}. Its planning cycle has the four stages Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt and Budget Lock, and the book must refuse any step that breaks the cycle. Each function returns True when it did the step and False when it refused, and a refused step changes nothing.


Write the six functions in book.py:


  • create(book, name, inputs): a new scenario in Initial, with its own copy of inputs, no plan and no parent. A name already in the book is refused.
  • checked(book, name): the data checks passed, so an Initial scenario moves to Pre-Opt.
  • optimized(book, name, plan): a run finished, so a Pre-Opt scenario moves to Post-Opt and keeps the plan.
  • edit(book, name, changes): update the inputs with changes. A scenario in Budget Lock refuses. An edit to a scenario in Post-Opt makes its plan out of date: it goes back to Pre-Opt and loses its plan. In Initial or Pre-Opt the stage stays as it is.
  • lock(book, name): a Post-Opt scenario becomes the plan of record. There is only ever one: while any scenario of the book is in Budget Lock, every other lock is refused.
  • copy(book, source, name): a new scenario in Initial whose inputs start as the source's, with no plan and parent set to the source. Editing either one must never change the other.


Any scenario may be copied, whatever its stage; copying a locked plan of record is how a change after lock starts. create and copy refuse a new name that is already in the book. Every other name the functions receive (the scenario to check, run, edit, lock, or copy from) is one the book has: the tests never pass an unknown one, and the reference simply looks it up, so an unknown name raises KeyError.


Before you code, draw the four stages and mark which function moves a scenario along each arrow, and which arrows go back. About thirty lines.

The Model


The planning model every scenario of this lesson builds (cycle_model.build), in one place. It is the multi-period model of lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows to Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms and Must-do vs. discretionary planning groups, with the region floors of lesson Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend and the planner overrides of this lesson.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $F^{+} \subseteq P$ the projects a planner forces in and $F^{-} \subseteq P$ those forced out (disjoint, and $F^{-} \cap M = \emptyset$); $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years; $R$ the regions, with $P_r \subseteq P$ the projects in region $r$.


Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work, $k = 0, 1, \dots$ ($k), and $\text{capex}_i = \sum_k c_{i,k}$; $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $F_r$, the least region $r$ must receive over the plan ($k).


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. We write $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ and $\text{by}(i, t) = \sum_{s \in T_i,\, s \le t} x_{i,s}$.

Summary


  • A plan moves through four stages, and each stage decides who may change what. Initial checks the data, Pre-Opt sets up the scenario, Post-Opt reads the run, Budget Lock freezes the plan of record.
  • A scenario is a copy with its own snapshot, never an edit of another scenario. The snapshot is a hash of every input, settings included; equal inputs give equal ids.
  • Post-Opt reads the plan, and every override sends the scenario back for a rerun. Price the override and show the diff it causes.
  • Budget Lock freezes the plan of record, and a change means a new scenario. Keep the locked plan with its snapshot; a rerun that repeats is evidence, not a guarantee.

Design Note: The Stage Belongs to the Scenario, Not to the Program


planning_cycle.py keeps the stages in a dictionary beside the scenarios, which is enough to show the rules. A real tool stores the stage with the scenario, next to its snapshot, its runs and who changed what (lesson Manual overrides as bounds, with an audit trail adds the audit trail, and Planning-cycle states and locking the states and locking). Then "who may change what" is a check the service makes on every request, not a habit people are trusted to keep.
