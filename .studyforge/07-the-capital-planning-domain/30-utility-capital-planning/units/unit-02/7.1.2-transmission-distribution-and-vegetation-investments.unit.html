GridCap's 40 candidate projects come from three teams, and a planner reading the list can tell them apart at a glance. The transmission team asks for $43,990k with 14 projects, among them a $5,840k line rebuild. The vegetation team asks for $3,900k with 12, the smallest a $130k circuit clearing. The distribution team sits between them. These are the three fleets a CIPT (lesson How a utility plans capital: programs, portfolios, rate cases) is built to serve: Transmission, Distribution and Vegetation planning. This lesson explains what each fleet is, why its projects look the way they do, and which rules of the section 6 model each one brings with it.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course: its projects, crews and the circuits of the vegetation example are teaching data. The facts about real power systems come from the sources in the Further reading line: the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), the Regulatory Assistance Project's guide (RAP), and NERC's transmission vegetation standard.

A few words first. A fleet is all the assets of one kind a utility owns and maintains, and the team that plans work on them; in GridCap each fleet is one program (lesson How a utility plans capital: programs, portfolios, rate cases). Voltage is measured in volts; a kilovolt (kV) is 1,000 volts. A substation is a fenced site where transformers change voltage from one level to another. A circuit or feeder is one line, with its branches, that carries power out of a substation to customers. A right-of-way is the strip of land a line runs along, which the utility has the right to use and must keep clear.

Transmission Moves Bulk Power, So Its Projects Are Few, Large and Long

Power travels from power plants to customers in two stages. EIA describes them as two kinds of lines joined by transformers at substations: transmission lines carry power at high voltage over long distances, because high voltage makes long-distance transmission more efficient and cheaper; distribution lines carry it at lower voltage, safe for homes and businesses, the last miles to customers. The RAP guide puts the transmission threshold at about 115 kV and above, mostly on steel towers, with the highest lines at 345, 500 or 765 kV.

Three things follow for planning.

  • Failures are wide. A transmission line serves a whole region, not a street. The RAP guide describes reliability standards, written by NERC (the North American reliability organization) and enforced by FERC (the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission), that hold transmission owners responsible for withstanding the loss of a critical element, planning often summed up as "N-1". A failure that is not contained can cascade across the system.
  • Projects are large and slow. Towers, line rebuilds and breaker replacements cost millions and take more than one year to build. FERC sets the rates for most bulk power transmission (RAP section 3.8), so transmission projects may be reviewed by a different regulator from the rest of the portfolio.
  • Work is chained. A line cannot be rebuilt before the towers that carry it, and an outage on a line must be scheduled; transmission is where most parent-child links and repair-or-replace choices come from.

The median transmission project costs $2,595k, and the largest $5,840k: more than half of a year's whole budget (the 2031 budget is $7,850k). One such project funded in a year crowds out many smaller ones, so the yearly budget rows are where transmission work and everything else compete.

Distribution Reaches Every Customer, So Its Projects Are Many and Mid-Sized

Distribution is the system that reaches every customer: substation transformers that step the voltage down, feeders running out along streets, poles, smaller transformers, and service lines. EIA notes that the US has thousands of miles of high-voltage lines and millions of miles of low-voltage ones, so distribution is where the assets are numerous. Local utilities operate it, and the state commission regulates it (RAP section 13.1 puts lines at 34.5 kV or less almost always under state regulation).

For the planner this means many mid-sized projects, each affecting fewer customers than a transmission project, and a value that is measured customer by customer: how often and how long customers on a feeder are without power. Those reliability measures, and how they become a value per project, are the subject of lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial. GridCap's distribution projects run from $520k to $2,440k, and include a kind of work the other fleets lack: putting a line underground, which removes it from storms and trees at a much higher cost than repairing it.

The RAP guide also describes how distribution planning is changing: rooftop solar, batteries and electric vehicles (distributed energy resources) make power flow both ways on feeders, and some states now require integrated distribution planning, which weighs those resources against building more wires. That is a source of new candidate projects and new alternatives, not a change to the selection model itself.

Vegetation Work Comes Back on a Cycle, So Deferring It Moves Work, Not Removes It

Trees grow into lines. A branch that touches a high-voltage conductor, or comes close enough for current to arc to it (a flash-over), trips the line, and on a transmission line that outage can spread. NERC's standard FAC-003 exists for this: its stated purpose is to manage vegetation on transmission rights-of-way to prevent the vegetation-related outages that could lead to cascading. It applies to overhead transmission lines of 200 kV and above, and to some lower-voltage lines that planners identify as critical. It requires, among other things, documented maintenance strategies, vegetation inspections every year, and completion of each year's planned work. FAC-003 says outright that it is not written for tree contact with distribution lines; tree work on distribution circuits falls under the state's rules, which this course does not survey.

Vegetation projects differ from the other fleets in kind, not only in size. GridCap's are small ($130k to $580k), one year long, and they never end: a trimmed circuit grows back. That makes a vegetation program a cycle: each circuit is trimmed every few years, and the work due in a year is set by when each circuit was last trimmed. vegetation_cycle.py shows it with eight invented circuits on an invented four-year cycle:

On time, the work is level: 64 to 79 miles every year. Defer 2028's two circuits (C03 and C04, 79 miles) by one year and nothing is saved: 2029 now carries 153 miles, twice a normal year, and because each circuit's cycle restarts from its new trim year, the lump comes back in 2033. The deferred circuits also went five years between trims instead of four, so they spent a year with more growth near the lines. Deferring vegetation work moves cost and risk into later years; it does not remove them. A model that values a trim only by what it costs today will defer it every year, which is why lessons Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year and Building the CIPT value function give each intervention a value that depends on when it is done.

Problem statement

A municipal utility (a fictional one) trims the trees along each of its circuits on a fixed cycle: a circuit is due in year $y$ when at least cycle years have passed since it was last trimmed ($y - \text{last} \ge \text{cycle}$). Its crews can trim at most capacity miles a year, and a circuit is trimmed whole or not at all. Work that is due and not done stays due.

Write plan_trims(circuits, cycle, capacity, years) in trims.py. circuits maps a circuit id to (miles, year last trimmed). For each year of years, in order, the crews:

  • take the circuits that are due, the one trimmed longest ago first (ties by circuit id);
  • trim each one whose miles fit in what is left of the year's capacity, and pass over any that do not, going on down the list;
  • record a trimmed circuit's new last-trim year, so its cycle restarts.

The result maps each year to a pair: the ids trimmed that year, sorted, and the backlog, the miles that were due that year but not trimmed. The backlog counts every circuit due and left untrimmed, whenever it first fell due: a circuit carried over from an earlier year counts again in each year it is still waiting, and a circuit longer than capacity, which no year can trim, counts in every year from the one it falls due.

Before you code, trace the instance with A (50 miles, last 2018), B (10, 2019), C (10, 2020), a 3-year cycle and 25 miles of capacity through 2024 by hand. The tests use small instances like it. About fifteen lines.

Problem statement


A gas utility (a fictional one) plans its fleets (mains, valves, regulators) with the same multi-period model as GridCap: a binary $x_{i,t}$ for every project $i$ and every start year $t$ from its earliest to its latest. When a test of the model fails, the first question is "which rows does this project's variables appear in?". Write the function that answers it.


The model's rows, with the names the code gives them:


  • must_<id> if the project is a must-do, otherwise once_<id>: the sum of its start variables.
  • alt_<group> for a project in an alternative group.
  • after_<child>_<t> for each child project and each of the child's start years $t$: $\text{by}(\text{child}, t) \le \text{by}(\text{parent}, t - \text{lag})$, where $\text{by}(i, t)$ is the sum of project $i$'s start variables for years up to $t$. A variable appears in a row only if the row has a term for it.
  • budget_<y> for each plan year $y$, summing the spend of every project at work in $y$; a project started in $t$ with a duration of $d$ years is at work in years $t$ to $t + d - 1$.
  • crew_<program>_<y>, the same as the budget row but one per program.


Write rows_for(pid, projects, years) in rows.py: the set of row names in which at least one of project pid's start variables appears. Only years in years have budget and crew rows.


A parent's variable is in a child's after_<child>_<t> row only when the row's right-hand side can reach it: $\text{by}(\text{parent}, t - \text{lag})$ sums the parent's starts up to $t - \text{lag}$, so the parent appears exactly when $t - \text{lag}$ is at least the parent's earliest start. Worked example: a parent P that may start only in 2028, and its child C (start 2028 to 2030, lag 1).


  • after_C_2028: the right side is $\text{by}(P, 2027)$, which sums no variable of P (P cannot start by 2027), so P is not in this row.
  • after_C_2029: the right side is $\text{by}(P, 2028) = x_{P,2028}$, so P is in it.
  • after_C_2030: the right side is $\text{by}(P, 2029) = x_{P,2028}$, so P is in it.


So P's rows are once_P, after_C_2029, after_C_2030 and its budget and crew rows for 2028; C's own variables are in all three of C's after rows. The tests use small hand-made fleets. About fifteen lines.

Summary


  • Transmission moves bulk power, so its projects are few, large and long. High voltage, long distances, NERC reliability standards, and most of the chained and either-or work.
  • Distribution reaches every customer, so its projects are many and mid-sized. Regulated by the state, valued by the customers each feeder serves.
  • Vegetation work comes back on a cycle, so deferring it moves work, not removes it. FAC-003 requires yearly inspection and planned work on critical transmission lines.
  • Each fleet brings its own rows to the model. Alternatives and precedence come mostly from transmission, compliance must-dos often from vegetation, and crew rows are per fleet.

Design Note: One Model, Fleet Data


It is tempting to build one model per fleet and add the results. Don't: the fleets compete for the same yearly budget and some of their projects depend on each other (P016 waits for P015). Keep one model, and keep what differs between fleets in the data: the program on each project, the crews by program and year, the alternatives and parents. Then a new fleet rule, such as a crew limit, is a loop over programs, and the solver still trades a transmission rebuild against ten trims.
