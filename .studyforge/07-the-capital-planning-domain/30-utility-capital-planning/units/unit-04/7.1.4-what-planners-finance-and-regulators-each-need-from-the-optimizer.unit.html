  14. What planners, finance and regulators need

GridCap's base plan is solved: 27 of 40 projects, NPV $24,260k, every rule kept. Three groups of people now read it, and each asks a different first question. A planner asks "what is in, when, and why is my project out?". A finance analyst asks "what do we spend each year, and does it match what was authorized?". A regulator asks "were the commitments kept, and would you get the same answer again?". A CIPT's requirements (lesson How a utility plans capital: programs, portfolios, rate cases) put all three in one sentence: planners, finance partners and regulators rely on the results, so each solution must be correct, deterministic, explainable and auditable. This lesson reads one plan from each side, then turns their needs into a stakeholder-requirements worksheet: each requirement tied to the model feature that meets it and to a check that proves it on a real run.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course; the requirements below are this course's examples of what each group asks of a CIPT-style optimizer, not any utility's list. The plan is the base scenario of lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock, built and solved by cycle_model.py (its full model is in that lesson's The Model section). Every project ID in this lesson is the small portfolio's: P003 below is small's must-do circuit clearing, not tiny's Coast tower set of lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial.

A stakeholder is anyone who relies on the result or can stop it. A requirement is one thing a stakeholder needs to be true of every result, stated so that it can be checked.

Planners Need a Plan They Can Act On and Explain

Planners own the projects. They will have to tell a fleet team why its project is in next year, deferred to 2031, or out, and they will push back with knowledge the model lacks (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock's override). So their view of a plan is project by project. stakeholder_views.py prints it:

Two things a planner will ask about are in this view, and neither is explained by it.

  • Deferrals. Nine projects start later than their earliest year, each giving up some NPV, because each year's budget is nearly full and moving them frees money for work worth more (lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows read the same nine).
  • Good projects left out. P002 has the best NPV per dollar of everything unfunded, and is still out. The reason is not in the ratio: P002 is the repair option for asset A001, whose replacement P001 is funded, and the alternative row allows one of the two (lesson Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace)). P030 is out for the same kind of reason: its group's other option, P031, is funded.

A ratio cannot explain a selection; only the model's rows can. That is the planner's real requirement: for every project, a reason in terms of the rules: a must-do, a window, an alternative taken, a budget it did not fit. Section 9 builds those reasons: forcing a project in or out and pricing it (lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out), diffs between scenarios (lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted), and messages written for planners (lesson Writing results for planners, finance and auditors). Planners also need speed: a what-if that takes an hour does not get asked. Section 11 is about keeping runs fast.

Finance Needs Spend by Year That Matches What Was Authorized

Finance owns the money. Its first question is about each year, because budgets are authorized year by year and money not spent in a year is, in GridCap's model, simply not spent (lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps shows the carry-over alternative). The same plan, read by finance:

Every year is within its budget, and the plan leaves $600k unspent in all, between $50k and $150k a year: with whole projects costing $130k or more, the last few dollars of a year can rarely be used. Finance's requirements follow from the table.

  • Spend by year, never only a total. A plan within its five-year total can still break a year.
  • Spend that adds up. The yearly spend must equal the capex of the funded projects, spread by each project's cost profile; a report that does not reconcile is not trusted again.
  • Value in money terms finance recognizes. NPV at the company's discount rate, in stated dollars (lessons Present value, NPV and discount rates to Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year), with the solver's status: an NPV from a run that stopped early (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap) is a lower bound on the best plan, not the best plan.
  • The cost of every rule and override, priced the way lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock priced forcing P024 in.

Regulators Need Commitments Kept and a Result Anyone Can Reproduce

The regulator does not choose projects, but decides whether customers pay for them. Lesson How a utility plans capital: programs, portfolios, rate cases gave the tests: an investment enters the rate base if it is prudent and used and useful, and a commission may review a project after the fact and disallow what it finds imprudent. The RAP guide also describes service quality indices, measures of the frequency and duration of outages and of customer service, which some commissions tie to penalties or rewards; lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial turns reliability measures into project value. The regulator's view of a plan:

Every must-do is funded and every region receives more than its floor. The last line carries the two things an auditor needs to repeat the run: the snapshot of the inputs and the solver settings. That gives the regulator's requirements.

  • Commitments are rows, and each one is checked on the result. A mandate written as a weight can be dropped silently (lesson Must-do vs. discretionary planning groups); written as a row it either holds or the run is infeasible.
  • The result can be reproduced. Given the snapshot, the settings and the same software, a rerun gives the same plan. Lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock saw one rerun repeat; lessons Where nondeterminism comes from: threads, time limits, ties to Versioned input snapshots, pinned solvers and run manifests cover what a snapshot alone does not fix (threads, time limits, solver versions) and how a run manifest records it. A rerun that returns a different plan with the same NPV is not by itself an error: a model can have several optimal plans, and which one the solver returns can depend on exactly those things (lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs measured CP-SAT with eight workers returning different plans from run to run).
  • Every decision is traceable. Who changed which input, when, and what it cost: the audit trail of lesson Manual overrides as bounds, with an audit trail. A plan whose overrides cannot be traced cannot be defended as prudent.

  1. Coding practice

    An auditor's replay check

    • a regulator checks that a stored run is reproduced from its snapshot and settings, field by field

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A state commission's auditor (in a fictional water utility's rate case) has the utility's stored record of the run behind its plan of record, and replays it. Each run record is a dictionary with a snapshot (the id of its inputs), its solver settings, the termination status, the objective value, and the plan (project id to start year). The auditor wants a list of what the replay did not reproduce.

Write audit(original, replay, tol=1e-6) in audit.py. It returns a list of findings, empty when the replay reproduced the run, in this order:

  • "inputs differ" when the snapshots differ. A replay of other inputs proves nothing about the run, so this is then the only finding.
  • "settings differ: <names>" with the setting names whose values differ, sorted and joined by ", " (a setting only one run has differs too).
  • "status differs".
  • "objective differs" when the objectives are further apart than tol times the larger of 1 and the original's absolute value. Solvers compute in floating point, so two equal answers can differ in the last digits.
  • "plan differs: <ids>" with the project ids whose start years differ, sorted and joined by ", "; a project in one plan only is one of them.

Before you code, decide which findings a replay with 8 threads instead of 1, the same objective and one project moved by a year should produce. The tests use hand-made run records. About fifteen lines.

A Requirements Worksheet Ties Each Need to a Model Feature and a Check


Needs written in prose are forgotten; needs written as checks are run on every result. A stakeholder-requirements worksheet is a table with one row per requirement: who needs it, what it says, which part of the model or the run meets it, and the check that proves it held. In a requirements document this is often called a traceability matrix, because it traces each requirement to what implements and tests it. requirements_worksheet.py writes GridCap's worksheet as data, each check a function of the run, and evaluates it:






Four habits make a worksheet useful rather than decorative.


  • Each requirement is checkable on one result. "The plan is fair to regions" is not; "each region receives its floor" is (R7). Rewrite a vague need until a function can return true or false on it.
  • The check reads the result, not the model. R4 sums the plan's spend from the cost profiles; it does not trust the budget row to have been written correctly. A missing or wrong row is exactly what such a check catches (lesson Unit-testing one constraint at a time writes one test per constraint the same way).
  • A requirement with no check is a gap, and the worksheet says so. The planners' "a reason for every project" has no row here yet, because section 9 builds it; a worksheet that lists it as untested is more honest than one that leaves it out.
  • "Proven optimal" means within tolerances. R6 passes because the solver reported OPTIMAL with a relative gap tolerance of 0; even then the solver's own feasibility and integrality tolerances apply (lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters).


The worksheet is also the shortest answer to an interview question this role is asked: "how do you know your optimizer is right?" Show the table, say which rows are checked on every run, and say which needs are not yet covered.

Problem statement


A water utility (a fictional one) keeps its stakeholder-requirements worksheet as data. Each requirement is a dictionary {"id", "stakeholder", "text"}. Each automated check that ran on the latest plan is a dictionary {"name", "covers", "passed"}, where covers lists the requirement ids the check proves. Before the plan goes to the regulator, the team wants the worksheet's status, requirement by requirement and stakeholder by stakeholder.


Write two functions in worksheet.py:


  • trace(requirements, checks): each requirement id mapped to one of three states. UNTESTED when no check covers it; MET when at least one check covers it and every check covering it passed; FAILED when a check covering it failed.
  • by_stakeholder(requirements, status): for each stakeholder, how many of their requirements are in each of the three states, with every state present, zero or not.


Before you code, take a requirement covered by two checks, one passed and one failed, and say which state it is in and why the worksheet must not call it met. The tests use small hand-made worksheets. About twenty lines.

Summary


  • Planners need a plan they can act on and explain. Project by project: in, when, out, and a reason in terms of the rules, which a value-per-dollar ratio cannot give.
  • Finance needs spend by year that matches what was authorized. Within each year's budget, reconciled to the funded capex, with NPV and the solver's status.
  • Regulators need commitments kept and a result anyone can reproduce. Mandates as rows checked on the result, a snapshot with the settings, and an audit trail.
  • A requirements worksheet ties each need to a model feature and a check. One row per requirement; a need with no check is listed as a gap.

Design Note: Write the Worksheet Before the Model


The worksheet is most useful before any code exists. Sit with a planner, a finance analyst and the regulatory team, write each need as a row, and agree on the check. The rows then become the model's constraints and the test suite's first tests (section 12). A model built first and documented after tends to have rows nobody asked for and needs nobody wrote down.
