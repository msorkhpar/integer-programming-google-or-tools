Every model in sections 1 to 6 took GridCap's budgets, projects and mandates as given and asked which projects to fund. This section asks where those inputs come from, because an engineer who builds a capital-planning optimizer is asked about them in every review and every interview. Here is the situation the rest of the module works in. GridCap's fleet teams have sent in 40 candidate projects for 2027 to 2031 (the small portfolio). Together they ask for $69,810k. The five yearly budgets add up to $42,230k. Someone has to decide what is funded, and the decision has to hold up in front of finance and in front of the state regulator who sets GridCap's rates.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course. Its projects, budgets and floors are teaching data and describe no real utility. It comes in three portfolios, tiny (8 projects), small (40) and medium (400), and each numbers its own projects and assets from P001 and A001, so one ID names different work in different portfolios: tiny's P003 is a Coast tower set replacement, small's P003 a Valley circuit clearing. This section says which portfolio an ID comes from whenever a lesson uses more than one. The facts about how real utilities are regulated come from the sources in the Further reading line, chiefly the Regulatory Assistance Project's guide Electricity Regulation in the US (Lazar, 2016).

The words this lesson defines, in the order it uses them: capital and operating expense, rate case, revenue requirement, rate base, program, portfolio, envelope.

Capital Buys Assets That Last, So Its Cost Is Recovered Over Their Life

A utility spends money in two ways, and the difference shapes everything below.

  • Capital expenditure (capex) buys or rebuilds something that serves customers for many years: a transmission tower, a substation transformer, a mile of underground cable. The asset goes on the books at its cost and is depreciated: a share of its cost is counted as an expense each year of its accounting life. The RAP guide lists the kinds of things that count: buildings, power plants, vehicles, poles, wires, transformers, computers and software.
  • Operating expense (opex, often called O&M for operations and maintenance) is spent and used up within the year: labor, fuel, repairs that do not extend an asset's life, taxes.

Why does this matter to an optimizer? Because the two are recovered from customers differently. In the regulatory formula below, an operating expense is recovered dollar for dollar in the year it is spent. A capital dollar is recovered over the asset's life, and while it is not yet recovered the utility earns a return on it. A capital plan is therefore a commitment that shows up in customers' rates for decades, and that is why the plan is reviewed so closely.

Every GridCap project is a capital project: each has a cost_profile of capital spend per year of work, and the models of section 6 fund it or not. Whether a particular real utility books a particular kind of work (vegetation clearing, for instance) as capital or as operating expense is an accounting and regulatory decision this course does not survey; GridCap simply treats all three of its programs as capital.

A Rate Case Turns Capital Into Rates Through the Rate Base

The wires business is treated as a natural monopoly: one network per area is cheaper than several, so one company serves the area and is regulated instead of facing competitors. The RAP guide puts about 75% of the US population with investor-owned utilities, private companies regulated by their state, and the rest with consumer-owned utilities (municipal utilities, utility districts and cooperatives), which are governed differently. For an investor-owned utility, a state commission (a public utility commission, or PUC) decides what it may charge. The commission does this in a rate case (also called a general rate case or rate proceeding): the utility files evidence of its costs and asks for rates that recover them; commission staff and intervenors (other parties, such as consumer advocates and large customers) test that evidence; and the commission decides. The RAP guide's typical schedule runs about nine months from the filing to the decision, with discovery, written testimony, rebuttal and hearings in between.

What the commission decides first is the revenue requirement: the total revenue the utility needs in a year to cover its costs and earn a fair return. The guide gives the basic formula as

with depreciation and taxes counted among the operating expenses. The pieces:

  • The rate base is the utility's investment in long-lived assets that serve customers, net of accumulated depreciation: plant in service at original cost, less the depreciation already charged, plus or minus a few adjustments the commission approves (working capital, deferred taxes). An asset that is half depreciated earns a return on half its cost.
  • The rate of return is the percentage the commission allows the utility to earn on its rate base, set high enough to attract capital (debt and equity) for a business of its risk.
  • To enter the rate base, an investment must generally be prudent (chosen and built at a reasonable cost with sound management) and used and useful (actually in service, and something without which costs would be higher or service worse). A commission can review a large project after it is built and disallow the part it finds imprudent, so the utility never earns on it.

The test year is the 12-month period whose costs and sales the case is built on: a recent actual year, or a forecast year. A forecast test year rests on the utility's budgets for that year, which is one route by which a capital plan reaches a rate case.

revenue_requirement.py follows one capital addition through this formula. The numbers are invented: $5,310k placed in service, a 30-year book life with straight-line depreciation (the same amount every year), a 7.5% allowed return, and the rate base taken as the average of each year's opening and closing net plant (the guide notes that the average is the usual accounting recommendation). Taxes, working capital and O&M are left out to keep the arithmetic visible.

Read it in three steps.

  • Depreciation returns the capital. $5,310k over 30 years is $177k a year, every year. Over the whole life it adds up to the $5,310k spent, no more.
  • The return falls as the rate base falls. In year 1 the average net plant is $5,222k ($5,310k less half a year's depreciation), and 7.5% of it is $392k. By year 30 almost everything is depreciated and the return is $7k.
  • Customers pay for the addition for 30 years, $11,284k in all, more than twice its cost. The same $5,310k spent as an operating expense would be recovered once, in the year it was spent, with no return.

The guide also names the incentive this creates. Because profit is tied to the rate base, the Averch-Johnson effect holds that a utility can raise its profit by investing more. That is one reason regulators review capital plans for prudence, and one reason the plan an optimizer produces must be explainable: it is part of the case for what customers will pay.

Problem statement

A small water utility (a fictional one) asks its commission for rates. Its pipe and plant additions are placed in service at the start of a year, and the commission in this exercise uses three conventions:

  • Straight-line depreciation over a book life of life years: an addition of cost $C$ is depreciated $C / \text{life}$ in its first year of service and in each following year until its life is over, and never after.
  • End-of-year rate base: in year $y$, each addition counts at its cost less all the depreciation charged on it up to and including year $y$, and never below zero. An addition not yet in service is not in the rate base.
  • Operating expenses (O&M) are recovered in the year they are spent, dollar for dollar.

Write revenue_requirement(additions, life, rate, om, years) in rates.py. additions maps the year an addition is placed in service to its cost ($k), rate is the allowed rate of return, om maps a year to that year's O&M ($k, a missing year is 0), and the result maps each year of years to its revenue requirement:

Before you code, work one case by hand: $1,000k placed in 2027, a 4-year life and a 10% return. What are 2027's depreciation, rate base and revenue requirement, and what is 2031's? Then decide how one addition's age in year $y$ tells you whether it depreciates, and how much of it is left in the rate base.

This exercise's rate base is the end-of-year one, not the lesson's. revenue_requirement.py takes the average of each year's opening and closing net plant, the convention the lesson's source calls usual; this commission uses the closing value alone, which is also a convention a commission may set, and which keeps each year's arithmetic to one subtraction. The two differ: for the $1,000k case above, 2027's rate base is $750k at year end and $875k on average, so the requirement is $325k here and would be $337.5k with the lesson's average. The tests use the end-of-year values.

The tests compare your numbers with values worked out by hand. About fifteen lines.

A Program Is a Line of Work, and the Portfolio Is Everything Competing for the Same Money


Utilities organize capital spending in layers. This course uses three words for them.


  • A project is one piece of work on one asset: replace this transformer, rebuild this line, trim this circuit. It has a cost, a timing window and a value. This is the unit the optimizer says yes or no to.
  • A program is a line of work with its own team, its own kind of asset and often its own budget line: transmission, distribution and vegetation management in GridCap. They are the three planning teams a Capital Investment Planning and Prioritization Tool (CIPT) serves: the kind of software this course builds toward, which decides which capital investments to fund, when, and why. Lesson Transmission, distribution and vegetation investments describes each.
  • The portfolio is the set of all candidate projects, across programs and regions, that compete for the same money in the same planning cycle. Choosing a portfolio is choosing which projects, from every program, are funded together.


An envelope is the amount of money set aside for a program, a region or a year before projects are chosen: the budget rows of section 6 are yearly envelopes, and GridCap's region_floor is a floor on what each region receives over the plan, which GridCap's data defines as a rate-case commitment (lesson Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend wrote it as a row).


portfolio_by_program.py shows what GridCap's small portfolio asks for, program by program and region by region:



The candidate list asks for 1.65 times the money there is. That ratio is normal for a capital plan, and it is the reason a plan needs an optimizer at all: if every request fit, there would be nothing to choose. The programs are also very unequal. Transmission is 63% of what is asked with 14 projects; vegetation is 6% with 12. A rule such as "fund the best projects per dollar until the money runs out" would compare a $130k trim with a $5,840k line rebuild directly. Whether that is the right comparison is a value question (lessons Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial to Building the CIPT value function), not a solver question.

Problem statement


A city water utility (a fictional one) collects capital requests from three programs: water mains, treatment plants and meters. Each request starts in a year and spends a profile of amounts ($k) in that year and the following ones. Finance has already set an envelope for some programs and years: the most that program may spend that year. Before anything is selected, the capital planning team wants to see where the requests already break an envelope.


Write two functions in envelopes.py:


  • requested_by_year(requests, programs, years): for every program in programs and every year in years, what the requests would spend there if all were funded as asked. Every program and year appears, with 0 where nothing is asked. Spend in a year outside years is left out.
  • oversubscribed(requests, envelopes): one tuple (program, year, requested, excess) for every envelope that the requests exceed, with excess the amount above the envelope, sorted by program and then year. An envelope met exactly is not exceeded.


A request for a program not in programs (or with no envelope) is left out. Before you code, take a request that starts in 2027 with the profile (500, 300, 100) and write down what it adds to 2027, 2028 and 2029. The tests use small hand-made request lists. About fifteen lines.

The Capital Plan Is a Selection Under Budgets the Optimizer Did Not Set


Put the pieces together and the shape of the problem is clear, and it is old. Klevorick's 1966 Princeton paper states the classic capital rationing problem: a firm has a fixed budget in each of $T$ periods, and must choose, from the investment opportunities over its horizon, the best subset that keeps within each period's budget. MIT's Applied Mathematical Programming, chapter 9, writes it as a go/no-go integer program, the kind section 3 taught you to solve:



where $x_j = 1$ accepts investment $j$, $c_j$ is its contribution, $a_{ij}$ the amount of resource $i$ (cash in period $i$, crews) it uses, and $b_i$ the amount available. This is the multi-dimensional knapsack of lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack, and section 6 extended it with start years, mandates, dependencies and floors. The OR-Tools knapsack page solves the one-row version with a dedicated knapsack solver; the GridCap models use MathOpt because every rule after the first needs more rows than a knapsack has.


What the domain adds is a clear line between what the optimizer decides and what it is given:

The optimizer never sets the budget. When a planner asks "how much should we spend?", the honest answer from the model is a set of plans, one per budget, and what each one buys; the budget itself is a business and regulatory decision. Lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock shows how the planning cycle runs those plans, and lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer what each group of people needs from them.


Summary


  • Capital buys assets that last, so its cost is recovered over their life. Operating expenses are recovered in the year they are spent; capital through depreciation and a return.
  • A rate case turns capital into rates through the rate base. Revenue requirement = rate base × rate of return + operating expenses; the rate base is plant net of accumulated depreciation, and an investment enters it only if prudent and used and useful.
  • A program is a line of work, and the portfolio is everything competing for the same money. GridCap's candidates ask for 1.65 times the budgets, which is why the plan is a selection.
  • The capital plan is a selection under budgets the optimizer did not set. It is the classic capital rationing problem, a multi-dimensional knapsack, extended in section 6.

Design Note: Keep the Budget an Input


It is tempting to let the optimizer "find the right budget" by adding a budget variable and a cost on it. Resist it unless finance asks for exactly that model. The budget is set by people who weigh things the model does not hold (rate impact, financing, the rate case's timing), and a plan that silently moved the budget would be a plan no one authorized. Treat the budget as data, run one scenario per budget the planners want to compare (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock), and report what each one buys.
