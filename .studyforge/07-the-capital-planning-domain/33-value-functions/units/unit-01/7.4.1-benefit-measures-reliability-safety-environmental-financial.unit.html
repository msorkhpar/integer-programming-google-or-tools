  16. Benefit measures

GridCap's planning board meets to rank the tiny portfolio's eight projects. Finance brings a list sorted by NPV, and tiny's P004, a $3,000k breaker replacement, is on top. The distribution engineer objects: P003, a tower set replacement on the Coast, keeps 12,000 customers from losing power for four hours each time those towers fail, and NPV does not see a single one of them. The safety lead adds that vegetation work prevents fires. Each of them is right about something the others' numbers leave out.

Before any of this can go into a model, each of these claims has to become a number with a unit: a benefit measure. This lesson defines the four families of measures a utility's capital plan uses (reliability, safety, environmental and financial), computes them for every GridCap tiny project, and shows that each one ranks the projects in a different order. Lessons Normalizing different units to Building the CIPT value function then turn the four into one value per project.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course. The consequence table, the customer count and the outage log in this lesson are invented too; no number here describes a real utility. Every project and asset ID in this lesson is the tiny portfolio's: tiny's P003 is a Coast tower set, while small's P003 (lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer) is a Valley circuit clearing, because each portfolio numbers its own projects from P001.

A Benefit Measure Counts One Consequence, in One Unit

A capital project is worth doing because of what it prevents or creates. Replacing a worn transformer prevents some of its future failures, and each failure would have had consequences: customers lose power, someone may be hurt, oil may burn, and the utility pays for an emergency repair. A benefit measure is one of those consequences, counted in one unit, as the amount the project changes it. The utility's job is to pick the measures, and each needs three things written down:

  • What it counts. "Customer-minutes of interruption avoided", not "reliability".
  • Its unit. Customer-minutes, incidents, tonnes, thousands of dollars.
  • Its direction. Every measure in this module counts something avoided or gained, so more is always better. A measure that counts something bad (minutes of outage after the project) has to be turned around before it can join the others; counting the reduction does that.

CO2e (carbon-dioxide equivalent) puts different greenhouse gases on one scale: a tonne of a gas counts as the tonnes of CO2 that would warm the climate as much. A breaker's insulating gas, SF6, warms it far more per tonne than CO2, so a leak of a few kilograms counts as tonnes of CO2e.

SAIFI Counts Interruptions and SAIDI Counts Minutes, per Customer Served

Reliability is the measure regulators watch most closely, and utilities report it with standard indices defined in IEEE Standard 1366. Two of them matter here. Let $N_T$ be the number of customers the utility serves, and let an outage log list, for each sustained interruption $j$, the customers it cut off, $N_j$, and the minutes until their power came back, $r_j$:

  • SAIFI, the System Average Interruption Frequency Index, counts customer interruptions: one customer losing power once is one customer interruption. Divided by the customers served, it is how many times the average customer lost power in the year.
  • SAIDI, the System Average Interruption Duration Index, counts customer-minutes of interruption: 1,000 customers out for 60 minutes are 60,000 customer-minutes. Divided by the customers served, it is how many minutes the average customer spent without power.
  • CAIDI, the Customer Average Interruption Duration Index, is the average restoration time of the customers who were interrupted.

Both indices count only sustained interruptions: under the IEEE method, those longer than five minutes. Shorter ones are momentary (a breaker that trips and recloses) and are tracked by a separate index. The denominator is all customers served, not the customers who were interrupted; dividing by those instead gives CAIDI's denominator, which is a different question.

Utilities also report each index with and without major event days, the storm days whose daily SAIDI is far above normal (the IEEE method sets the threshold from the previous five years). The two can differ a lot: the U.S. Energy Information Administration reported an average of about two hours a year without major events since 2013, and 5.8 hours in 2018 with them. A plan's benefit is normally stated against the "without major events" index, the part routine renewal changes.

Here is a year of GridCap outages (invented), eight of them, two momentary:

Check it by hand. The six sustained outages interrupted 12,000 + 4,000 + 1,500 + 400 + 6,000 + 1,200 = 25,100 customers, so SAIFI is 25,100 / 600,000 = 0.0418. Their customer-minutes add up to 5,214,000, so SAIDI is 5,214,000 / 600,000 = 8.69 minutes. The 20,000 customers of the 2-minute outage count in neither index: a momentary outage is not a sustained interruption, however many customers it touches.

A project changes these indices by removing customer interruptions and customer-minutes from future years. Because the denominator $N_T$ is the same for every project, a project's cut in SAIDI is just its customer-minutes avoided divided by $N_T$, and its cut in SAIFI is its customer interruptions avoided divided by $N_T$. The two carry the same information as the counts, in a different unit; lesson Normalizing different units shows why that difference in unit must not change any decision.

    A cooperative's outages, restored in steps

    • SAIFI and SAIDI from customer interruptions and customer-minutes over customers served, sustained only

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A rural electric cooperative serves customers_served customers. When a storm takes a line down its crews rarely restore everyone at once: they switch some customers onto another feeder within minutes, fix the main line an hour later, and reach the last houses after that. So each outage in the cooperative's log is a list of restoration steps: Step(customers, minute) says that customers customers came back on supply minute minutes after the outage began. Every customer in an outage lost power at its start.

Write indices(outages, customers_served) in outages.py, returning the year's SAIFI, SAIDI and CAIDI as {"SAIFI": ..., "SAIDI": ..., "CAIDI": ...}. The cooperative follows the IEEE rule for what counts: a customer who is back on supply within five minutes (five included) had a momentary interruption, which counts in none of the three indices, even when other customers of the same outage waited much longer. CAIDI is the average wait of an interrupted customer, and 0 in a year with no sustained interruption.

Work the first test's outage by hand before you code: 600 customers restored at 30 minutes and 400 at 90, out of 10,000 served. How many customer interruptions is that, and how many customer-minutes?

Expected Failures Avoided Turn Into Every Non-Financial Measure


Where does a project's "customer-minutes avoided" come from? From the failures it prevents. Lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal (and lessons Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) to Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year, Asset risk) model each GridCap asset's failures with a Weibull curve of shape $\beta$ and scale $\eta$ years. If a failed asset is repaired just enough to run again, the expected number of failures between ages $a$ and $a + h$ is



An intervention makes the asset younger. As in lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal, give each intervention a depth $d$: the asset's age becomes $(1 - d)\,a$. This module uses $d = 1$ for a replacement, rebuild or undergrounding (the asset is new), $0.8$ for clearing or widening a vegetation corridor, and $0.5$ for a repair or a trim (lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year writes the same reset the other way round, as the share of its age the asset keeps; the section Two Renewal Conventions, One Failure Model below compares the two). Benefits are counted over an evaluation window of 10 years from the plan's first year, 2027 to 2036. A project started in year $t$, on an asset of age $a_t$ by then, has $h_t = 2027 + 10 - t$ years of the window left and avoids



expected failures: those the old asset would have had, less those the renewed one will have.

Two Renewal Conventions, One Failure Model


Lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year also values a renewal by the failures it removes from the asset's Weibull curve, but it writes the renewal down differently. Side by side:

The two parameters are one number seen from opposite ends: $d = 1 - \rho$. A repair keeps half its age ($\rho = 0.5$) and has half taken away ($d = 0.5$); a replacement keeps none ($\rho = 0$, $d = 1$). (Lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year uses $\rho_i$ for something unrelated, GridCap's benefit ratio.)


Only clearing and widening disagree. Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts gave every partial intervention the same 0.5, and lessons Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) to Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year kept it. This module gives corridor clearing and widening a deeper reset, $d = 0.8$ (that is, $\rho = 0.2$): taking a corridor back to its full cleared width removes most of the growth that causes its outages, where a trim cuts back only what is nearest the line. Both values are assumptions about the fictional GridCap, not measurements; a utility sets them from its own records of how failure rates change after each kind of work.

So there is one method, and this lesson uses a short form of it. To value a renewal, follow the asset's age year by year with and without the work (lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year): it ages through the years of work, then keeps the share $\rho$ of the age it had reached and ages again; each year's benefit is the drop in expected failures times what one failure does, discounted if it is money. This lesson's $N(a_t, h_t) - N((1 - d)\,a_t, h_t)$ is that method with the years of work left out and nothing discounted, written with $d = 1 - \rho$. It keeps each measure one difference a reader can check by hand, and it counts customer-minutes, incidents and tonnes in their own units, which this module does not discount: a customer-minute in 2036 counts as much as one in 2028.


The rest of the course keeps both, each for its own job. The risk objective that section 8 trades against NPV is Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's, age path, time for the work and lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts's $\rho$ included, because it is stated in dollars beside NPV. The value function of lessons Normalizing different units to Building the CIPT value function is built on this lesson's measures and depths. A utility that wants its value function on the full method changes failures_avoided to sum along the age path, and every coefficient after it follows.


Financial Benefit Is NPV, and Each Consequence Is Counted Once


The financial measure is the project's net present value at its start year, $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ in $k: its money benefits discounted to today, less its discounted cost. Lessons Present value, NPV and discount rates to Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year (Financial evaluation) teach how it is computed; GridCap stores it as Project.npv[start].


A failure also has a money consequence: the emergency repair. GridCap records it as the asset's cof in $k, and lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year turns it into a risk reduction in dollars. Is it inside GridCap's NPV? No. Lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year rebuilt every NPV entry from three things: the project's cost profile, a benefit equal to its capex times a benefit ratio drawn between 1.15 and 2.05, and a decay of 3% to 8% of that benefit per year of delay. No term uses cof or the failure curve. GridCap's NPV is a stand-in for the money case finance would bring, and it does not say what that money is.


So the course counts the avoided repair cost in exactly one place: the risk objective of lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year, which section 8 puts beside NPV as a second objective and trades against it at a stated price. Counted there, it must not come back in the value function too, so the value function does not add $F \times \text{cof}$ as a fifth measure. Adding it would count the same dollars twice, and a doubly counted effect gets twice the weight it deserves in the final decision. The rule is about where the dollars already sit. With a real utility's NPV built from avoided repair bills, the repair cost is inside NPV, and it is added neither as a fifth measure nor as a separate risk objective summed with that NPV; GridCap's NPV has no such term, which is why section 8 may weigh GridCap's NPV and risk against each other without counting anything twice. The UK government's multi-criteria analysis manual makes the same rule for public appraisals: the same impact may be shown from several points of view, but it is counted once when options are compared.


The practical test from that manual: take two projects that are identical in every measure except one, say customer-minutes. Would the board prefer the one with more? If yes, customer-minutes carry value that NPV does not, and they belong in the value function. If the board says "no, the customer-minutes are already priced in the NPV", then they are a double count and must go. Reliability, safety and environment pass that test at GridCap: none of them is in its NPV.


Correlated is not the same as doubly counted. Tiny's P006 scores high on reliability, safety and emissions because all three come from the same failures avoided. That is fine: each counts a different consequence of those failures (customers in the dark, people hurt, gas released), and the board values each separately.


Each Measure Ranks the Projects Differently


Here are all eight tiny projects, each started as early as it can, in all four measures (SAIDI is the customer-minutes column divided by 600,000):




Four measures, three different winners. P004 tops the finance list and is last on safety. P003 tops reliability and is next to last on emissions. So the board needs one number per project that weighs all four, and a model needs it too: a MILP has one objective. Combining measures in different units is not a matter of adding them, and lesson Normalizing different units shows what goes wrong when you try.


Summary


  • A benefit measure counts one consequence, in one unit. Write down what it counts, its unit, and its direction; count reductions so that more is always better.
  • SAIFI counts interruptions and SAIDI counts minutes, per customer served. SAIFI = customer interruptions / customers served; SAIDI = customer-minutes / customers served; only sustained (over five minutes) interruptions count. GridCap's log gives SAIFI 0.0418 and SAIDI 8.69.
  • Expected failures avoided turn into every non-financial measure. Failures avoided (from the Weibull curve and the intervention's depth) times what one failure does: P003 avoids 0.574 failures, 1,653,216 customer-minutes.
  • Two renewal conventions share one failure model. This lesson's depth is lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's share kept turned around, $d = 1 - \rho$, and its formula is Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's year-by-year age path with no years of work and no discounting. Clearing and widening take $d = 0.8$ here and $\rho = 0.5$ there; section 8's risk objective uses Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's, this module's value function this lesson's.
  • Financial benefit is NPV, and each consequence is counted once. GridCap's NPV has no repair-cost term; the avoided repair cost is counted once, in lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's risk objective, so it is not added to the value function as well.
  • Each measure ranks the projects differently. On GridCap tiny, reliability, safety and finance each pick a different first project.

Design Note: Measures Before Weights


It is tempting to start with the weights ("safety matters most") and fill in measures later. The order matters the other way round. A weight means nothing until you know what one unit of its measure is (lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in), and the arguments that slow a value function down for months are nearly always about what a measure counts: does vegetation work prevent fires or only outages, are customer-minutes counted with or without major events, is the repair bill in NPV or not. Settle the measures, their units and their sources first, and write them down in a table like the one above.
