Lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial left the GridCap board with four lists that disagree. An analyst offers a quick fix: add each project's four benefits into one score and sort. Tiny's P003, the tower set, comes out on top. A week later a colleague redoes the sheet with reliability in SAIDI minutes instead of customer-minutes, the unit the regulator reports, and P004, the breaker replacement, comes out on top. Same projects, same benefits, a different winner, and nobody changed their mind about anything.

This lesson finds the cause and the cure in four rules: raw measures cannot be added; dividing each measure by a reference in its own unit makes the units cancel; when scores are added across the projects of a plan, zero must mean "no benefit"; and the references are fixed before the candidates are scored.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course, and its consequence table and references are invented. The first three rules use the tiny portfolio of lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial and the last uses small, and the two number their projects independently: tiny's P006 is a transformer replacement, small's P006 a vegetation corridor widening.

The rules below combine scores with the board's swing weights, one per measure. Lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in shows how they are elicited; here they are only used. The board rates the four measures 100 (reliability), 80 (safety), 60 (financial) and 20 (environmental), and each weight is its rating divided by the total, 260:

Adding Raw Measures Lets the Units Choose the Winner

The analyst's score is the plain sum of the four measures of lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial, each in its own unit:

Compare these rankings with lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial's. The first is exactly the reliability ranking, and the second is exactly the financial one. In customer-minutes, reliability numbers run to the millions and drown the rest: P003's sum is 1,653,792.6, of which 1,653,216 are customer-minutes. In SAIDI minutes the same benefit is 2.76, NPV's hundreds and thousands of $k take over, and the ranking becomes the finance list. Safety, in fractions of an incident, never mattered in either.

A sum of raw measures is a weighted sum whose weights nobody chose: each measure's weight is "one per unit", and the unit is an accident of how the data was stored. Changing a unit is not a change of opinion, so it must not be able to change a decision. That is the test every combined score has to pass, and the raw sum fails it.

A Normalized Score Divides by a Reference, So the Units Cancel

The cure is to divide each measure by a reference amount of the same measure, in the same unit, before anything is added:

$m_{i,k}$ is project $i$'s benefit in measure $k$ and $R_k$ is measure $k$'s reference. The result $n_{i,k}$ is the normalized score: a pure number with no unit, saying what share of the reference the project delivers. $n = 0.5$ means "half the reference amount", whatever the unit.

Why the units cancel: restating a measure in another unit multiplies every amount of it by the same factor $c > 0$ (customer-minutes to SAIDI minutes: $c = 1/600{,}000$; $k to $M: $c = 1/1{,}000$). The reference is an amount of the same measure, so it is multiplied too, and

The normalized score is unchanged, so every sum built from it, every ranking and every plan chosen on it are unchanged as well.

GridCap's references (lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in explains where they come from) are 3,000,000 customer-minutes, 1 safety incident, 200 tCO2e and $5,000k. Here is the tiny portfolio scored in those units, and again with every measure in a second set of units (SAIDI minutes, $M and kilograms of CO2e), with the references restated the same way:

The value used for the ranking is the weighted sum of the scores, with the weights in the table at the top of the lesson. Its two versions differ by $1.4 \times 10^{-17}$, floating-point rounding, and the rankings are the same. P003 delivers 55% of the reliability reference whether you call it 1,653,216 customer-minutes or 2.76 SAIDI minutes.

The one way to break this is to rescale a measure and forget its reference. Lesson Building the CIPT value function does that on purpose and shows the ranking move; a test of the value function should catch it.

Problem statement

A city's transit agency ranks bus-stop upgrades by three measures: rider time saved, complaints avoided and operating savings. The data comes from three teams, and each records amounts in the unit it likes: rider time in minutes or hours, complaints one by one or by the dozen, savings in dollars or thousands of dollars. Every amount arrives as a pair (amount, unit), and so does each measure's reference, the swing the agency's board agreed on. UNITS and in_base (given) say what each unit measures and how many base units it is.

Write score(benefit, reference, weights) in stops.py: the weighted sum, over the measures, of the benefit as a share of its reference. Then write rank(projects, reference, weights), the project names from the highest score to the lowest, with equal scores in name order so that the list is the same on every run.

A team switching from hours to minutes, or the board restating a reference in dollars instead of $k, has not changed anyone's mind about any project, so it must not change any score. About eight lines.

Zero Must Mean No Benefit When Scores Are Added Across Projects


A common alternative is the min-max (or "0 to 100") scale: the worst candidate scores 0 on each measure and the best scores 1, with the others in between:



It also cancels units (a unit change multiplies the top and the bottom of the fraction alike), and it is the "relative scale" of decision analysis, anchored at the least and most preferred options, much as the Celsius scale is anchored at freezing and boiling water. For choosing one option out of several it works well.


A capital plan does not choose one project. It chooses a set, and its value is the sum of the selected projects' values. Under min-max, a project's score is its benefit minus the worst candidate's, so every selected project pays a deduction of $\sum_k w_k L_k / (H_k - L_k)$ in value, once per project. A plan of two projects pays it twice; a plan of one pays it once. The scale's zero is not "no benefit" but "as little as the worst candidate", and adding scores multiplies that error by the number of projects.


Compare two tiny plans: two vegetation projects, P005 and P008 ($830k together), against one line rebuild, P002 ($2,730k), all tiny's. On each scale, the program sums the weighted scores, with the weights in the table at the top of the lesson. To isolate the zero, the "from zero" scale uses the same top as min-max, the best candidate:






The two scales prefer different plans. P005 is the worst candidate on reliability and on NPV, so min-max scores those two at zero, as if the $123k of NPV and the 17,092 customer-minutes it delivers did not exist. It is scored only on what it delivers beyond the worst, while P002 is scored on its excess over the same worst amount, deducted once. With two projects the pair pays the deduction twice, and the single project wins.


On the from-zero scale a project with no benefit scores exactly 0 and adding it to a plan changes nothing, which is what a sum over projects needs. That is why the CIPT value function uses $n = m / R$: a ratio scale whose zero is "no benefit". The general rule: when a score is added up over a set, an additive shift in the score becomes a per-item bonus or penalty, so the scale's zero must be the zero of what it measures. A z-score (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) fails for the same reason: its zero is the average candidate.

Each measure has a reference (the swing the board agreed on, in the measure's own unit) and a weight.


Write improvement(measure, before, after), how much better a measure gets, so that more is always better, and project_score(project, reference, weights), the weighted sum of each improvement as a share of its reference. plan_score (given) adds the scores of the projects a plan funds; the tests call both.


The board will compare plans of different sizes by their total score, so be careful what a score of 0 means. A replacement that makes one measure worse (a new machine that uses more energy) should lose score for it, and the loss is not capped. About four lines.

The Reference Is Fixed Before the Candidates Are Scored


If the reference is not the best candidate, what is it? The tempting answer is "the best candidate"; it is easy and needs no meeting. But then each project's score depends on every other candidate. Here GridCap small's 39 projects other than small's P006 are ranked four times: with references taken from the best of all 40 candidates, from the best of the 39 after P006 is withdrawn (the vegetation team moves it into next year's maintenance budget), and twice with GridCap's fixed references:






None of the 39 projects changed, yet withdrawing P006 moved P027 from fifth to first and pushed P028 out of the top five. P006 held the top of three measures, so its withdrawal shrank those references, and every remaining project's reliability, safety and environmental scores grew while its financial score stayed put. The weights were not touched, but the measures' relative pull was. A board that sees its ranking of P027 and P028 flip because of a project nobody discussed will not trust the model again.


With fixed references both rankings are the same, because a project's score depends only on its own benefits. So the references are set once per planning cycle, written into the portfolio snapshot, and changed only on purpose. Two ways to set them:


  • A fixed scale from the business: the smallest change worth anything (normally 0, no benefit) to the largest a single project in this program could plausibly deliver. The UK government's multi-criteria manual suggests fixed scales whenever options are judged one after another rather than all at once, which is exactly the situation of a portfolio that gains and loses candidates between planning cycles.
  • A frozen snapshot: the best candidate of the portfolio at Budget Lock (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock), stored and never recomputed for later what-if runs.


A score above 1 is allowed: a project that delivers more than the reference simply scores more than the full swing (small's P006 scores 1.08 on reliability against 3,000,000 customer-minutes). Capping it at 1 would throw away real benefit: P006's last 8% of reliability would count for nothing.

monetize$m \times p$ ($ per unit)yes, if $p$ is restated with the unityesno


Monetizing, pricing each unit in dollars (say $2.78 per customer-minute), is the ratio method in disguise: a fixed price $p_k$ is the same as $w_k / R_k$ in lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in. Some regulators publish such prices, and when a utility has them it can use them directly. A rank throws away magnitude: the first and second project differ by one place whether they differ by a hair or by a factor of ten.


Summary


  • Adding raw measures lets the units choose the winner. On GridCap tiny the raw sum follows reliability in customer-minutes and finance in SAIDI minutes.
  • A normalized score divides by a reference, so the units cancel. $n = m/R$ with $R$ in the same unit; restating both by $c$ changes nothing, and the tiny ranking is the same in two unit systems.
  • Zero must mean no benefit when scores are added across projects. Min-max deducts the worst candidate once per project and prefers P002 to P005 + P008; the ratio scale prefers the pair.
  • The reference is fixed before the candidates are scored. References taken from the candidates let a withdrawn project reorder the others; fixed ones do not.

Challenges


  1. Restate safety in "incidents per 1,000 failures" and forget to restate its reference. Which way does safety's pull on the value move, and by what factor?
  2. A board member wants SAIDI after the plan as the reliability measure (smaller is better). Write a normalized score for it whose zero is "no improvement".
  3. The best candidate is frozen as the reference at Budget Lock. In a later what-if run a new project beats it on reliability. What is its reliability score, and is anything wrong?


Design Note: Test the Units, Not Only the Numbers


A value function is easy to break in a way no reviewer sees: someone loads emissions in kilograms from a new source and the references still say tonnes. Every emissions score is now a thousand times too large, each one still looks like a number, and the plan quietly starts caring about little but emissions. The defence is a test that restates each measure in another unit, together with its reference, and checks that every value and the ranking are unchanged (lesson Building the CIPT value function writes one), plus a check on load that each measure and its reference name the same unit. The units cancel only if they are the same units.
