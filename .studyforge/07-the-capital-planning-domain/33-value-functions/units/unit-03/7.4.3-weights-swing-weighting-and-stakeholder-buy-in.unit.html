  14. Weights and swing weighting

With the measures normalized (lesson Normalizing different units), the GridCap board must say how much each one counts. The safety lead opens: "Safety first. Forty percent safety, thirty reliability, twenty finance, ten environment." Heads nod. It sounds principled, and it is the most common way weights are set. It is also meaningless until someone says "forty percent of what?". The same 40% can mean that an injury avoided is worth $10M or $100M, depending on a number the board never looked at.

This lesson shows what a weight really is (an exchange rate between units), how swing weighting gets weights a board can defend, why a weight must change when its reference does, and how to show the result so that the people who set the weights trust what the model does with them. The weighted-sum optimization that uses these weights, and what it can and cannot find, is the subject of lessons Weighted sums and what the weights really mean and Supported and unsupported Pareto points (Weighted sums); this lesson only builds the weights.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course. Its references, ratings and the prices they imply are invented. The project IDs below are GridCap small's (lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial used tiny, whose P006 is a different project).

A Weight Is an Exchange Rate Between Units

The value of project $i$ is the weighted sum of its normalized scores:

with weights $w_k \ge 0$ that sum to 1. The last form is the one to read: one more unit of measure $k$ adds $w_k / R_k$ to the value. The weight never acts alone, only together with its reference. Dividing one measure's value per unit by another's gives the exchange rate between them, how many units of measure $j$ the value function treats as equal to one unit of measure $k$:

With $j$ the financial measure, this is "what one unit of $k$ is worth in $k of NPV". Here are the rates the safety lead's weights imply against GridCap's references (3,000,000 customer-minutes, 1 incident, 200 tCO2e, $5,000k), and again if the safety reference had been 0.1 incident:

Check the first safety rate: $(0.4 / 1) / (0.2 / 5{,}000) = 10{,}000$, so $10,000k, $10M, per incident avoided. Shrink the safety reference tenfold and the same 40% prices an incident at $100M. Nobody in the room changed their view about safety; the weight's meaning changed because the amount it refers to changed. So "safety is 40%" is not a statement anyone can agree or disagree with. "We would give up $10M of NPV to avoid one expected safety incident" is.

That is the whole idea of this lesson: a weight is a price. Every weighted sum, whether its author meant it or not, sets an exchange rate between every pair of measures, and the plan the model picks is the plan that is best at those prices.

Swing Weighting Compares the Swings, Not the Measures

The safety lead's question, "which measure is most important?", has no answer, because importance depends on how much of each measure is at stake. The UK government's multi-criteria analysis manual puts it with a car purchase: if every car on your shortlist costs within £200 of the others, price deserves little weight, however much you care about money in general. A weight has to reflect both how much the options differ on a measure and how much that difference matters. That is why a measure everyone calls "very important", such as safety, can end up with a lower weight than a duller one, if the options hardly differ on it.

Swing weighting builds the weights from that idea. Each measure's swing is the move from no benefit (0) to its reference $R_k$. The board compares swings, not measures:

  1. Describe every swing concretely. "From no customer-minutes avoided to 3,000,000 avoided, about five minutes of SAIDI for every GridCap customer." "From no incident avoided to one."
  2. Find the biggest swing. Ask: if you could move only one measure from 0 to its reference, which would you move? Compare two at a time and keep the winner when there are many. The winner gets a rating of 100. The sizes of the references decide nothing here: 3,000,000 customer-minutes is a bigger number than 1 incident only because the units differ.
  3. Rate every other swing against it. "Moving safety from 0 to 1 incident avoided is worth 80% of moving reliability from 0 to 3,000,000 customer-minutes." The manual's group version has each person write a rating down privately, shows the spread, asks those at the extremes to explain, and then lets the decision maker or a small senior group settle the number.
  4. Divide by the total. $w_k = r_k / \sum_j r_j$, so the weights sum to 1 and keep the ratios of the ratings.

GridCap's board rated the reliability swing biggest, safety 80, finance 60 and environment 20:

The ratings total 260, so reliability's weight is 100/260 = 0.3846. The prices follow from the ratings and the swings: the $5,000k financial swing is rated 60 and the 1-incident safety swing 80, so one incident is worth $80/60 \times 5{,}000 = 6{,}667$ $k. These are GridCap's weights from here on.

Two conditions make a weighted sum a fair summary at all, and the manual names both. No double counting (lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial). And preference independence: you must be able to say how much you prefer one level of a measure to another without knowing the other measures. "More customer-minutes avoided is better, whatever the NPV" is independent. "Emissions only matter for projects in the wildfire region" is not; it needs the two folded into one measure, or a hurdle that screens projects first.

Problem statement

A water utility's board weighs three measures for its main renewals: breaks avoided, leakage avoided (megalitres) and operating savings ($k). Each has a reference swing, from no benefit to the amount in REFERENCE. The board follows the group procedure of swing weighting: every member writes down a private rating of each measure's swing against the biggest one, and the board then settles on the middle rating of its members for each measure (the median, which for an even number of members is halfway between the two middle ratings), so that one member at an extreme cannot drag the result.

Write panel_weights(ratings) in panel.py, where ratings[measure] lists the members' ratings, returning the weights. Then write price(weights, reference, measure, base): how many units of base the weights say one unit of measure is worth, so the board can read its weights as prices ("one break avoided is worth $125k").

Work the first test's weights by hand before coding. About five lines.

A Weight Belongs to Its Reference: Change One, Re-rate the Other


Because the price of a unit is $w_k / R_k$, the weight and the reference only mean something as a pair. Suppose the board decides that the reliability swing should cover 6,000,000 customer-minutes, because next cycle's storm-hardening projects are bigger. Three ways to handle it, on GridCap small:




  • Old ratings on the wider swing halve the price of a customer-minute, to $1.39. Nobody decided that customers' minutes are worth half as much; the board only redrew the scale. The ranking changes (P022 drops out of the top five, P036 comes in), and each project's value moves by its own factor, from 0.61 to 0.99 of the old, depending on how much of it is reliability.
  • Re-rated properly: a swing twice as large is worth twice the rating, 200 instead of 100. The price of a customer-minute is back at $2.78, and every project's value is the old one times the same number, $260/360 = 0.7222$ (the ratings now total 360 instead of 260). Multiplying every value by one positive number never reorders anything, so the ranking, and any plan chosen on these values, is the same.


The same holds the other way: a weight copied from another utility, or from last year's model, means nothing unless its reference came with it. When a reference changes, ask the board whether the new swing's rating should scale with it. If their answer is "yes, proportionally", the exchange rates, and every decision, stay the same.

Problem statement


A city bus operator values its projects by rider-hours saved, accidents avoided and savings ($k). Its board set swing ratings once (the biggest swing rated 100, the others as a share of it) against references old_reference. Next year the planners redraw the swings: new_reference gives each measure's new swing, in the same units. The board did not change its mind about what an accident or a rider-hour is worth, only about how big the swings are.


Write rerate(ratings, old_reference, new_reference) in swings.py, returning ratings for the new swings that keep the price of every measure in every other (price, given, computes one), and that follow the convention again: the biggest swing is rated 100 and the others as a share of it.


Before coding, write down what a price is in terms of a rating, its reference and the ratings' total, and ask which part may change. About three lines.

Buy-In Comes From Prices People Can Read and Rankings That Survive Disagreement


Weights decide which projects get funded, so the people affected will challenge them. Three things make the weights hold up.


Show the prices, not the percentages. "Reliability 0.3846" invites a debate about decimals. "Avoiding one customer-minute is worth $2.78 of NPV; avoiding one safety incident is worth $6.7M; a tonne of CO2e, $8.3k" invites the right debate: would we really pay that? Prices can also be checked against numbers the utility already uses elsewhere, such as a value of lost load from a rate case or an internal carbon price, if it has them. If the board flinches at a price, the rating behind it is wrong; change the rating and show the prices again.


Show what the weights do to the projects. Read out the projects that rise and fall against an NPV-only ranking, with the reason in measures. GridCap's top project under these weights, small's P006 (lesson Building the CIPT value function), has an NPV of $60k; it is on top because it avoids 3.2 million customer-minutes. That is exactly the kind of result that is either the point of the value function or a sign of a bad number, and the board has to see it to decide which.


Test how far the weights can move before the answer changes. Board members will disagree about ratings. Often the disagreement does not matter: the same projects come out on top across the range of views. Here the safety rating swings from 0 to 200 while the others stay put, and the program prints a line only when the top five change (and at 200):




From "safety counts for nothing" to "safety counts for more than half", P006, P030 and P028 stay the top three. Only fourth and fifth place move. That is a sentence a board can act on: "you disagree about safety by a factor of anything you like, and it does not change the three projects at the top". The multi-criteria manual makes the same point: sensitivity analysis often shows that the same two or three options win under every group's weights, so the argument can move on to the projects where the weights do matter.


This is a ranking of projects one at a time. Whether a plan, which must also fit a budget, changes with the weights is a question for the solver, and lessons Weighted sums and what the weights really mean and Supported and unsupported Pareto points (Weighted sums) ask it.


Summary


  • A weight is an exchange rate between units. One unit of measure $k$ is worth $w_k / R_k$; the safety lead's 40% priced an incident at $10M or $100M depending on the reference.
  • Swing weighting compares the swings, not the measures. Rate each swing from 0 to its reference against the biggest (100) and divide by the total; GridCap's ratings 100, 80, 60, 20 give an incident a price of $6,667k and a customer-minute $2.78.
  • A weight belongs to its reference: change one, re-rate the other. Doubling the reliability swing and its rating multiplies every value by 0.7222 and keeps the ranking; doubling only the swing halves a customer-minute's price and reorders the projects.
  • Buy-in comes from prices people can read and rankings that survive disagreement. Show prices, show winners and losers, and sweep the contested weights: on GridCap small the top three hold for any safety rating from 0 to 200.


Reference


SymbolMeaning
$r_k$swing rating of measure $k$: the biggest swing is 100, the others a share of it
$w_k = r_k / \sum_j r_j$swing weight; $w_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k w_k = 1$
$w_k / R_k$value of one unit of measure $k$
$\text{rate}_{k \to j} = (w_k/R_k)/(w_j/R_j)$units of $j$ worth one unit of $k$
GridCapratings 100 / 80 / 60 / 20 (reliability, safety, financial, environmental); $2.78 per customer-minute, $6,667k per incident, $8.33k per tCO2e


Further reading: UK Department for Communities and Local Government, Multi-criteria analysis: a manual (2009): section 6.2.10 on swing weighting, the car-price example and the group procedure; section 5.4.4.4 and 6.2.12 on mutual preference independence; section 6.2.14 on sensitivity analysis between interest groups.


Challenges


  1. The environmental lead wants a tonne of CO2e to be worth $50k. Keeping the other ratings, what environmental rating gives that price?
  2. Two board members rate safety 60 and 120. Using the sensitivity output, does their disagreement change the top five? Which places?
  3. Why can a swing weight be 0 for a measure everyone agrees is important? Describe a portfolio where that would be right.


Design Note: Store Ratings and References, Derive Weights


A model that stores only the weights loses the information needed to keep them meaningful: when a reference changes, nobody can tell whether the weight was meant for the old swing or the new. Store the board's ratings and the references, with the date and the meeting that set them, and compute the weights and the prices from them every run. Print the prices in the run's report. A price that changes between two runs is then a visible event, never a side effect.
