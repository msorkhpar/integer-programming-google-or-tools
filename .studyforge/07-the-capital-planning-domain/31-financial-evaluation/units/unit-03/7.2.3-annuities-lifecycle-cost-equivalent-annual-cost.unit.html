Asset A006, a Valley substation transformer, has two projects in GridCap tiny: P006 replaces it for $1,970k and P007 repairs it for $290k, and lesson Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace) makes them mutually exclusive. The repair is far cheaper today. But a repaired transformer lasts ten more years and keeps costing money to run and patch; a new one lasts forty. Comparing $290k with $1,970k compares two different things. This lesson builds the tools that make them comparable: the present value of a level stream of payments (an annuity), the lifecycle cost of owning an asset, and the equivalent annual cost that puts options with different lives on the same yearly footing. Four rules, with numpy-financial checking each number.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio. It stores no running costs or asset lives, so the O&M costs, lives and overhaul in this lesson are invented for it; the capex of the two options are P006's and P007's.

An Annuity's Present Value Has a Closed Form

An annuity is the same payment $A$ at the end of each of $n$ years: a maintenance contract, a lease, a yearly saving. Its present value is a sum of $n$ discounted payments,

and that sum has a closed form. Write $v = 1/(1 + r)$. The sum is $A(v + v^2 + \dots + v^n)$, a geometric series. Multiply it by $v$ and subtract: everything cancels except the first and the last term, $S - vS = A(v - v^{n+1})$, so $S = A v (1 - v^n)/(1 - v)$. Since $v/(1 - v) = 1/r$,

The annuity factor $a(r, n)$ is the present value of $1 a year for $n$ years. At a zero rate it is just $n$ (no discounting), which the code handles separately because the formula would divide by zero:

For $100k a year for 20 years at 7%, the loop, the closed form and numpy-financial agree:

Twenty payments of $100k, $2,000k in total, are worth $1,059k today. Three variations matter:

  • Payments at the start of each year (an annuity due) are each one year less discounted, so the PV is the ordinary annuity's times $(1 + r)$. numpy-financial's when='begin' does this.
  • A payment forever (a perpetuity) has $(1 + r)^{-n} \to 0$, so its PV is $A/r$: $1,429k. Twenty years already capture 74% of forever at 7%, which is why distant years matter so little.
  • The payment from the PV inverts the factor: $A = \text{PV} / a(r, n)$. npf.pmt returns it, and the next two rules lean on it.

numpy-financial's functions follow a cash-sign convention: money paid and money received have opposite signs. npf.pv(0.07, 20, -100) reads "paying 100 a year is worth receiving 1,059.40 now", and npf.pmt(0.07, 20, 1000) returns −94.39 because receiving 1,000 now is repaid by paying 94.39 a year. When you compare with your own functions, which use positive costs, flip the sign.

Lifecycle Cost Is Every Cost of Owning the Asset, Discounted to One Date

The price of an asset is only its first cost. Over its life it also has running costs (operations and maintenance, O&M: inspections, oil tests, repairs after faults), occasional large one-off costs (an overhaul), and sometimes a value at the end (salvage). The lifecycle cost (LCC) is all of them, discounted to the date of the decision:

with $L$ the life in years and $j$ the year of life of each one-off. Two options for A006, with invented running costs, in 2027 dollars at the real rate of 7%:

The new transformer costs $25k a year to run and needs a $300k mid-life overhaul in year 20. The repaired one costs $150k a year, because an old unit needs constant attention, and lasts ten years.

Read the lifecycle column first. Running costs change the picture a lot: the repair's $290k becomes $1,343k once ten years of O&M are counted, nearly five times its price. The replacement's 40 years of O&M add only $333k, because most of those years are far away, and its overhaul, $300k in year 20, is worth $77.5k today. On lifecycle cost the repair still looks better: $1,343k against $2,381k.

That comparison is wrong, and it is the most common mistake with lifecycle costs. The repair's $1,343k buys ten years of service; the replacement's $2,381k buys forty. The two numbers are not costs of the same thing.

Equivalent Annual Cost Compares Options With Different Lives


Turn each lifecycle cost into a level yearly payment over its own life, the payment whose annuity has the same present value. That is the equivalent annual cost (EAC):



It answers "what does a year of service from this option cost?", which is comparable across lives.



In the table above, the replacement costs $178.6k per year of service and the repair $191.3k. The ranking has reversed: replacing is cheaper, by $12.7k a year, even though its lifecycle cost is $1,037k higher. npf.pmt gives the same EAC, which is a useful check, since EAC is exactly the payment of a loan of LCC over the asset's life.


Do not shortcut EAC to $\text{LCC}/L$. Dividing by the life ignores the time value of the money spent up front: for the replacement it gives $2,380.8k / 40 = $59.5k a year, a third of the real figure, because it spreads the $1,970k of capex over forty years as if that money cost nothing to tie up. The divisor is the annuity factor, not the number of years (they are equal only at a zero rate).

Equivalent Annual Cost Assumes the Option Is Repeated Like for Like


Why is EAC the right comparison? Picture forty years of service from A006. One replacement provides them. Four repairs in a row provide them too, if each repair costs what the first did and lasts as long. Value both plans over the same forty years:






On a common horizon the replacement is cheaper, the same verdict as EAC, and this is no coincidence: when an option repeats like for like, its EAC is the level yearly cost of the whole chain, so comparing EACs is comparing chains over any common horizon. EAC is the shortcut that saves you from building the chains.


The shortcut carries the chain's assumption, and that is what a planner should hear with the number: each repair is followed by another like it, at the same real cost. In reality, an old transformer cannot be repaired four times in a row, and the second repair would be on an even older unit. When the assumption fails, build the actual sequence of decisions over a common horizon and compare present values directly. That is what a multi-period model does: lesson Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace) makes repair and replace alternatives inside one plan, and lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal values partial renewals with a lifecycle value curve.


Summary


  • An annuity's present value has a closed form. $\text{PV} = A \cdot a(r, n)$ with $a(r, n) = (1 - (1 + r)^{-n})/r$; at zero rate, $a = n$; forever, $A/r$.
  • Lifecycle cost is every cost of owning the asset, discounted to one date. Capex, O&M, one-offs, less discounted salvage. LCCs of options with different lives are not comparable.
  • Equivalent annual cost compares options with different lives. $\text{EAC} = \text{LCC}/a(r, L)$, never $\text{LCC}/L$; on A006 it reverses the LCC ranking.
  • Equivalent annual cost assumes the option is repeated like for like. When it is not, compare present values over a common horizon.

Design Note: EAC Outside the Model, Present Values Inside It


EAC is a screening tool: it ranks options that would each be repeated forever, one asset at a time. A capital-planning MILP does not need it. The model chooses among projects whose cash flows it already sees year by year over a fixed horizon, so it compares present values, and the "repeat like for like" assumption never enters. Use EAC to explain a repair-or-replace choice to a planner, and to sanity-check an alternative group's coefficients; put present values in the objective.
