A Start-Year Coefficient Is the Project's Cash Flow, Shifted to Start in That Year

A project's cash flow is written once, relative to its own start: it spends $c_{i,k}$ in its $k$-th year of work ($k = 0$ is the start year) and receives benefits $b_{i,k}(t)$ in year $k$ after starting, which may depend on the start $t$. Starting in year $t$ moves every amount to calendar year $t + k$. The coefficient is the NPV of that moved cash flow, on the model's one valuation date $y_0$:

The shift is the same one lesson Cash-flow spreading across years used for the budget rows: the amount in calendar year $y$ is the profile entry $c_{i,\,y - t}$. In code, one function moves the cash flow and one sum values it, for every allowed start:

GridCap's benefit model is simple, and it is worth stating exactly, because the table depends on it. Each project $i$ delivers one benefit, counted in its start year: $B_i = \rho_i \cdot \text{capex}_i$, where the benefit ratio $\rho_i$ (between 1.15 and 2.05) says how much the work is worth per dollar of cost. Every year the project waits past its earliest start $e_i$, the benefit shrinks by the decay $\delta_i$ (between 3% and 8%): the risk the work would remove is carried a year longer. So $b_{i,0}(t) = \rho_i\, \text{capex}_i\, (1 - \delta_i)^{t - e_i}$ and $b_{i,k}(t) = 0$ for $k > 0$. Costs are the 2027-dollar profile, unescalated, and $r = 7\%$, $y_0 = 2027$: by lesson Escalation; real vs. nominal dollars, real dollars at a real rate.

On P022, whose ratio and decay are 1.4953 and 0.0313:

Rounded to a whole $k, the computed NPVs are the stored ones. Read the columns to see why a later start is worth less. Two things happen to the benefit: it shrinks by the decay (3,813.0 to 3,693.6) and it is discounted one more year (to 3,452.0). The cost's present value falls too (2,316.9 to 2,165.3), because the same payments move a year further away. Delay saves on the cost side and loses on the benefit side; here the benefit's loss is bigger, so the NPV falls by about $209k for the first year of delay.

Discount Every Start to One Valuation Date

The most common mistake in a start-year table is to value each start from its own start year: "the NPV of the project, if we start it in $t$", computed as if $t$ were today. Each entry then looks reasonable, and the table is wrong as a set:

Valued from its own start, P022's cost is $2,316.9k in every year, so waiting costs nothing on that side, and the only loss left is the decay. A 2029 start then seems to give up $235k against a 2027 start; on one common date it gives up $395k. A project with no decay would have the same entry in every year, and a model would defer it to the last allowed year for free, pushing work out of every year's budget into the future at no cost. The fix is the design note of lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates: one valuation date for the whole model, the same for every project and every start. In the formula above, that is $y_0$ in the exponent, not $t$.

Count Benefits to the End of the Asset's Life, Not the End of the Plan


GridCap books each benefit as one amount in the start year. Many real benefits are streams instead: fewer breaks, outage minutes and repairs, year after year, for as long as the new asset lasts, which is usually far longer than the plan. Take an invented mains relining: $600k in each of two years of work, then $120k a year of avoided breaks for 25 years. The plan runs 2027–2031. If the benefits are cut at the end of the plan, the table is ruined:






Counted over the asset's life, the relining is worth about $146k and loses a little with each year of delay (each entry is the one above divided by 1.07, because the whole cash flow moves a year). Cut at 2031, it is worth nothing at any start, and later starts are punished most because they keep the fewest benefit years: a 2030 start keeps none. A model with that table would never fund long-lived work late in the plan, and it would favour quick-payback work everywhere. This is the end effect of a finite horizon. Klevorick's 1966 paper on capital budgeting by mathematical programming raises it explicitly: projects started inside the horizon keep paying after it ends, and the paper adds, to the last period's return, the project's value at that point. Counting the benefit stream over the asset's full life, as here, does the same job.


The plan's horizon limits when the decisions are made (which start variables exist). It does not limit which consequences count. A coefficient should include every cash flow the decision causes, whenever it falls.

GridCap's Table Is This Calculation, Entry for Entry


GridCap's generator draws each project's benefit ratio and decay, computes the NPV of every allowed start as above, rounds it to a whole $k and stores only the result (Project.npv). The ratio and the decay are not in the data. In practice they are what finance hands you: a benefit forecast per project, and a view of what delay costs. To check the table, the library gridcap_assumptions.py replays the generator with the same seed and records the two numbers as they are drawn; it leaves the portfolio unchanged (a module test checks its content hash). Then every entry is rebuilt from the cash flow:



The rebuilt table is compared with the stored one for three sizes. The cost side is also checked independently with numpy-financial: each start's costs are laid on a calendar-year list from 2027 (zeros before the start) and discounted with npf.npv, whose first value is not discounted, the same convention as present_value (lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates).



All 980 entries match, and the cost present values agree with numpy-financial to within float rounding. So the coefficients of lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows–Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps are exactly NPVs of shifted cash flows on one date, in real 2027 dollars at 7%, with a benefit that decays with delay. Rounding happens once, at the end; rounding the benefit and the cost separately could move an entry by $1k.


Two simplifications of GridCap's model are worth knowing, because a real table would not make them. Its benefit is one lump in the start year, before the work is finished; the previous rule's stream, starting after the work, is the more faithful shape. And its costs are not escalated, which is correct only if they rise with general inflation (lesson Escalation; real vs. nominal dollars).

That closes the loop: the model of section 6 and the finance of this module agree.


It is also the NPV of lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock's base scenario, although that scenario adds the region floors this model leaves out. The program checks the 7% plan against them:



Every region gets at least $3,710k more than its floor. Dropping rows can only let a plan do better; this plan already keeps the dropped rows, so it is optimal with them too, and the floored model has the same $24,260k. The floors do not bind on GridCap small. On data where one did, the row $\sum_{i \in P_r} \text{capex}_i \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t} \ge F_r$ of lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock goes back in.


At 3% every coefficient changes, and the plan does not move at all. At 12%, 14 starts change and two fewer projects are funded. A plan can be insensitive to the rate over a wide range and then change in many places at once, because the solver compares whole combinations of projects, not one project at a time. The program prints which projects moved; explaining why each one moved is the job of the tools in section 9. A sensitivity run like this one, re-solving at the rates finance might plausibly argue for, is cheap and tells planners whether the rate is a decision they need to fight over.


One warning about the NPV column: $25,300k at 3% and $23,161k at 12% are not "more" and "less" value for the same plan. They are measured with different yardsticks. Compare plans at one rate; across rates, compare what the plans do.


Summary


  • A start-year coefficient is the project's cash flow, shifted to start in that year. Move every amount from year $k$ of the project to calendar year $t + k$, then take the NPV.
  • Discount every start to one valuation date. Valuing each start from its own year makes delay look free (P022: $235k of loss shown instead of $395k).
  • Count benefits to the end of the asset's life, not the end of the plan. Cutting at the horizon turned a $146k project into a loss at every start.
  • GridCap's table is this calculation, entry for entry. 980 of 980 entries rebuilt; the cost side matches npf.npv.
  • The rate reaches the plan only through the coefficients. GridCap small's plan is the same at 3% and 7% and changes 14 starts at 12%.

Challenges


  1. Escalate GridCap's costs at a real 1% a year from 2027 and rebuild the small table. How many entries change, and in which direction?
  2. Replace GridCap's lump benefit with a stream: $\rho_i\,\text{capex}_i / 20$ a year for twenty years after the work ends. Does the 7% plan change?
  3. Re-solve at 8%, 9%, 10% and 11%. Find the lowest rate at which the plan differs from the 7% one.


Design Note: Precompute the Table, Do Not Model the Finance


The NPV of a start is a nonlinear function of the rate, and its benefit can depend on the start in any way finance likes. None of that belongs in the solver. Precompute $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ for every allowed start, check it against an independent library, store it with the data, and give the model a linear objective. The model stays a MILP, the finance stays testable on its own, and a change of rate or benefit forecast is a change of data, not of model code. It also means the table is a place where errors hide, so each check in the reference table above belongs in the project's tests.
