GridCap's cost profiles are price quotes. P022, a Valley tower set repair, is listed at $360k, $720k and $1,470k for its three years of work, priced when the estimate was made, in 2027. If the work starts in 2029, the crews and steel will be bought at 2029–2031 prices, and prices rise. Finance calls that rise escalation. Once costs escalate, the discount rate of lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates has to be the right kind of rate too, or the escalation is counted twice or not at all. This lesson gives the rule for escalating a quote, the rule that pairs dollars with rates, the one case where escalation changes a real model, and a check for borrowed formulas, which two common ones fail.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. The escalation and inflation rates in this lesson are assumptions made for it.

Escalation Turns a Price Quoted in One Year Into the Price Paid in Another

A cost estimate is quoted in the prices of one year, its price base year $b$. If prices for that kind of work rise by $e$ a year, an amount $c$ quoted in year $b$ costs, when paid in year $y$,

The exponent counts the years from the price base year to the year of payment. It is the compounding of lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates, applied to prices instead of money in an account. Amounts at the prices of the year they are paid in are nominal (or "then-year") dollars; amounts at the prices of one fixed base year are real (or "constant") dollars. A cost profile straight from an estimate is real: every entry is in base-year prices.

At 3% a year, the same work costs $2,661k if it starts in 2027 and $2,823k if it starts in 2029. Every year of delay adds escalation to every year of work, because each payment moves one year further from 2027. The first year of a 2027 start is paid in the price base year, so it is not escalated at all ($360.0k).

The exponent is the part people get wrong. A tempting shortcut escalates the $k$-th year of work by $(1 + e)^k$, counting from the project's own start, as if every project's estimate were priced in the year it begins. It gives the same answer for a project that starts in the price base year, and the wrong answer for every other start:

Counted from the start, P022 costs $2,661k whenever it starts: two years of delay come free of price rises. Both present-value columns discount to the start of 2027 at 7% in the same way, so the whole gap ($128k for a 2029 start) is escalation the shortcut forgot. In a start-year model that gap makes later starts look cheaper than they are, so the solver defers work for a saving that does not exist.

This course measures escalation from the price base year, never from the project's start. For GridCap that base year is 2027, the year the estimates were priced and also the plan's valuation date. To spot the shortcut in someone else's formula, find the exponent on $(1 + e)$ and ask whether it changes when the start year changes. If it depends only on the year of work ($k$ or $\tau$ counted from the start), it is the shortcut.

Real Dollars Pair With a Real Rate, Nominal Dollars With a Nominal Rate


General inflation is the rise in prices across the whole economy, at rate $i$ a year. A discount rate can be quoted two ways. A nominal rate $n$ is what money actually earns or costs, in then-year dollars: a loan at 6.6% is a nominal rate. A real rate $r$ is what money earns in purchasing power, after inflation. The two are tied by the Fisher relation:



The familiar shortcut $n \approx r + i$ drops the small product $r \cdot i$. With a real rate of 4% and inflation of 2.5%:



Now value P022, started in 2028, at the start of 2027. Assume its costs escalate at exactly general inflation, so its real costs are the 2027 quotes and its nominal costs are those quotes escalated at 2.5%. There are four ways to pair flows with a rate, and an approximate fifth:






The first two lines are equal, to the cent, and they must be: each nominal amount is the real amount times $(1 + i)^{y - b}$, and the nominal rate's discount factor is the real one divided by $(1 + i)^{y - b}$, so inflation cancels year by year. That is the rule. Either escalate everything and discount at a nominal rate, or keep everything in base-year dollars and discount at a real rate. The answer is the same.


The next two lines are the two mixes, and they err in opposite directions:


  • Nominal flows at a real rate count inflation in the flows and never take it out: the cost looks $143k bigger. Later costs are hurt most, so long projects and late starts look too expensive.
  • Real flows at a nominal rate take inflation out of the rate without ever putting it into the flows: the cost looks $134k smaller. Here later costs look too cheap, and a model defers work to "save" money that was never saved.


The last line shows that the $r + i$ shortcut is close but not exact: $5k on one project. It is small here and grows with the rates and the horizon. When the numbers feed a table that is supposed to reconcile with finance's, use the exact Fisher relation.


GridCap applies no escalation anywhere: its cost profiles are 2027 quotes, used unchanged in every year, and its 7% rate is applied to them. So the course reads GridCap's amounts as real 2027 dollars and its 7% as a real rate. That is the consistent reading; GridCap's data does not say it, and lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year states it with the model.

Only Escalation Above Inflation Changes a Real Model


If a cost escalates at exactly general inflation, a real model can ignore it: in base-year dollars the cost does not change. Costs rarely do that. Skilled labour, copper and transformers have all at times risen faster than prices in general. What a real model needs is the real escalation, the part above inflation, given by the same Fisher arithmetic:




So in a real-dollar model, labour-heavy work should escalate at about 1.95% a year, not 4.5% (that would count inflation twice) and not 0% (that would ignore the part of the rise that is real). A cost that rises slower than inflation has a negative real escalation and gets cheaper in real terms. Mixed projects escalate each cost component at its own rate before the profile is summed.


Check a Borrowed Formula's Indices Before You Use It


Interview-prep material is full of NPV formulas, and a formula can be dimensionally fine and still wrong for a start-year model. Take one that writes the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$, with years numbered $1, \dots, T$, escalation $e$ and discount rate $r$, as



Two questions find its problems.


What is $\text{CF}_{i,\tau}$ indexed by? The calendar year $\tau$, and it has no $t$. Read literally, the cash flow does not move when the start moves; a later start only cuts off the early years of the sum. Written for P022 as if it started in year 1, a start in year 3 keeps only the $1,470k of the third year:






The literal reading says a year-3 start costs $1,273k in present value, $819k less than the shifted profile's $2,092k, because the first two years of cost have vanished from the sum. The cost must be shifted with the start: the amount paid in year $\tau$ by a project started in $t$ is its profile entry $c_{i,\tau - t}$, exactly the $c_{i,\,y - t}$ of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years's budget rows. The "shifted" column does that and keeps everything else.


Where do the exponents start? Escalation has exponent $\tau - 1$, discounting $\tau$. So prices are those of year 1 (no escalation in year 1), but money is valued one year earlier, at time 0, with year 1 discounted once. That is consistent only if both choices are intended. The test is to set $e = r$: if escalation and discounting start from the same date, they cancel and the PV equals the undiscounted cost. Here they do not:



The formula leaves one year of discounting over, so it values year-1 prices at time 0. That is a defensible convention, but it must be stated, and every other number in the model must use the same one. The course's convention avoids the offset: the price base year and the valuation date are both the start of 2027, and amounts are paid at the start of their year (lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates). The shortcut of the first rule, escalation counted from the project's start, fails the other test (does the escalation exponent move with the start?), so between them the two formulas show both ways to get an escalation exponent wrong.


Summary


  • Escalation turns a price quoted in one year into the price paid in another. Escalate by $(1 + e)^{y - b}$, counted from the price base year $b$; counting from the project's start makes later starts look cheaper (P022 at 2029: $128k of PV missing).
  • Real dollars pair with a real rate, nominal dollars with a nominal rate. Both give the same PV; the two mixes err in opposite directions; use the exact Fisher relation.
  • Only escalation above inflation changes a real model. $e_{\text{real}} = (1 + e)/(1 + i) - 1$.
  • Check a borrowed formula's indices before you use it. The cash flow must shift with the start, and $e = r$ should cancel exactly when the price base year and the valuation date coincide.

Design Note: Why the Course Models in Real Dollars


A real-dollar model has one fewer number to forecast (inflation) and keeps every year's budget comparable with every other. Its risk is the one this lesson names: a cost that escalates faster than inflation must be escalated at its real rate, or the model treats late work as too cheap. A nominal model matches what finance books and what a budget actually pays, so reports often need nominal numbers. Keep the model in one kind of dollar, and convert at the edge, in the report.
