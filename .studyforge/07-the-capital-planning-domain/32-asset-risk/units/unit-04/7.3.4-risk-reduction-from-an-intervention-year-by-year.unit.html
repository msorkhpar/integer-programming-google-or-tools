  16. Risk reduction by start year

GridCap's planners want a second plan next to the NPV plan of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years: the plan that removes the most failure risk within the same budgets and rules. The start-year model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows can find it, if each variable $x_{i,s}$ (project $i$ starts in year $s$) gets the right objective coefficient: how much risk the project removes if it starts in $s$. Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts gave each project one number, the risk avoided next year. That cannot tell 2028 from 2030. This lesson builds the coefficient year by year from lessons Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) to Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF, then solves the plan, in five rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its assets, projects and budgets describe no real company. Every ID in this lesson is the small portfolio's (tiny and medium reuse the same IDs for other assets).

Work Resets the Asset's Age in the Year After It Ends

Every quantity in this lesson follows from one table: the asset's age in each year, with and without the project. Let $y_0$ be the plan's first year (2027), $a$ the asset's age in $y_0$, $d$ the project's years of work and $\rho$ the share of its age the asset keeps after the work, lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts's convention: 0 for a replacement, a rebuild or undergrounding, 0.5 for a repair, a trim, a clearing or a widening. Lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial writes the same reset as a depth $d = 1 - \rho$, gives clearing and widening a deeper one, and starts the benefit at the start year; its section Two Renewal Conventions, One Failure Model compares the two and says which the course uses where.

  • Without the project the asset ages one year per year: $a^0_y = a + (y - y_0)$.
  • With the project started in $s$, the asset keeps aging during the work, the years $s, \dots, s + d - 1$. From year $s + d$, the first full year after the work, it is $\rho$ times the age it had reached, and it ages one year per year from there:

Small's P024 undergrounds the feeder A024, two years of work, and may start in 2028, 2029 or 2030. Its asset's ages over ten years, for each start:

The convention that benefit starts in the year after the work ends is a choice, and a cautious one: a crew may finish in March. Whatever your utility's rule, write it into this one function, so every coefficient follows it.

Problem statement

A water utility plans to swap an old pump for a unit of a newer design. The old pump has age age in the plan's first year and a Weibull curve (beta, eta); the new design has its own curve (beta_new, eta_new). The work starts in start and lasts duration years; the old pump runs until the work ends, and the new unit is in service, at age 0, from the year after. Failed pumps are repaired and kept, so a year's risk is cof times the expected failures in that year.

Write yearly_cut(pump, years, start, duration) in refurb.py: a dict from each year in years (the first is the plan's first year) to the risk removed that year, $k.

In the lesson's notation, write $\delta_y$ as the risk of the old pump at its age $a^0_y$ minus the risk with the work, which now comes from a different curve. failures(age, beta, eta) is written for you. The tests compute expected failures from scipy.stats.weibull_min.logsf. About ten lines.

After the Work, the Asset Ages Again


A common shortcut, found in interview notes, writes the reduction as the PoF without the work minus a fixed "post-renewal" PoF, $(\text{PoF}_{i,y} - \text{PoF}^0) \times C_i$, as if the renewed asset stayed as good as on the day the crew left. It does not; it starts a new life on the same curve (or on a new design's curve). The program compares the aging version with the frozen one for a replacement and for a repair:




For a replacement the error is small over ten years, because a new asset's hazard is tiny for its first years. For a repair it is four times larger: the repaired transformer restarts at 21 years old, where its hazard is already climbing, and freezing it there ignores that climb. The longer the counting window, the larger the error. The cost of doing it right is nothing: the yearly computation is one function either way.


Count the Reduction Over a Fixed Window and Discount It


Two decisions turn the yearly reductions into one coefficient.


Which years count. Counting only the plan's own years (2027 to 2031 for GridCap small) gives the "plan years" column above: $546k, $277k and 0 for P024's three starts. A start in 2030 finishes in 2031 and removes no risk inside the plan, so the model would never start it late, and would favor short projects over long ones for the same reason. This is the end-of-horizon effect. The fix is to count every start over the same evaluation window $W$, long enough to include each project's service after the plan ends. This course uses ten years from the plan's first year, 2027 to 2036, the "ten years" column.


How to weigh later years. A dollar of failure cost avoided in 2036 is worth less today than one avoided in 2028, for the reason lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates (Financial evaluation) gives for cash flows. Discount each year at the portfolio's rate $r$ (7% for GridCap) back to $y_0$:

Every coefficient falls with a later start: each year of delay is a year the old asset's risk is carried. P001 and P002 are the replacement and the repair of the same line A001: the one-year repair starting in 2027 removes more over the window than the two-year replacement starting in 2028, because it is in service two years sooner. Those two projects form an alternative group, and the model, not a ranking, decides between them.

Problem statement


A gas utility's planners fix the end-of-horizon effect differently from lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year. Instead of one window from the plan's first year, they credit every start with the same service period: the risk removed in the service_years years that follow the end of its work, whenever that is. Each year's reduction is discounted at rate back to the plan's first year first_year.


yearly_cut(main, years, start, duration, keep) is written for you: the lesson's yearly risk reduction, for any list of calendar years that begins at first_year. Write coefficients(main, starts, first_year, duration, keep, service_years, rate) in coefficients.py: a dict from each start year in starts to its coefficient, $k.


In the lesson's notation, write $\text{rr}_{s}$ as a sum: over which years $y$, of what $\delta_{s,y}$, divided by what. Problem statement


A regulator agrees a rate case with a utility on one condition: in every year of the plan, the expected failure cost left in the network, after the plan's projects, must not exceed that year's cap[y] ($k). The utility's model already chooses start years; it does not yet know about the condition.


What the model has, in the lesson's notation: the plan's years $Y$; the start variables $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$, given to you as x[(i, t)]; at most one start per project, already written; the network's risk with no project at all in year $y$, base[y] ($k); and, for each start variable, cut[(i, t)][y]: the risk project $i$ removes in year $y$ if it starts in $t$ (0 in the years before it takes effect).


Write the formulation first, in that notation: each new row with its quantifier and its label. Then write add_risk_caps(model, x, base, cut, cap) in cap.py to add it, and return your rows in a dict keyed by year.


The tests fix a plan and ask the solver whether it is allowed, so any correct formulation passes. About five lines.

Summary


  • Work resets the asset's age in the year after it ends. Before that the asset keeps aging; after it, it is $\rho$ times the age it had reached and ages again.
  • The risk removed in a year is the risk without the work minus the risk with it. Each is CoF times the expected failures of that year's age.
  • After the work, the asset ages again. Freezing the post-work risk overstated a repair's ten-year reduction by 8% on GridCap small.
  • Count the reduction over a fixed window and discount it. Counting only the plan's years gives late starts zero; a ten-year window from $y_0$, discounted at 7%, gives $\text{rr}_{i,s}$.
  • The coefficients enter the start-year model unchanged. On GridCap small the risk plan removes 52% more risk than the NPV plan and gives up $5,374k of NPV.

Design Note: Why the Coefficient Is Precomputed


The risk of an asset in a year is a nonlinear function of its age, and the age depends on the decision. Writing that inside the model would need nonlinear constraints or piecewise curves (lessons Convex piecewise costs without binaries to Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal). It is not needed here: each start year fixes the whole age path, so every consequence of choosing $x_{i,s} = 1$ can be computed before the solve, and the model stays a linear one in binary variables. The price is one coefficient per project and start year, which the model already has a variable for. The same trick works for anything that depends only on which start is chosen: NPV (lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year), risk, emissions. It stops working when the value of one project depends on another's choice, such as two projects on the same feeder: then the pair needs a variable or a row of its own, as the bundles and alternatives of lessons Parent-child bundles and all-or-nothing and Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace) do.
