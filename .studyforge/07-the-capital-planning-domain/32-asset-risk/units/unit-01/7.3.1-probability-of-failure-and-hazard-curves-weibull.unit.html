Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts used them through one formula, next year's probability of failure, and promised that section 7 would explain them. This is that explanation. By the end you can say what each of the two numbers means, draw the curve they describe, turn it into the probability that a working asset fails next year, check every number against scipy.stats, and explain where the two numbers came from, in five rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its assets and curves describe no real company.

A Failure Curve Says How the Lifetimes of Similar Assets Are Spread Out

Take one asset, the substation transformer A006 in the tiny portfolio. Imagine a large fleet of transformers built the same way and run the same way. Some fail young, most fail in middle age, a few run for a very long time. The lifetime $T$ of one of them, the age at which it fails, is a random quantity, and the failure curve describes how it is spread out. Three functions say the same thing in three ways, for an age $t$ in years:

  • the cumulative distribution $F(t) = P(T \le t)$, the probability that the asset has failed by age $t$. It starts at 0 and climbs to 1;
  • the reliability or survival function $R(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t)$, the probability that it is still working at age $t$;
  • the density $f(t) = F'(t)$, how densely the failures are packed around age $t$. The probability of failing between ages $t$ and $t + 1$ is the area under $f$ over that year, which is $F(t+1) - F(t)$.

The Hazard Is the Failure Rate Among the Assets Still Working

The density answers a question about a new asset: how likely is it to fail at age $t$? A planner looks at an asset that is still working today. For it the right question is: given that it has lasted to age $t$, how fast are assets like it failing now? That is the hazard, or failure rate:

Dividing by $R(t)$ keeps only the assets still working at age $t$. For the Weibull the exponentials cancel and the hazard is a power of age:

Its running total is the cumulative hazard $\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du = (t/\eta)^{\beta}$, and the reliability is $R(t) = e^{-\Lambda(t)}$. Those two facts are the whole of the Weibull algebra this module needs.

The shape decides whether risk rises with age. The exponent $\beta - 1$ sets the trend. Here is the hazard for four shapes with the same scale of 40 years:

  • $\beta < 1$: the hazard falls with age. Weak units fail early and leave the survivors stronger, the pattern called infant mortality. Replacing such an asset with a new one raises its risk.
  • $\beta = 1$: the hazard is constant, $1/\eta$ per year. Failures come from outside causes that do not care about age, such as storms or animals. The curve is the exponential distribution, and an old asset is exactly as risky as a new one.
  • $\beta > 1$: the hazard rises with age, the pattern of wear-out. At $\beta = 2$ it rises in a straight line; at $\beta = 3.5$, steeply. Every GridCap asset has a shape between 1.5 and 4, because the generator draws it there: GridCap models equipment that wears out.

So the hazard of a Weibull grows as a power of age, not exponentially, as some interview notes claim; a power of age grows far more slowly than an exponential over a long life.

A hazard is a rate, not a probability. Its unit is failures per year among the assets still working, so it can exceed 1. An invented, badly worn asset (shape 4, scale 20 years) shows it:

A hazard of 1.6 per year means that, at that age, units fail at a pace that would see 1.6 failures per unit-year if the pace held. It does not hold: the hazard changes within the year, and a unit fails only once. The probability that it fails in the next year is the next rule.

Problem statement

A rail operator keeps thousands of signal relays of a few types in service. Its maintenance rule says: start inspecting a relay type once the failure rate of the relays of that type still working reaches rate failures per relay per year. Each type's lifetimes follow a Weibull curve with shape beta and scale eta (years), fitted by the operator's reliability team.

Write trigger_age(rate, beta, eta) in relays.py. It returns the age, in years, at which the type's failure rate reaches rate. A type whose failure rate never rises with age gets None: the rule should never trigger on age for it.

In the lesson's notation you are solving $h(t) = \text{rate}$ for $t$, with $h(t) = (\beta/\eta)(t/\eta)^{\beta - 1}$. Solve it on paper first; then decide, from the exponent $\beta - 1$, which shapes have no answer that means "the rate has risen to the limit." The tests check your age against scipy.stats.weibull_min (pdf / sf at that age). Three to five lines.

Next Year's PoF Is Conditional on the Asset Working Today


The probability of failure next year, PoF for short in this course, is the probability that an asset of age $a$, working today, fails before age $a + 1$. "Working today" makes it a conditional probability:



and with $R = e^{-\Lambda}$ it has a third form, through the hazard accumulated over the year:




The program computes it for every tiny asset in three ways, with this module's function, with scipy's survival function and through the cumulative hazard, and puts two numbers that are not next year's PoF beside them: the unconditional $F(a+1) - F(a)$, and the cumulative distribution $F(a)$ itself:






The three conditional columns agree. The unconditional column is close for young assets, because nearly all of their fleet is still standing and $R(a)$ is near 1. It is badly wrong for old ones: for A006 it gives 1.6% where the conditional PoF is 9.5%, six times more. The unconditional number answers "what share of a new fleet fails in its 69th year?", and most of that fleet failed long before. A006 did not; it is standing in front of you, and its PoF is the conditional one. (The tiny portfolio has no asset A007: projects P006 and P007 are the replacement and the repair of the same transformer, A006.)


The cumulative distribution is not a yearly PoF. Some sources write the PoF in year $y$ as $1 - e^{-(\text{age}_y/\eta)^\beta}$, which is $F(\text{age}_y)$. That is the probability of having failed at some point before that age, counted from new; it is not the chance of failing during year $y$. The last column shows how far apart they are: for A006, $F(68) = 0.84$, while the chance that the working transformer fails in the coming year is 0.095, almost nine times smaller. Used as a yearly PoF, $F$ heads to 1 for every old asset, so it turns a fleet's old assets into near-certain failures every year. A yearly PoF for an asset in service is always the conditional $(F(a+1) - F(a))/(1 - F(a))$.


Beta Is the Shape, and Eta Is the Age by Which 63% Have Failed


The two numbers have meanings you can check and say out loud in a planning meeting.


The scale $\eta$ is the characteristic life. Put $t = \eta$ into $F$: the exponent is $-(1)^{\beta} = -1$ for every shape, so $F(\eta) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$. Whatever the shape, 63.2% of a fleet has failed by age $\eta$. It is not the average life and not the median.


The shape $\beta$ is how tightly lifetimes bunch. A large $\beta$ packs the failures close to $\eta$, so most units last about the same time; a $\beta$ near 1 spreads them out from very young to very old.


The usual summaries, all from scipy for the tiny portfolio's assets:






  • F(eta) is 0.632 in every row, as the algebra said.
  • The B10 life is the age by which 10% of the fleet has failed, the 0.10 quantile. ppf (the "percent point function") inverts $F$: it returns the age $t$ with $F(t) = 0.10$. In closed form, $t = \eta\,(-\ln(1 - 0.10))^{1/\beta}$. Equipment makers often quote B10 or B1 lives.
  • The median is the age by which half have failed, $\eta (\ln 2)^{1/\beta}$, and the mean is the average lifetime, $\eta\,\Gamma(1 + 1/\beta)$, where $\Gamma$ is the gamma function.


Compare A004 and A006: similar scales (58.8 and 57.8 years), different shapes (1.94 and 3.66). A004's B10 life is 18.4 years, A006's is 31.3: with the smaller shape, a tenth of the breakers are gone 13 years sooner, although both fleets reach 63% failed at about the same age.

A Curve Fitted to Failures Alone Is Wrong: the Assets Still Working Count Too


The analytics team does not invent $\beta$ and $\eta$; it fits them to history: the ages at which assets of a kind failed, and the ages of those that have not failed yet. The second group is the trap. An asset still working at age 35 tells you its lifetime is more than 35, not what it is. Such an observation is right-censored at 35. Most of a utility's assets are censored, because most are still in service.


The standard fitting method, maximum likelihood, picks the $\beta$ and $\eta$ that make the observed history most probable. Each failure at age $t_j$ contributes the density $f(t_j)$; each asset still working at age $c_k$ contributes the probability of lasting that long, $R(c_k)$. In logarithms, the quantity to maximize is



Leave out the second sum and the fit sees only the assets that failed early, so it concludes that the kind fails young and all at about the same age. A fictional fleet of 300 poles shows it: draw their lifetimes from a known curve, observe them until each is 35, and fit twice. CensoredData is scipy's container for observations that are only known to lie above (or below) a value, and weibull_min.fit accepts it; floc=0 keeps the curve starting at age 0:






The fit to failures alone puts the characteristic life at 27.6 years instead of 45, so it would call a healthy 30-year-old pole worn out. The censored fit recovers the true curve to within sampling noise. When you receive curves, ask: "Were the assets still in service included as censored observations?" A team that answers "we used the failures" has handed you a curve that overstates every PoF in the fleet.

Where This Leaves the Model


Nothing in this lesson is a decision; it is the arithmetic that turns two fitted numbers into a probability. Lesson Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF multiplies that probability by the cost of a failure, and lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year turns the result into the coefficient $\text{rr}_{i,s}$ that the start-year model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows maximizes. The model's sets, variables and rows do not change; only its objective coefficients do.


Summary


  • A failure curve says how the lifetimes of similar assets are spread out. $F$, $R = 1 - F$ and $f = F'$ are three views of one Weibull curve, and scipy.stats.weibull_min(c=beta, scale=eta) computes the same numbers.
  • The hazard is the failure rate among the assets still working. $h = f/R = (\beta/\eta)(t/\eta)^{\beta-1}$, a power of age; $\beta < 1$ falls, $= 1$ is flat, $> 1$ rises. It is a rate and can exceed 1.
  • Next year's PoF is conditional on the asset working today. $1 - R(a+1)/R(a)$; the unconditional $F(a+1) - F(a)$ understates an old asset's risk, six times for A006, and the cumulative $F(a)$ is not a yearly probability at all (0.84 against 0.095 for A006).
  • Beta is the shape, and eta is the age by which 63% have failed. Medians, means and B10 lives come from both.
  • A curve fitted to failures alone is wrong. Assets still working are right-censored observations and belong in the likelihood.

Design Note: Why the Course Uses a Parametric Curve at All


The analytics team could hand over a table instead, the share of each kind failing at each age, read straight from history (a life table, or a Kaplan–Meier estimate). Such a table needs no assumption about shape. It also runs out where the data runs out: a utility with few transformers older than 60 has almost nothing to say about A006 at 68. A two-parameter curve extends the trend past the data. That is its strength and its danger: past the oldest observed failure, the curve's answer comes from the assumed shape, not from evidence. When an asset's age is beyond most of its fleet's history, treat its PoF as an extrapolation and ask for its uncertainty.
