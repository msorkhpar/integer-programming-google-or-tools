  16. Consequence and risk

GridCap's reliability engineer brings two lists to the planning meeting. The first ranks assets by probability of failure: an overgrown corridor and an aging transformer lead it. The second ranks them by what a failure would cost: a vegetation circuit and a transmission line lead that one. "Which list do we fund from?" Neither. A planner needs one number per asset that puts likelihood and cost on the same scale, and that number is risk: the expected cost of failure per year. This lesson builds the consequence of failure from its parts, shows what "expected" means, why the two lists and the risk list disagree, and which of two counting conventions the course uses, in four rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its assets, costs and customers describe no real company. Its tiny, small and medium portfolios each number their assets from A001, so an ID names a different asset in each: this lesson's simulation uses tiny's A006, a transformer, and its rankings use small's A006, a vegetation corridor.

Consequence Is the Cost of One Failure, Added Up From Its Parts

The consequence of failure (CoF) of an asset is what one failure of it costs the utility and its customers, in dollars. It is a sum of parts, each of which someone has to estimate:

where $N$ is the number of customers who lose power, $H$ the hours until they are restored, $v$ the cost to a customer of one hour without power, and each $p\,C$ is the chance that a failure causes an injury or a spill times its cost. Utilities differ in which parts they count and how they price them (a cost per customer-hour, a regulator's penalty, a safety weighting), so ask for the parts, not only the total.

One invented substation transformer, with numbers made up for this lesson:

The second line connects the consequence to the reliability measures regulators track. SAIFI (System Average Interruption Frequency Index) is the number of customer interruptions in a year divided by the customers served; SAIDI (System Average Interruption Duration Index) is the customer-minutes (or hours) without power divided by the customers served. One failure of this transformer interrupts 4,200 of the utility's 120,000 customers for 6 hours, so it adds 4,200 / 120,000 = 0.035 to SAIFI and 4,200 × 360 / 120,000 = 12.6 minutes to SAIDI. Lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial uses those measures as benefits in their own right; here they are one way to price the interruption part of CoF.

GridCap keeps it simple: each asset carries one integer cof in $k, which the generator draws evenly between 50 and 4,000. It has no parts and no link to the asset's kind. That is enough for the arithmetic of this section; a real plan needs the parts, because they are where planners and regulators will push back.

Problem statement

A distribution company prices the failure of each feeder before it ranks renewals. A failure interrupts every customer on the feeder. Crews then switch a share switch_share of them to a neighbouring feeder after switch_hours; the rest wait for the repair, repair_hours. The switched share is the same for both customer classes. Customers come in two classes: residential customers cost residential_cost ($k per customer-hour) and critical ones (clinics, water pumping) cost critical_cost. The crew and parts cost repair_cost ($k).

Write two functions in feeders.py:

  • consequence(f): the cost of one failure of feeder f, in $k;
  • saidi_minutes(f, customers_served): how many minutes one failure adds to the company's SAIDI, where customers_served is every customer of the company.

Write each as a formula first: the average hours a customer of this feeder is out, then the interruption cost of each class, then the sum. With $s$ the switched share, the average hours out are $s \cdot \text{switch\_hours} + (1 - s) \cdot \text{repair\_hours}$: the switched customers wait for the switching, the others for the repair. Lesson Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF has the one-class, one-step version. About eight lines.

Risk Is the Expected Failure Cost per Year


Risk combines the two lists. For an asset of age $a$ that is working today:



in $k per year, with PoF from lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull). It is an expected value: the average cost per year over many imagined repetitions of the coming year, not the cost of any actual year. In a real year the asset either fails, costing CoF, or does not, costing nothing. A simulation of tiny's A006, a 68-year-old transformer whose PoF is 0.0947, makes that concrete. rng.random(100_000) draws 100,000 numbers evenly between 0 and 1, and each one below the PoF is a year in which A006 fails:






No single year costs $258.9k; every year costs either 0 or $2,734k. The average over many years is $260.7k, within sampling noise of PoF × CoF. That is why risk adds up across assets and years: the expected cost of a portfolio is the sum of its assets' expected costs, whether or not their failures are independent. And it is why risk is the right objective for a utility that runs thousands of assets for decades, but says nothing about the spread: a plan that cuts expected cost can still leave a small chance of a very bad year.


PoF, CoF and Risk Rank Assets Differently, and a Risk Matrix Blurs Them


Rank the small portfolio's 38 assets three ways:






Only A019 is in all three lists. Small's A006 (a corridor, not the tiny transformer simulated above), the most likely to fail, is out of the risk top five because its failure is cheap ($641k); A014, the most expensive to lose, is out because it is young and unlikely to fail. Two of the risk top five (A005 and A033) are in neither of the other lists: they are moderately likely and moderately expensive. Sorting by one factor at a time misses exactly the assets where both are high enough to matter.


Many utilities show risk as a risk matrix: PoF and CoF each cut into five bands, and each asset placed in one of 25 colored cells. It is a good picture and a poor number. With PoF bands at 1%, 2%, 5% and 10% and CoF bands at $500k, $1,000k, $2,000k and $3,000k:



Four assets share a cell and a color, and the riskiest of them carries 1.6 times the risk of the least. An optimizer that received the cell instead of the product would treat them as equal. Use the matrix to show risk; give the model the product.

Problem statement


A gas utility keeps a register of its line valves. For each valve it has the age today, the Weibull curve of its type (beta, eta in years) and the cost of one failure cof ($k). Each valve in the register is working today. A failed valve is replaced, so the register uses the chance of at least one failure in the coming year, as in lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts. That is why this practice uses PoF although the lesson counts expected failures from its last rule on: the expected count is for an asset that is repaired and kept, and can fail again in the same year, while a valve that fails here is replaced, not repaired, so the question is whether it fails at all.


Write two functions in register.py:


  • risk(v): valve v's expected failure cost in the coming year, $k;
  • rank_by_risk(valves): the valves' ids, highest risk first.


Write the risk as a formula in the lesson's notation first. The tests compute each valve's chance of failing with scipy.stats.weibull_min. About five lines.

Count Expected Failures When a Failed Asset Is Repaired and Kept


PoF counts whether the asset fails in the year: at most once. But a utility usually repairs a failed breaker or line and puts it back in service, and a repaired asset can fail again in the same year. If the repair returns the asset to working order without making it any younger, a convention called minimal repair, its failures arrive at the rate $h(t)$ of lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) for as long as it is kept. Failures then follow a Poisson process whose expected count between ages $a$ and $a + 1$ is the hazard accumulated over the year (the NIST handbook's power-law process, whose expected count $M(t) = (t/\eta)^\beta$ is exactly the Weibull cumulative hazard):




So the two conventions in this course's earlier lessons are two counts of the same curve. Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts multiplied CoF by the PoF; lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal multiplied it by the expected number of failures. Since $1 - e^{-x} \le x$, the expected count is never smaller, and the two are close while both are small:



The A006 in that first line is small's corridor again. On GridCap small the two never differ by more than 0.018 a year. On a badly worn asset they part: at age 40 the invented asset fails 1.66 times a year on average, while the chance of at least one failure can never pass 1. If each failure costs CoF, the expected yearly cost is CoF × expected failures, not CoF × PoF. This course uses the expected count from here on (lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year builds on it):



It adds up exactly over years, because expected counts over consecutive years sum to the expected count over the whole span. The PoF version answers a different question, "how likely is at least one failure," which is the right one for an asset that is retired, not repaired, when it fails.

Where This Enters the Model


Risk is still a coefficient, not a decision. Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts used one year's risk avoided as the objective coefficient $a_i$ of a one-period selection model; lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year sums the risk removed year by year into $\text{rr}_{i,s}$, one coefficient per project and start year, for the multi-period model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows. The rows of those models do not change.


Summary


  • Consequence is the cost of one failure, added up from its parts. Repair, customer interruption ($N \cdot H \cdot v$), safety and environment; the interruption part links to SAIFI and SAIDI. GridCap's cof is one number drawn between $50k and $4,000k.
  • Risk is the expected failure cost per year. PoF × CoF is an average over many years; any one year costs 0 or the CoF.
  • PoF, CoF and risk rank assets differently, and a risk matrix blurs them. Give the model the product, not the cell.
  • Count expected failures when a failed asset is repaired and kept. Expected failures $\Lambda(a+1) - \Lambda(a) \ge \text{PoF}$; the course's yearly risk is CoF times that count.

Design Note: Why Not Put the Risk Matrix's Score in the Model


A risk matrix often comes with a score per cell, such as the PoF band times the CoF band, from 1 to 25. Those scores are ranks, not amounts: a cell scored 20 does not hold twice the risk of one scored 10, and two assets with the same score can differ by a factor of several. An optimizer adds its coefficients and trades them against dollars of budget, so every coefficient must be an amount on a common scale. PoF × CoF in $k per year is such an amount; a matrix score is not. When a utility's only risk data is the matrix, ask for the underlying PoF and CoF; if they do not exist, say in the plan's assumptions that the model ranks by a score, not by expected cost.
