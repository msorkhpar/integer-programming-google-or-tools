A008: 25 years." A director reads the list and asks for A004 and A006 to be replaced this year, since they "have one year left." Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts already noticed that A004's RUL of 1 sits next to a PoF of only 3.4%. RUL Is One Number Taken From a Remaining-Life Distribution

An asset of age $a$ that is working today has a remaining life $T - a$: the years until it fails. Like the lifetime $T$ of lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull), it is random. Its distribution follows from the failure curve by the same conditioning that gave next year's PoF: the chance that the asset is still working $r$ years from now, given that it works today, is

An RUL is one number chosen to summarize that distribution. There are several natural choices, and they are different numbers:

  • the median remaining life $m$, the $r$ at which that ratio falls to one half. For the Weibull, $R(a+m)/R(a) = 1/2$ means $((a+m)/\eta)^\beta - (a/\eta)^\beta = \ln 2$, so
  • the mean remaining life (or mean residual life), the average of $T - a$ over the assets still working at age $a$: $\int_a^\infty R(u)\,du \,/\, R(a)$;
  • a quantile such as P10, the $r$ by which the chance of having failed reaches 10%, a cautious planning figure; or P90, by which it reaches 90%.

scipy computes the others. isf is the inverse survival function: curve.isf(p) is the age at which the survival function equals p. To find where the conditional ratio falls to $1 - q$, ask for the age where $R$ equals $(1 - q) R(a)$. The mean uses expect, which averages a function over the curve; with lb=a and conditional=True it averages only over lifetimes beyond $a$:

Here is every tiny asset's remaining-life distribution next to the RUL the GridCap data carries:

Three things to see. First, the spread is wide: A008's remaining life runs from 5 years (P10) to 67 years (P90). One number cannot carry that. Second, mean and median differ, by up to 5.7 years here (A008), so "RUL 20" means different things to two teams that use different statistics. Third, A004's curve gives a median remaining life of 17.6 years, and its RUL says 1. Both cannot describe the same belief about the asset; the next rule explains where the 1 came from.

Problem statement

A wind farm books a crane months ahead to swap a turbine's gearbox, and wants to book it before the gearbox's chance of failing climbs too high. For a gearbox that is working today at age age, with a Weibull curve of shape beta and scale eta (years), the farm asks: how many years from today until the chance that it has failed reaches q? With q = 0.1 that is the gearbox's P10 remaining life.

Write years_until(age, q, beta, eta) in gearbox.py.

In the lesson's notation the chance of still working $r$ years from now, given working today, is $R(a + r)/R(a)$. Set it to $1 - q$, write it out with $R(t) = e^{-(t/\eta)^\beta}$, and solve for $r$ on paper first. The tests check your answer with scipy.stats.weibull_min. One or two lines.

Problem statement


A water utility's asset team budgets pump renewals on each pump's mean remaining life: the average number of years still ahead, over all pumps of its kind that are working at its age. Each kind's lifetimes follow a Weibull curve of shape beta and scale eta (years).


Write mean_remaining_life(age, beta, eta) in pumps.py. The survival function survival(t) is written for you inside it, and quad from scipy.integrate is imported: quad(g, a, math.inf) returns the area under g from a to infinity, and a bound on its error.


In the lesson's notation the mean remaining life at age $a$ is $\int_a^\infty R(u)\,du \,/\, R(a)$. Decide what each part does before you code it: which area, and why the division.


One numerical trap: for a very old pump, survival(age) is exp of a large negative number and underflows to 0.0 (at age 200 with shape 3 and scale 20 it is $e^{-1000}$), so the area and the divisor are both 0. Integrate the ratio instead, $R(u)/R(a) = \exp\big((a/\eta)^\beta - (u/\eta)^\beta\big)$, which is the same quantity and never forms the tiny number; the reference does that. The tests check your value with scipy.stats.weibull_min.expect, and the old pump against its hazard. About three lines.

Ask What the Number Is: Which Statistic, Which End Point, What Error


An RUL from a machine-learning model is the output of a regression trained on past assets, and each design choice in that training changes what the number means. Before an RUL enters a plan, get written answers to four questions.


  1. Which statistic? Mean, median, a quantile, or "the model's output" with no stated meaning. A regression trained to minimize squared error estimates something like a conditional mean; one trained on a quantile loss estimates that quantile.
  2. Until what? "Useful" life often ends at a threshold, such as a health index below a limit, a dissolved-gas reading above one or a condition grade of 5, not at a failure. An asset at its threshold is due for attention, not certain to fail.
  3. Trained on what? Assets still in service are censored, exactly as in lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull)'s fit. A model trained only on assets that already failed learns that assets fail young. Many models also cap their target or their output at a ceiling or a floor.
  4. How wrong is it? An interval (say P10 to P90) per asset, or at least the error on held-out assets by age band. Prognostics always carries uncertainty, and a point estimate without its error invites the director's reading of "1 year left."

with $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ cutting toward zero (Python's int). So the GridCap RUL is "years until the asset reaches its characteristic life," with noise, cut to whole years and floored at 1. For an asset younger than $\eta$ that lands near the curve's median remaining life. For one already past $\eta$, $\eta - a$ is negative and the floor turns it into 1, whatever the curve says. Across the small portfolio:



Away from the floor, the RUL tracks the curve's median well (within 0.4 years on average). At the floor it carries no information: ten assets read "1 year," and their own curves give them median remaining lives from 3.2 to 21.5 years. A director who funds "everything with RUL 1" funds a list the prediction cannot rank. That is the answer to the scenario: A004 is old for its kind, it is past the age the RUL measures to, and its RUL hit the floor. Its PoF of 3.4% comes from the curve, which says a breaker of 62 that is still working is likely to keep working for many years.


RUL Alone Does Not Fix the PoF: the Shape Decides


Planning needs a probability for each year (lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull)), and the tempting shortcut is a mapping "RUL to PoF": a short RUL means a high PoF. But a median remaining life is one point of the remaining-life distribution, and many curves pass through the same point. Take two assets of the same age, 30, both with a median remaining life of exactly 10 years, one with shape 1 and one with shape 6. For each, the program finds the scale that gives that median with scipy's root finder brentq, which searches an interval for the value where a function crosses zero:






Same age, same RUL, and next year's PoF differs by a factor of two. With shape 1 failures come at a steady rate, so some come early; with shape 6 they bunch near the median, so the next year is fairly safe and the risk is concentrated 8 to 12 years out. An RUL becomes a PoF only together with a shape. Some interview notes say PoF "increases exponentially as RUL approaches 0"; no such rule exists without a curve, and with a Weibull curve the hazard grows as a power of age (lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull)).


To Use an RUL, Make It a Point on a Curve


When the analytics team supplies both a predicted RUL for each asset and a fleet shape $\beta$ for its kind, the two can be combined: choose the scale $\eta$ that makes the RUL the median remaining life of an asset of this age. From $((a+m)/\eta)^\beta - (a/\eta)^\beta = \ln 2$ with $m = \text{RUL}$:




The asset now has its own curve, consistent with its prediction, and every number lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) reads from a curve (next year's PoF, the hazard, the chance of failing within five years) follows. The program checks with scipy that the RUL really is the median of the new curve, then compares the scale and the PoF with the ones GridCap's curve gives directly:






For five assets the two sources roughly agree. For A004 and A006 the RUL of 1 becomes a PoF of exactly 0.5 next year: a median remaining life of one year is a 50% chance of failing within the year. That shows what the floored prediction claims if you take it at its word, and why you must not: the curve built from the assets' history says 3.4% and 9.5%. When an RUL and a curve disagree this much, the answer is a question to the analytics team, not a choice made quietly in the optimization code. Summary


  • RUL is one number taken from a remaining-life distribution. The median, the mean and P10 or P90 of $R(a+r)/R(a)$ are different numbers, and the spread can be decades.
  • Ask what the number is: which statistic, which end point, what error. And how the training treated assets still in service. GridCap's RUL is $\eta - a$ plus noise, floored at 1, so its floor hides a 3- to 21-year range of medians.
  • RUL alone does not fix the PoF: the shape decides. Same age and same median, PoF 6.7% or 3.2%.
  • To use an RUL, make it a point on a curve. With a fleet shape, $\eta = (((a+\text{RUL})^\beta - a^\beta)/\ln 2)^{1/\beta}$ makes the RUL the median, and every probability follows from that curve.

Design Note: Keep the Prediction and the Plan Apart


It is tempting to fit the RUL-to-PoF mapping inside the optimization code, a sigmoid here, a lookup table there, so that the model "uses the ML output directly." This course keeps them apart: the analytics side delivers curves (or RUL plus shape plus its definition), a separate, tested step turns them into coefficients, and the model only reads coefficients. When a plan looks wrong, lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts's question, whether the coefficient, the rule or the objective is at fault, then has an answer you can check: the coefficient step has its own tests against scipy, like the ones in this lesson's practices.
