You are interviewing for an optimization engineer role on a utility's capital-planning tool. The first round is the one every loop has: the interviewer describes planning rules in words and asks you to turn them into a model, out loud and then on a whiteboard. You practised on GridCap, the course's fictional utility; the interviewer brings their own (also fictional) company, a water utility renewing its pipes. Nothing on this page is real data.

How to use the page: read a question, answer it aloud or on paper in about two minutes, and only then open the model answer. Each model answer ends with the follow-ups an interviewer asks next and the lessons that teach the answer. The trap questions are built from answers that sound right and are wrong; some of them come from study guides written for this kind of role. The whiteboard at the end is graded twice: by you, against the model formulation, and by tests, when you code it.

Round 1: A Model Is Decisions, One Objective and Labelled Rules

The interviewer starts broad and narrows down. Say the formulation in the order sets, parameters, variables with their domains, objective, labelled constraints: it is the order an interviewer checks.

Q1. A planner says: "We have 300 candidate pipe renewals and a budget for each of the next five years. Which should we fund, and when?" What are the decisions, the objective and the rules?

Model answer

The decisions are yes/no choices: fund renewal $i$ starting in year $t$, one binary $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for each renewal and each year it may start. The objective is one number that ranks every plan, here the total value of the funded work, $\max \sum_{i,t} v_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$. A second goal (risk, a regional balance) is either folded into that number with a stated weight or written as a constraint; otherwise the model answers a different question. The rules are constraints: each renewal starts at most once, each year's spend stays within its budget, and every rule the planners care about (must-dos, dependencies, alternatives) is a row, because a rule the model does not know about is one it will break. Checking one plan against the rules is cheap; finding the best of $2^{300}$ or more is why a solver is needed.

Follow-ups. "Why not rank by value per dollar?" A ranking ignores every rule but one and cannot say when. "What if the value is uncertain?" Predictions enter as coefficients, and their errors travel with them: ask how wrong a coefficient can be before the plan changes.

Taught in: lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model; lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts; lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows.

Q2. Why one binary per renewal and start year, and not one integer variable "start year" per renewal?

Model answer

Because everything that depends on the start year, the value, the spend in each year and the crews needed, becomes a nonlinear function of a single integer start year. With one binary per year, each of them is a plain sum of data times variables: the spend in year $y$ is $\sum_{t \le y} c_{i,y-t}\, x_{i,t}$, linear in the $x$. The trade is more variables for every expression linear. A timing window costs nothing: create $x_{i,t}$ only for the years in the window, and check that a start in year $t$ still finishes inside the horizon.

Follow-ups. "What stops a renewal starting twice?" The row $\sum_t x_{i,t} \le 1$, or $= 1$ for a must-do; forget it and the solver claims the value twice. "How do you read the schedule?" From the variables whose value is above 0.5, never == 1, because a MILP solver promises whole numbers only within a tolerance.

Taught in: lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows; lesson Cash-flow spreading across years.

Q3. A renewal costs $2.4M over three years. Can you charge it all to its start year?

Model answer

No. A project's cost lands in the years it is worked on, so the budget row of year $y$ sums, over every start year $t \le y$, the cost of the project's $(y-t)$-th year of work. Charging everything to the start year breaks the budgets the plan claims to respect: on GridCap small it overstated NPV by $656k and overspent 2031 by $3.47M. Build the spend of each year once, as an expression, and let every money rule (budgets, caps, regional floors) reuse it.

Follow-ups. "And unspent money?" If it may carry over, a carry variable links each year to the next and the cash row stays an inequality, because money that is neither spent nor carried lapses; written as an equation, it makes a feasible plan infeasible.

Taught in: lesson Cash-flow spreading across years; lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps.

Round 2: Business Rules Are Rows, Written Strong

The interviewer now adds the rules planners actually bring, one at a time, and listens for whether you can say each one as a row.

Q4. "Renewal R12 is a regulatory must-do." Can you give it a very large value instead of a constraint?

Model answer

No. A must-do is an equality row, $\sum_t x_{i,t} = 1$. A large weight hides an infeasible mandate behind an OPTIMAL plan that quietly drops the project; the equality makes the solver report INFEASIBLE, which is the truth the planners need. Must-dos claim their money first, so you can check before solving that each one fits its window and that the must-dos' least spend fits each year; those checks are necessary, not sufficient.

Follow-ups. "What does the mandate cost?" Solve with and without the row and compare the optima; the costs of several mandates do not add up. "One of three mains must be renewed, any one?" One row on the group's sum, $\ge 1$; making each a must-do over-mandates.

Taught in: lesson Must-do vs. discretionary planning groups.

Q5. "R7 may start only after R3 has started, at least two years later." Write the rule.

Model answer

Write it in the cumulative form, one row per year of the child's window: the child can have started by year $t$ only if the parent had started by $t - L$,

with $L = 2$ here. It needs no big-M: if the parent is never funded, the right side is 0 in every year and the child cannot start. The aggregated alternative, one row comparing start years with a big-M term, allows the same 0/1 plans but has a weaker LP bound; in 96 GridCap scenarios the cumulative bound was never weaker and was tighter in 9.

Follow-ups. "What if the lag is from the parent's finish?" Convert it first: finish-to-start with gap $g$ is start-to-start with lag $d_p + g$, where $d_p$ is the parent's duration. "A maximum lag too?" A second row bounds the child from below, switched off when the child is unfunded.

Taught in: lesson Parent-child bundles and all-or-nothing; lesson Precedence with lags; lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms.

Q6. "For main M4 we can repair or replace, not both." And if you must use a big-M somewhere, how do you choose M?

Model answer

Alternatives for one asset share one at-most-one row over all their start variables, $\sum_{i \in g} \sum_t x_{i,t} \le 1$; without it a plan can repair and replace the same asset and claim value that does not exist. A "keep" option makes the row an equality and names the choice.

For a big-M, the smallest valid $M$ is the most the switched row's left side can exceed its right side over every plan the rest of the model allows; variable bounds give a safe value with no solve. Too small an $M$ silently refuses real plans; too large weakens the LP bound and lets the integrality tolerance switch a rule off (with $M = 10^7$, HiGHS reported OPTIMAL for a plan worth more than any allowed plan). After every solve, check the plan against the rule from the data.

Follow-ups. "Why not an indicator constraint?" See the trap questions below.

Taught in: lesson Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace); lesson Deriving big-M from bounds; lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics.

Trap Questions: Rows That Look Right and Are Not

Each trap is a question whose common answer is wrong. Decide what is wrong before you open it.

T1. "A lasts $d_A$ years, and B may start only after a gap of $g$ years once A has finished, so B starts at least $d_A + g$ years after A starts. Write $\sum_t t\,x_{B,t} \ge \sum_t (t + d_A + g)\,x_{A,t} - M(1 - \sum_t x_{B,t})$." Is that right?

What is wrong

The row compares start years, which is fine when both are funded, but when A is not funded its right side is $-M(1 - \sum_t x_{B,t})$, at most 0, so B may start whenever it likes: the rule "B needs A" has gone. The program formulation_traps.py fixes three plans and asks HiGHS whether each form allows them (A lasts 2 years, then a 1-year gap, so the lag start to start is 3):

The row also needs an $M$ the size of a calendar year (2034 here), which is the scaling trouble of lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics. The cumulative rows of Q5 state both the need and the lag with no $M$.

Taught in: lesson Precedence with lags; lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms.

T2. "B may start one year after A is completed: $\sum_{s \le t-1} x_{A,s} \ge x_{B,t}$ for every $t$." Is that the rule?

What is wrong

That row is start-to-start with a lag of one year: it lets B start one year after A starts. If A takes three years, "one year after A is completed" is start-to-start with lag $d_A + 1 = 4$. Convert every lag to start-to-start before writing rows: $L = d_A + g$ for a finish-to-start gap $g$. Read that way, lesson Precedence with lags found that one of GridCap's projects could never start in its window, which is worth knowing before the solve.

Taught in: lesson Precedence with lags.

T3. "Indicator constraints avoid big-M, so always use them." Agree?

What is wrong

Whether an indicator helps depends on the solver. In OR-Tools 9.15, MathOpt's add_indicator_constraint is solved by SCIP (one-sided implied rows only: no ranges, no equalities), and refused by HiGHS, CP-SAT through MathOpt, GLOP and PDLP, so a model written with indicators cannot move to HiGHS without being rewritten. A solver that accepts one still needs bounds on the variables to relax it. Use a tight big-M when the data give the bounds, an indicator when they do not and the solver takes it, and check the plan against the rule after every solve. CP-SAT's own only_enforce_if is the native form when the model is all-integer.

Taught in: lesson Indicator constraints; lesson Indicator constraints vs. big-M.

T4. "Two renewals are identical, so add $x_{1,t} \ge x_{2,t}$ for every year to break the symmetry." Safe?

What is wrong

An ordering row must keep at least one copy of every plan. This one says "if the second starts in year $t$, the first starts in that same year", so a plan that starts the two in different years has no copy left. With one start a year affordable, the best plan funds both, a year apart, and the same-year row throws it away:

Order by "started by year $t$" instead, $\sum_{u \le t} x_{1,u} \ge \sum_{u \le t} x_{2,u}$: the first renewal starts no later than the second, and every plan keeps one copy. Then measure: HiGHS also breaks symmetry itself.

Taught in: lesson Symmetry and how to break it.

Problem statement

A water utility's planners write their dependency rules in whatever words come to hand: "R7 starts two years after R3 starts", "R9 starts a year after R4 is finished", "R5 must be finished no earlier than R2 is finished". Before any row is written, every rule is turned into one start-to-start lag $L$, meaning the child may start in year $t$ only if the parent started in some year $s \le t - L$. A renewal of duration $d$ started in year $s$ finishes at $s + d$.

Write two functions in lags.py:

  • start_to_start(kind, lag, parent_duration, child_duration) returns $L$ for a lag measured between the two events kind names: "SS" (parent's start to child's start), "FS" (parent's finish to child's start), "FF" (parent's finish to child's finish) or "SF" (parent's start to child's finish). The child's event must come at least lag years after the parent's. $L$ may be negative.
  • usable_child_years(parent_starts, child_starts, lag) returns, in increasing order, the child's start years that at least one of the parent's start years serves, for a start-to-start lag. The others can be deleted from the model before it is built.

Write each conversion as an inequality between the two events first, then solve it for the child's start. The tests use their own numbers. About ten lines in total.

Summary


  • Round 1. Decisions are binaries, one per renewal and start year; the objective is one number; every rule is a labelled row. A cost spread over years is charged in each year it is spent, and an integer "start year" variable would make every year-dependent quantity nonlinear.
  • Round 2. A must-do is an equality row, never a large weight. Precedence is a cumulative row per year, with the lag converted to start-to-start first. Alternatives share one at-most-one row. A big-M comes from bounds, and a loose one weakens the bound and invites tolerance tricks.
  • Traps. A time-sum precedence row with big-M lets the child start when the parent is unfunded. A start-to-start row does not state a lag from the parent's finish. Indicator support depends on the solver (SCIP takes them through MathOpt, HiGHS refuses them). A symmetry row must keep one copy of every plan; a same-year order row keeps none for plans that start the two in different years.
