This round tests whether you can answer them with something better than a shrug: statuses you can trust, conflicts you can name, prices you can qualify, and re-solves that give a decision its cost. The interviewer's portfolio is fictional, like GridCap; the questions are the ones a planning team asks every cycle.

Round 1: A Failed Scenario Gets a Status, a Conflict and a Repair

Q1. The solver says INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED. What do you do?

Model answer

Read it as "the solver could not tell", not as "infeasible". INFEASIBLE means no plan exists; UNBOUNDED means plans exist and improve forever, almost always a modeling bug (a missing bound or a sign error). To settle it, solve a copy with a zero objective: a plan means the original was unbounded, no plan means infeasible. Turning presolve off settled it for GLOP and for HiGHS, not for every solver; Gurobi answers INF_OR_UNBD until DualReductions=0. To find what runs away, cap every free variable at a large bound and see which ones reach it. And a limit that ends with no plan means "unknown", never "infeasible".

Follow-ups. "Before the solve?" Validate the inputs: each check reads one project, group or region against one limit and names the numbers. A check must never refuse a scenario that has a plan, and passing every check does not prove a plan exists.

Taught in: lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell; lesson Validating inputs before the solve; lesson Infeasible, unbounded and degenerate LPs.

Q2. What is an IIS, and how do you find one without Gurobi?

Model answer

An irreducible infeasible subsystem is a set of rows (and bounds) that cannot hold together, where dropping any one of them leaves a feasible set. It is one conflict: irreducible, but neither unique nor the smallest, and a scenario can have several. A deletion filter finds one with any solver: drop each row in turn and keep it out if the rest is still infeasible, one solve per row. Its order picks which conflict survives: it keeps the conflict it tests last, so put the rows you want named last. An additive filter builds a conflict up from nothing instead. To list every conflict, find one, switch off one of its rows and look again. gurobipy's computeIIS also counts variable bounds as members unless you force them in.

Follow-ups. "Pyomo has an IIS tool, doesn't it?" Its MIS tool works on the LP relaxation, so it missed a conflict that only integrality causes and declared that scenario feasible; write_iis asks Gurobi.

Taught in: lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo; lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem; lesson Piecewise functions and the IIS tools.

Q3. The planners want a plan anyway. How do you tell them what to change?

Model answer

Make the rows elastic: give each row that may bend a nonnegative variable, $a^\top x - o \le u$, and minimize the bends at a price. The elastic model always has a plan, and the original is feasible exactly when the least violation is 0. Price every bend in one currency: when every unit costs 1, dollars, crews and projects mix, and the flat repair broke a board override because one project cost the same as $1k. With prices in money, the course's repair was $40k more in 2028 plus seven crew-months, and the best plan with that repair was worth $17,060k. Then write it for the planners: the rules and projects by name (never row names), who set each number, the arithmetic of the closest miss, and the changes that end each conflict, checked by a solve.

Taught in: lesson Elastic constraints and feasibility relaxation; lesson Turning an IIS into a planner message.

A conflict you report is only as good as the filter that produced it. The first code practice writes the deletion filter against an oracle, so the order rule of Q2 is something you have seen happen.

  1. Coding practice

    A deletion filter

    • return one conflict among a failed scenario's rules, one feasibility question per rule, in a set order

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A planning service must tell planners which of a failed scenario's rules clash. Its solver can only answer "can these rules hold together?", one solve per question. Write deletion_filter(rules, feasible) in filters.py:

  • rules is a list of rule names; feasible(subset) returns True when the rules in subset can all hold together.
  • Return one conflict: a list of rules that cannot hold together, from which no rule can be dropped without the rest becoming feasible. Keep the rules in the order they were given.
  • Start with every rule kept. Then take the rules one at a time, in the order given, each exactly once: ask whether the rules kept so far, without this one, are still infeasible. If they are, drop this rule for good; if not, it stays. A rule dropped early is never asked about again, so the conflict whose rules come last in the list is the one that survives, and the caller decides which conflict is reported by ordering the rules.
  • When all the rules can hold together, there is no conflict: return an empty list. One call of feasible on the whole list before the loop is allowed for this, and the worked solution makes it; it is not one of the per-rule tests.

The tests replace the solver with a small oracle. About eight lines.

Round 2: A MILP Has No Prices, So Reasons Come From Re-Solves


Q4. Finance asks for the shadow price of the 2029 budget in the capital plan. What do you say?


Model answer
A MILP has no duals: the solver reports none (MathOpt's has_dual_feasible_solution() is False and dual_values raises ValueError), because the value of the budget is a step function and a price is a line that touches it from above, which a step function almost never has. The LP relaxation has prices, but they price a plan nobody can fund. The fixed-integer re-solve (fix every integer at its value, make it continuous, re-solve) prices only what stayed free, holds only with that plan fixed and only inside its range, and in an all-binary model there is nothing free for the budget to price. Tell finance the steps: where the next step in the budget-value curve is, how high, and what a round amount buys.


Follow-ups. "How do you find the steps?" Two solves per step: the cheapest plan worth more, then the best value at that cost; GridCap small has 1,090 steps.


Taught in: lesson Why a MILP has no duals; lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something; lesson Parametric analysis: the budget-value frontier; lesson The marginal value of the next dollar.


Q5. "Why was P24 not funded?"


Model answer
Force the opposite and re-solve: $\text{started}(i) = 1$, and the cost of the decision is what the best plan loses (GridCap's P024 costs $273k to force in; P032 costs $112k to lose, against its own NPV of $3,678k). Then say why in the rules: the rows that refuse the project as the plan stands, the projects that give way, then the cost. A forcing the rules cannot satisfy (a must-do forced out) is answered by the conflicting rules, never a number. Small costs are near ties, not reasons, and a cost of 0 would mean another optimum.


Follow-ups. "And between two scenarios?" A diff by project id in three lists (selected, deferred, shifted), with the input diff and the money beside it, changing one input at a time so each change can be charged to its cause.


Taught in: lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out; lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted.


Q6. What goes into a binding-constraints report for a MILP?


Model answer
For each row, its slack measured from the plan and the data, with no help from the solver, so it works on any model; a row binds when the slack is zero. On a MILP, the rows that bind are often the once, must and alternative rows, which record what was chosen. A row with slack can still be the row that blocks, because whole projects cannot use the last few dollars, so list for each budget row the single additions it refuses (on GridCap small the 2030 budget refused 15 of 25 starts). Prices, where there are any, go in their own column. Different readers then get different reports from one record of the run: projects and rules for planners, money by year and the gap for finance, inputs, settings and checks for auditors.

Trap Questions: Prices and Conflicts That Promise Too Much


T1. "A constraint is binding when its slack is zero and its shadow price is nonzero." Right?


What is wrong
Binding means zero slack. A binding row can have a zero price when the LP is degenerate, more rows tight at the vertex than it needs. diagnostic_traps.py solves $\max x + y$ with $x \le 1$, $y \le 1$ and $x + y \le 2$ with GLOP:



All three rows bind; GLOP put the price on two of them. Lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something saw must_P003 bind with price 0, and lesson A binding-constraints report found 11 binding GridCap rows at price 0 in the LP relaxation. Test slack and price separately and report both.


Taught in: lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness; lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something; lesson A binding-constraints report.


T2. "Fix the integers, re-solve, and the budget price of 1.25 means each extra $1,000 adds $1,250." Fair?


What is wrong
It is a local, conditional statement. The price holds only with the selection fixed and only inside its range; inside, it is a floor on the MILP's gain, and outside it says nothing. The MILP's value of the next dollar is a step function: with GridCap's costs in multiples of $10k, the next $1k buys nothing at most budgets and a whole step at a few. In an all-binary model the fixed LP has nothing continuous left for the budget to price:



The budget row binds (cost 50 of 50) and its price is 0; the fixed bounds carry the prices instead, and a fixed binary's reduced cost is its value against that, not a reason it was funded.


Taught in: lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something; lesson The marginal value of the next dollar; lesson Duals, reduced costs and sensitivity attributes.


T3. "Pyomo's dual suffix gives MILP duals, and its IIS tool explains any infeasible model." True?


What is wrong
Neither. The dual suffix raises on a MILP; it does not run the fixed-integer re-solve for you. Fixing the integers, relaxing integrality and re-solving is your job, after checking the plan. Pyomo's MIS tool works on the LP relaxation, so a conflict that only integrality causes is invisible to it: it declared GridCap's integer conflict feasible.


Taught in: lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something; lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo; lesson Solving with HiGHS and reading results.


T4. "The solve hit its time limit with no plan, so tell the planners the scenario is infeasible." OK?


What is wrong
A limit without a plan means "unknown". NO_SOLUTION_FOUND with a limit says the search stopped, not that no plan exists; only INFEASIBLE is a proof. Report it as its own status (the course's API returns time_limit with or without a plan, and infeasible only when proven).


Taught in: lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell; lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count; lesson Long solves: submit, poll, fetch.


Whiteboard: The Cheapest Repair of an Over-Committed Scenario


"Our must-do projects no longer fit the yearly budgets. Finance will lend money in some years more cheaply than in others. Find the cheapest overspend, year by year, that makes the plan possible, and tell us what any given plan would cost in overspend."


Model formulation
Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the years; $M \subseteq P$ the must-dos; $P_y$ the projects that spend in year $y$.


Parameters. $c_i$, project $i$'s cost in its year ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $\pi_y$, the price of one $k of overspend in year $y$.


Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$; $o_y \ge 0$ for every $y \in Y$, the overspend allowed in year $y$ ($k).


Objective.



Constraints.



The must-do rows stay hard: the question is what the money must do, not which mandate to drop. Follow-ups: "Why $o_y \ge 0$?" Without it an underspent year earns a credit that pays for another year's overspend, which no lender offers. "And then the best plan?" Summary


  • Statuses. INFEASIBLE is a proof; INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED is settled by a zero-objective copy; a limit without a plan is unknown, never infeasible.
  • Conflicts. An IIS is one conflict, irreducible but not unique; a deletion filter finds one with any solver and keeps the conflict it tests last. Pyomo's MIS tool sees only the LP relaxation.
  • Repairs. Elastic rows priced in one currency give a repair planners can price.
  • Prices. A MILP has no duals. A fixed-integer price holds only with the plan fixed and inside its range; the next dollar's value is a step function. Binding means zero slack, and a binding row can have a zero price.
  • Reasons. Force a decision the other way and re-solve; report the rules, the projects that give way and the cost, and read small costs as near ties.
