This round is about the numbers the model maximizes and about what happens when there is more than one of them. The interviewer works for a (fictional) gas utility whose repair candidates each carry an NPV and a safety risk removed, and whose planners argue about which matters more. Expect questions on discounting, failure probabilities, value functions and Pareto fronts, and expect the interviewer to check your formulas term by term.

Round 1: Every Coefficient Is Computed Before the Model

Q1. How do you value a project that may start in any of five years?

Model answer

One coefficient per project and start year, computed before the model: shift the project's cash flows so that its year-$k$ amount lands in calendar year $t + k$, then discount every amount to one valuation date, $F/(1 + r)^{y - y_0}$. Valuing each start from its own year makes delay look free (lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year: P022's loss from waiting showed as $235k instead of $395k). Count benefits to the end of the asset's life, not the end of the plan: the plan's horizon limits which start variables exist, not how long a project pays. The rate reaches the plan only through the coefficients; GridCap small's plan was the same at 3% and 7% and changed 14 starts at 12%.

Follow-ups. "NPV or IRR to rank?" NPV at the chosen rate. "Why is the model still linear?" The nonlinear work happens before the model; the objective is $\sum_{i,t} \text{npv}_{i,t}\,x_{i,t}$.

Taught in: lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates; lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year.

Q2. The cost estimate is in 2026 dollars and costs escalate 4% a year. How do you treat it?

Model answer

Escalate from the price's base year $b$: the price paid in year $y$ is $c\,(1 + e)^{y - b}$. Pair real dollars with a real rate and nominal dollars with a nominal rate; mixing them errs in opposite directions, and the exact Fisher relation converts between them. In a real model only the escalation above inflation matters, $e_\text{real} = (1 + e)/(1 + i) - 1$. A check on any borrowed formula: with $e = r$ and the base year equal to the valuation date, escalation and discounting should cancel exactly.

Taught in: lesson Escalation; real vs. nominal dollars.

Q3. The data team hands you a Weibull curve and an RUL per transformer. How does that become a coefficient?

Model answer

Next year's probability of failure is conditional on the asset working today: $\text{PoF} = 1 - R(a + 1)/R(a)$, with $R(a) = e^{-(a/\eta)^\beta}$. Risk is the expected failure cost per year, PoF times the consequence of failure, and with minimal repair the course counts expected failures, $\Lambda(a + 1) - \Lambda(a)$. The risk an intervention removes in a year is the risk without the work minus the risk with it, the asset ageing again after the work, summed over a fixed window and discounted; those coefficients $\text{rr}_{i,s}$ enter the start-year model unchanged. An RUL is one statistic of a remaining-life distribution: ask which one (median, mean, P10) and how censored assets were treated, and turn it into a point on a curve; the same age and median can give PoF 6.7% or 3.2% depending on the shape.

Taught in: lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull); lesson Remaining useful life: what an ML model actually hands you; lesson Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF; lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year.

Round 2: Two Objectives Need a Rule for the Trade

Q4. Planners want reliability, safety, environmental and financial benefit in one objective. How do you build it so it survives a review?

Model answer

Each benefit measure counts one consequence, in one unit, with more always better. Raw measures cannot be added: the units choose the winner. Divide each by a reference fixed before the candidates are scored, with zero meaning no benefit, so the scores are unit-free and a withdrawn project cannot reorder the others. A weight is then an exchange rate between units, so set weights by swing weighting (rate each measure's swing from 0 to its reference against the biggest), restate the result as prices people can read ("an incident is worth $6,667k"), and sweep the contested weights to show which rankings survive. The value function is one coefficient per project and start year, $v_{i,t} = \sum_k w_k\, m_{i,t,k}/R_k$, and each consequence is counted once.

Taught in: lesson Benefit measures: reliability, safety, environmental, financial; lesson Normalizing different units; lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in; lesson Building the CIPT value function.

Q5. Weighted sum, lexicographic or ε-constraint for NPV against risk removed?

Model answer

A weighted sum folds the two into one objective; only the ratio of the weights matters, the ratio is a price that depends on the units, and the plan moves in jumps. It reaches only supported Pareto points, those on the upper convex hull of the front: GridCap small has 65 Pareto-optimal plans and weighted sums reach 8. Lexicographic order solves one objective, then lets the next choose among plans within a stated tolerance of the first: a 1% NPV tolerance bought 20.6% more risk removed. ε-constraint keeps one objective and turns the other into a floor; it reaches unsupported points too. Choose by the question the planners ask: a price (weights), a priority with a tolerance (lexicographic), or a target (ε).

Follow-ups. "Can a weighted sum return a dominated plan?" With positive weights, no; a zero weight promises only weak Pareto optimality. "How do you implement lexicographic in OR-Tools?" No runner solver takes MathOpt's auxiliary objectives, so solve in passes, each to a zero gap, holding earlier objectives within their tolerances.

Taught in: lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean; lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points; lesson Hierarchical objectives with tolerances; lesson Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve; lesson The ε-constraint method.

Q6. How do you present the trade-off to planners?

Model answer

Generate the frontier once and check that no point dominates another, with a tolerance for solver noise. Show a short table where each row is a plan a planner could pick: the two ends, the best plan at round targets and the knees (9 rows instead of 65 on GridCap small). State each step as a price, "$x of NPV per $1 of risk removed", and say where the price jumps; then say the trade-off in one sentence generated from the same data as the table.

Taught in: lesson Generating a frontier and presenting it to planners.

Trap Questions: Formulas That Look Right and Are Not

T1. "The Weibull CDF $F(a) = 1 - e^{-(a/\eta)^\beta}$ is the asset's probability of failing this year." Agree?

What is wrong

$F(a)$ is the chance of having failed by age $a$, for an asset of unknown history. The asset in the plan is working today, so next year's PoF is conditional: $1 - R(a+1)/R(a)$. The program value_traps.py prints the three readings for one asset:

The CDF overstates the yearly risk eightfold here; the unconditional difference understates it by half. Lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull) found the same pattern on GridCap's A006 (0.84 against 0.095). The same study guides say PoF "rises exponentially" as RUL runs out; a Weibull hazard is a power of age, $(\beta/\eta)(a/\eta)^{\beta - 1}$, and RUL alone does not fix the PoF.

Taught in: lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull); lesson Remaining useful life: what an ML model actually hands you.

T2. "Escalate each project's cost by $(1+e)^\tau$, with $\tau$ counted from the project's start." Right?

What is wrong

Counted from the start, a project that starts later escalates less, so delay looks cheaper than it is. Escalation runs from the price's base year:

The error grows with every year of delay, $129.6k of present value at a 2030 start. Lesson Escalation; real vs. nominal dollars found $128k missing for GridCap's P022 at 2029.

Taught in: lesson Escalation; real vs. nominal dollars.

T3. "$\text{NPV}_{i,t} = \sum_{\tau = t}^{T} \text{CF}_{i,\tau}/(1+r)^\tau$, with $T$ the plan's last year." Is the sum right?

What is wrong

It stops counting at the plan's end, so a project that pays back over decades looks like a loss, and a later start looks worse than it is:

Count to the end of the asset's life; lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year saw a $146k project turn into a loss at every start when it was cut at the horizon.

Taught in: lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year.

T4. "Sweep ε over a grid and you have the exact Pareto frontier." True?

What is wrong

Only with steps fine enough for the data, and only if each answer is efficient. A sweep finds at most one plan per floor: 21 floors found 20 of GridCap small's 65 Pareto plans. Plain ε-constraint can also return a weakly efficient plan (6 of 11 answers were dominated when vegetation was booked at NPV 0). AUGMECON rewards the floor's slack with a weight too small to cost any of the main objective, and with the whole-number objective in the floor and steps of one, the frontier is exact.

Taught in: lesson The ε-constraint method; lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points.

T5. "Model the value curve with SOS2: no binaries needed." In OR-Tools?

What is wrong

MathOpt's Python model in OR-Tools 9.15 has no SOS method, nor does CP-SAT, and HiGHS takes no SOS2 (Pyomo's default SOS2 piecewise form fails with HiGHS). The course writes the λ form with one binary per segment, or the incremental or logarithmic forms. A convex cost pushed down, or a concave value pushed up, needs no binaries at all; any other combination does.

Summary


  • Coefficients. Value each start year by shifting the cash flows and discounting to one valuation date; count benefits to the end of the asset's life. Escalate from the price's base year. Next year's PoF is conditional on the asset working today; the CDF is the chance of having failed by now, not a yearly probability.
  • Value functions. Normalize by fixed references, treat weights as exchange rates, set them by swing weighting, and show the prices they imply.
  • Two objectives. A weighted sum reaches only supported Pareto points; lexicographic order holds earlier objectives within a tolerance; ε-constraint turns one objective into a floor and reaches unsupported points, but a coarse sweep misses plans and plain ε can return weakly efficient ones.
  • Traps. CDF as yearly PoF, escalation from the start year, NPV cut at the plan's end, "ε grids are exact", and SOS2 in a stack that has none.
