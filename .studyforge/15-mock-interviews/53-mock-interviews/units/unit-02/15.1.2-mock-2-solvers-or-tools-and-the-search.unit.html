Round 1: The Search Brackets the Optimum Between Two Numbers

Q1. What is the LP relaxation of a MILP, and how does branch-and-bound use it?

Model answer

The LP relaxation drops the whole-number rule, so a binary may take any value in $[0, 1]$. Every allowed plan is a point of the relaxation, so its value bounds every plan: from above when maximizing, $z_\text{LP} \ge z_\text{MILP}$. Branch-and-bound solves that LP, picks a variable with a fractional value and splits the problem into two children, $x \le \lfloor v \rfloor$ and $x \ge \lceil v \rceil$; every integer plan lies in exactly one child and the fractional point in neither. A node is pruned when its LP is infeasible, when its LP answer is integral (a plan, maybe a new incumbent), or when its bound cannot beat the incumbent. The size of a model does not decide how hard it is; the strength of the bound does: GridCap medium's 400 binaries were solved at the first node, while a 30-binary puzzle with a weak relaxation was not solved in 100,000.

Follow-ups. "Which node next?" Depth-first finds plans early and keeps few nodes; best-first solves the fewest nodes but holds many and finds its first plan late; no rule wins everywhere, so measure on your own model. "Why not round the LP answer?" Rounding up broke GridCap's budget on all 30 small portfolios tried, rounding to the nearest on 13; rounding down kept the rows and lost 3.1% on average. A rounded plan comes with no proof of how far it is from the best.

Taught in: lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails; lesson Why MILP is hard: combinatorial growth in numbers; lesson Branching on a fractional variable; lesson Bounds, incumbents and pruning; lesson Node and variable selection.

Q2. The log says the gap is 0.01% and the status is OPTIMAL. What exactly do you know?

Model answer

A MILP solver keeps two numbers: the incumbent, the best plan found (the primal bound), and the best bound, a value no plan can beat (the dual bound). The optimum lies between them, and the run stops when they are close enough. OPTIMAL means optimal within the tolerances the run used: HiGHS's default relative gap is $10^{-4}$ (lesson What "optimal at 0.01%" means on a $2B portfolio measured it in the HiGHS log), so its OPTIMAL can leave a gap above zero. 0.01% is an amount of money: on GridCap large's $6.0B NPV it is $601k, more than most single projects. Plans within the gap can pick different projects, so a question about one project needs its own solves. Report the tolerance and the bracket, and choose the tolerance in money.

Follow-ups. "Can the relative gap exceed 100%?" Yes; nothing caps it. "What if a constant is in the objective?" It shrinks the relative gap without improving the plan: with $20B added, a 0.0021% gap hid a 0.1873% gap on the NPV.

Taught in: lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap; lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count; lesson What "optimal at 0.01%" means on a $2B portfolio.

Q3. Walk me through a MIP log: what happens before the first branch?

Model answer

The header says what the solver received (sizes, coefficient ranges). Presolve then rewrites the model into a smaller one with the same optimal value: on the five-year model, 400 binaries became 172. At the root, cutting planes cut off the LP's fractional point without removing any plan, and primal heuristics (rounding, diving, RINS) race to find plans, so the gap can fall from Large to a fraction of a percent before any branching. Each table row is a snapshot of nodes, bound, incumbent and gap; a letter says which heuristic found each new plan. The final report is the verdict, and Optimal there means within the gap tolerance: the default run stopped at 274,979 against a proved optimum of 274,999.

Follow-ups. "Do cuts and heuristics matter?" On the five-year model SCIP needed 718 nodes with cuts and 1,966 without, and 1,333 without heuristics. "Should I turn presolve off?" It changes the work, not the answer; measure before deciding.

Taught in: lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand; lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first; lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS; lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line.

A gap is a number you will compute and report yourself, for logs, dashboards and finance. The first code practice writes the report, with both denominators the course measured.

  1. Coding practice

    A gap report

    • report the absolute gap and HiGHS's and SCIP's relative gaps from an incumbent and a bound

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A planning team's dashboard shows every solve's gap, and finance asked why HiGHS and SCIP runs of the same scenario report slightly different percentages. Write gap_report(primal, dual) in gaps.py. primal is the incumbent's objective value (infinite, with either sign, when no plan was found) and dual is the best bound. It returns a dict with three entries:

  • "absolute": the distance between the two numbers, never negative;
  • "relative_highs": the absolute gap divided by the size of the incumbent;
  • "relative_scip": the absolute gap divided by the smaller of the two numbers in size.

When there is no plan, every entry is None. A relative gap whose divisor is zero is None too: a dashboard must not show a made-up number. When there is a plan but no bound has been proved yet (dual infinite, with either sign), the gap is not unknown but unbounded: the absolute gap is math.inf, and so is each relative gap whose divisor is not zero. The function works for maximizations and minimizations alike. About ten lines.

Round 2: The API Is Chosen by the Model, and the Result Is Read in Order


Q4. MathOpt, CP-SAT or the linear solver wrapper: which for a multi-year capital plan, and which for a crew schedule with intervals?


Model answer
Start in MathOpt: it keeps the model apart from the solver, so one model is built once and solved by HiGHS, SCIP or CP-SAT by changing one argument, and it is the API that returns LP duals when the model has continuous amounts. Intervals, sequencing and dense logic over integers go to CP-SAT, which has no continuous variables and returns no duals. A problem that is exactly an assignment, flow or knapsack can go to its special solver, until one rule cannot be said in its inputs; routes go to the routing library. pywraplp is for code you inherit and migrate. On a plain selection both MathOpt with HiGHS and CP-SAT prove the same optimum; on GridCap medium's ten-year selection HiGHS ended closest to proven, and on a big-M disjunction CP-SAT proved optima HiGHS could not.


Follow-ups. "How do you choose for sure?" Measure on your own model: one model, deterministic limits, statuses and bounds, several instances.

Q5. Your code calls result.objective_value() right after mathopt.solve. What is missing?


Model answer
The termination check. Read result.termination.reason first: OPTIMAL and FEASIBLE carry a plan, INFEASIBLE and NO_SOLUTION_FOUND do not, and objective_value() and variable_values() raise ValueError when there is no plan. A gap stop ends OPTIMAL; a time, node or solution limit ends FEASIBLE (a plan) or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND (none), with termination.limit naming the limit. A limit without a plan means "unknown", never "infeasible". The bounds, primal_bound() and dual_bound(), are always there and use infinities for "nothing found" and "nothing proven". Map each reason to what the caller does, with no catch-all branch.


Follow-ups. "And dual values?" Only from an LP solve, after checking has_dual_feasible_solution(); no solver returns duals for a MILP.


Taught in: lesson Reading SolveResult: termination, primal and dual solutions, statistics; lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count; lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell.


Q6. How do you set a time limit and a gap for HiGHS, and what happens if you pass a parameter the solver does not take?


Model answer
Through mathopt.SolveParameters: time_limit as a timedelta and relative_gap_tolerance or absolute_gap_tolerance; those three worked on every solver tried. Solver-specific options go in that solver's own parameters and win over common ones. Each solver accepts only some common parameters: HiGHS refuses threads, solution_pool_size and cuts, CP-SAT refuses node_limit. A refusal raises before the solve; in OR-Tools 9.15 it surfaces as an AttributeError whose real error is on __context__. HiGHS's thread count is fixed at the first solve of a process. Lesson Common vs. solver-specific parameters, and what each solver rejects measured each of these: which solver refuses which parameter, and the error on __context__.


Follow-ups. "Which solvers ship in the pip package?" GLOP, PDLP, HiGHS, SCIP and CP-SAT; Gurobi needs its own library and license.


Taught in: lesson Common vs. solver-specific parameters, and what each solver rejects; lesson Switching solvers: HiGHS, SCIP, CP-SAT, GLOP, Gurobi.


Trap Questions: Claims About the API That Do Not Hold


T1. "The relative gap is $|P - D| / \max(10^{-10}, |P|)$." Is that the definition?


What is wrong
It is one convention, not the definition. Solvers divide by different things, and MathOpt's own docstring calls the normalization solver-dependent. Lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap stopped HiGHS and SCIP at their first plans and matched each log against three divisions: HiGHS divides by the incumbent, SCIP by the smaller of the two numbers in size. The same tolerance therefore asks the two solvers for slightly different stops. Near zero a relative gap means little (dividing by an incumbent of 0 gives infinity), so profit-like models are judged by the absolute gap.


Taught in: lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap.


T2. "Call solver.SolveWithAssumptions(model, flags), and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility() gives you the minimal conflict." Right?


What is wrong
Neither half. The Python API has no SolveWithAssumptions: assumptions are added to the model with add_assumptions, the model is solved as usual with one worker, and the solver reports sufficient_assumptions_for_infeasibility(), a list of variable indices to map back to rule names. The set is sufficient for the conflict, not promised minimal. solver_traps.py guards five rules with flags, three of which clash:



Here the named set was already minimal, as it was in lesson CP-SAT assumptions as a conflict finder; on GridCap small at 60% of the budget, lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility got all 28 rule flags back, and a deletion filter cut them to 3. Check a named set with a filter before calling it minimal.


Taught in: lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility; lesson CP-SAT assumptions as a conflict finder.


T3. "Import google.ortools.math_opt, solve with SolverType.GUROBI, and MathOpt has no IIS, so switch to gurobipy for that." What is wrong?


What is wrong
Three things. The import is from ortools.math_opt.python import mathopt. MathOpt does have an IIS call, mathopt.compute_infeasible_subsystem; in the course runner HiGHS, SCIP and CP-SAT refuse it with UNIMPLEMENTED, its docstring names Gurobi as the only solver that supports it, and Gurobi cannot be loaded through MathOpt in the runner (OR-Tools 9.15 against the Gurobi 13 library), so no lesson shows a MathOpt-Gurobi result. A deletion filter finds a conflict with any solver, and gurobipy's computeIIS is the route when Gurobi is available.


Taught in: lesson Versions and the backends that ship with it; lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem; lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo.


T4. "CP-SAT is fast, so give it the whole model through MathOpt, budget amounts in dollars and cents included." Fine?


What is wrong
CP-SAT has no continuous variables. Handed one through MathOpt, it rounds it and still reports OPTIMAL, so the plan answers a different model. Give CP-SAT all-integer models, scaling any fractional data to whole numbers yourself with an exact multiplier, or keep continuous amounts in a MIP solver.


Taught in: lesson Switching solvers: HiGHS, SCIP, CP-SAT, GLOP, Gurobi; lesson Objectives, integer scaling and solution callbacks.


T5. "The GPU solver said OPTIMAL, so the plan is exact."


What is wrong
OPTIMAL means optimal within the run's tolerances on any solver (Q2), and a GPU's LP method works to a looser one. PDLP stops when its errors fall below a tolerance, not at an exact vertex as the simplex method does. On the same 1M-variable transportation LP, HiGHS's GPU PDLP landed within $2 \times 10^{-9}$ relative of the interior point optimum, and cuOpt's PDLP about $3 \times 10^{-5}$ away at its default tolerance. On a MILP, cuOpt's answer is a plan within the gap, like any solver's. Read the tolerance with the status, record it in the run's manifest, and check the plan's feasibility and objective on the CPU before anyone relies on it.

Summary


  • The search. The LP relaxation bounds every plan; branch-and-bound splits on a fractional variable and prunes nodes that are infeasible, integral or cannot beat the incumbent. The optimum lies between the incumbent and the best bound, and OPTIMAL means within the run's tolerances, a gap that is an amount of money.
  • The relative gap's denominator is the solver's choice: HiGHS divides by the incumbent, SCIP by the smaller of the two numbers in size. A max of $10^{-10}$ and the incumbent is one convention, not the definition, and near zero only the absolute gap means much.
  • The API. MathOpt for general models and LP duals, CP-SAT for intervals and dense integer logic, and the termination reason read before any value. A limit without a plan is unknown, not infeasible.
  • Traps. CP-SAT's Python API adds assumptions to the model and reports a sufficient set, which is not promised minimal. MathOpt has compute_infeasible_subsystem, which only Gurobi supports and which no runner solver accepts. CP-SAT rounds a continuous variable and still says OPTIMAL. A GPU's PDLP says OPTIMAL at a tolerance, not at an exact vertex.
