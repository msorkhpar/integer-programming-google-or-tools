  14. Cash-flow spreading

GridCap finance reviews last year's plan and finds that 2031 overran its budget by $3.47M. The model said the plan fit. It did fit the model: whoever built it charged each project's whole cost to the year it starts. A three-year tower set repair does not spend its money in year one. This lesson spreads each project's cost over the years it is worked on, in three rules.

A Project's Cost Lands in the Years It Is Worked On

P022, a tower set repair in the small portfolio, takes three years. Its cost profile is what it spends in each year of work, and the start year shifts the profile along the calendar:

So the money P022 spends in calendar year $y$ depends on which start variable is 1:

In general, with $c_{i,k}$ the cost of project $i$ in its $k$-th year of work (counting from 0):

Each start variable appears in the spend of every year its project is worked on, with the coefficient for that year of work. At most one $x_{i,t}$ per project is 1, so in each year at most one term per project survives: the one for its actual start, if it is being worked on then.

Build Spend as an Expression Once, and Every Budget Rule Reuses It

The sum above is a linear expression in the $x$. Build it once per year and keep it:

The yearly budget is then one row per year, $\text{spend}_y \le B_y$. The same expressions serve every other rule about money: a carry-over (lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps), a regional cap (lesson Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend), a report of planned spend by year (section 9). Writing the triple loop again in each place is how two parts of one model end up disagreeing about what a project costs in 2030. Nothing fails when the copies drift apart: the model still solves, and no solver merges or rejects two rows because they disagree. Each rule simply uses its own idea of spend.

+= on a LinearExpression does not grow it in place. MathOpt's expressions are immutable (the OR-Tools source says so in their docstrings), so spend[year] += ... builds a new, deferred sum (a LinearSum) and stores it under the same key. The program's last lines show the difference:

The dictionary entry gets the new sum, which is all spend_by_year needs. A second name bound to the old expression keeps the old, empty one, so build the expressions first and hand them out afterwards. A plain sum(...) over a generator works as well, and for the large portfolio section 11 compares both against building from DataFrames.

A rule a planner states with an absolute value or a "between" is still linear rows on these expressions: "within 500 of last year" is two rows, one bounding the rise and one bounding the fall, because a solver row compares one linear expression with a number and cannot take $|\cdot|$.

Problem statement

Three counties fund a shared fiber-backbone program, and each county's board approves its own yearly cap. The plan's model must report, for every county and every year, what the plan spends there, so a cap can be written against each number.

Write spend_by_region_year(projects, x, years) in regional.py. It returns a dict with one entry for every (region, year) pair (every region that has a project, every year in years), each a linear expression in the start variables x[(pid, start_year)]. A project's cost_profile[k] is what it spends in its k-th year of work, counting its start year as year 0. A cost that would fall in a year outside years belongs to no entry.

The tests fix a schedule and evaluate your expressions, so any correct expression passes. About eight lines in total.

In the lesson's notation, with $R$ the regions, $P_r$ the projects in region $r$, $T_i$ the start years of project $i$, $c_{i,k}$ its cost_profile[k] and $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ the start variables, you are writing the expression $\text{spend}_{r,y}$ for every $r \in R$ and $y \in Y$. Write it as a sum first (which pairs $(i, t)$ put money into year $y$?), then code it. A cap would then be the row $\text{spend}_{r,y} \le \text{cap}_{r,y}$.

Charging Everything to the Start Year Breaks the Budget the Plan Claims to Respect


Here is the shortcut finance caught, and the spread model, both solved on the small portfolio. Each plan is then checked against the budgets using what it really spends each year:






The lump model reports $656k more NPV, and that number is the cost of the shortcut being wrong: it buys more work than the budget allows, by $1.5M in 2030 and $3.47M in 2031. The overrun lands at the end of the plan because that is where long projects started in earlier years keep spending. The lump model thinks that money was paid in the start year, so it fills those later years with new starts as well. Nothing about value or discounting causes it: the overrun is spending the model never counted. The budgets do not cause it either: the spread model meets the very same budgets in every year.


Stepping back: a model is only as truthful as its accounting. The solver respected every constraint it was given; the constraint described a world in which projects pay up front.

Problem statement


A water utility relines its old mains with one contractor, who has asked for a steady workload: the crews and the lining plant cost money to stand up and stand down. The contract says that from one year of the plan to the next, the utility's total relining spend may rise or fall by at most max_step. The model in steady.py already has the start variables, a row saying each project starts at most once, and yearly_spend, which builds each year's spend as an expression.


Write add_steady_spend(model, spend, years, max_step), adding the rows that keep this rule. spend[y] is year y's expression and years are the plan's years in order. Spend before the first year or after the last is not part of the rule.


The model so far, with $P$ the projects, $T_i$ the years project $i$ may start in, $Y$ the plan's years, $c_{i,k}$ what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work and $S$ the step:



Write your rows in this notation first, each with its quantifier and a label, then code them. A solver row is linear: it cannot take an absolute value, so think about what "rise or fall by at most $S$" says as plain inequalities.


The tests fix a schedule by setting the variables' bounds and ask the solver whether it is allowed, so any correct formulation passes. About four lines.

The Model


The model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, whole, with this lesson's budget row marked: the spend of year $y$ sums each start variable times the slice of its project's cost that lands in $y$.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use (its window, cut so the work ends inside $Y$); $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.


Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work, $k = 0, 1, \dots$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. No other variable exists, so no other start is possible.


Objective.



Constraints.



Summary


  • A project's cost lands in the years it is worked on. The spend in year $y$ is a sum over start years $t \le y$ of $c_{i,y-t}\, x_{i,t}$.
  • Build spend as an expression once, and every budget rule reuses it. One definition keeps every rule about money consistent.
  • Charging everything to the start year breaks the budget the plan claims to respect. On GridCap small it overstates NPV by $656k and overspends 2031 by $3.47M.

Further reading: MathOpt expressions, variables.py (OR-Tools source) (LinearExpression and LinearSum are immutable, and sum(iterable) builds a nest of deferred sums); MathOpt sample facility_location.py (OR-Tools) (an objective grown term by term from an empty mathopt.LinearExpression() with +=, the pattern of spend_by_year); Integer Programming, Chapter 9 of Applied Mathematical Programming (MIT 15.053) (section 9.1: cash-flow rows, one per period, whose coefficient for an investment is its net cash flow in that period, the $c_{i,y-t}$ of this lesson).


Challenges


  1. Projects in the medium portfolio last up to three years. How many nonzero coefficients does the 2031 budget row have in the small and in the medium model?
  2. Some utilities pay a contractor 10% on award and the rest on completion. Write the cost profile.
  3. Should spend be escalated for inflation before or after spreading? Section 7.2 has the answer; guess first.


Design Note: Spend Is Data Times Decisions, Not a Decision


You could add a continuous variable $s_y$ for each year's spend and a constraint $s_y = \sum \ldots$. It reads well in a solver log, and it gives every report one named number. It also adds a variable and a row per year for nothing the expression does not already say. This course keeps spend as an expression and adds a variable only where the model must choose an amount, as the carry-over in lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps does.
