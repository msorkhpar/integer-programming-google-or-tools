GridCap's finance team has good news and a condition. Money left unspent at the end of a year may roll into the next one, but the regulator caps how much: at most 10% of a year's budget. The rate case also caps total spend in 2030 and 2031 together. The model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years treats every year's budget as a wall. This lesson adds the two rules, one trap to avoid while writing them, and a third rule: the money comes from separate pots, each with its own budget.

Carry-Over Is a Variable Linking Each Year to the Next

Unspent money is an amount the plan chooses, so it gets a continuous variable: $r_y \ge 0$, the money carried out of year $y$. Each year may spend its budget plus what came in, less what it carries out:

The budget rows of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years are deleted and replaced by these cash rows, one per year. With every $r_y = 0$ they are the old rows again, so carry-over can only add plans:

Rolling money forward is worth $1,889k of NPV on the small portfolio, because a project whose big spending year collides with a tight budget can now be paid from an earlier year's leftovers. The carried amounts are one of many equally good choices; another solver run may move money between years and keep the same NPV, so never report them as "the" carry-over without fixing a rule for ties (section 10).

Unlimited carry-over is the same as a cumulative budget: spend up to year $y$ is at most the budgets up to year $y$. Money only moves forward, never back.

A Cap on Carry-Over and a Cap on a Window Are More Rows on the Same Expressions

The regulator's 10% rule bounds each carry variable, and the rate case bounds a sum of two years' spend, written with the same spend expressions:

The carry cap is a row here so that it has a name, carry_cap_2029, to find when a scenario misbehaves. Written as the variable's upper bound, add_variable(lb=0, ub=max_share * budget), it allows exactly the same plans; the formulation below writes it as the bounds $0 \le r_y \le \alpha B_y$, which say the same thing.

The 10% cap keeps $183k of the $1,889k gain. The window cap, 90% of the two years' budgets, then costs more than the carry-over gave: the plan ends below the no-carry-over plan. That is a normal result, and the kind a planner needs to see as a table of scenarios rather than as one number, which is what section 12's scenario workflow produces.

Unspent Money Lapses, So the Cash Row Is an Inequality


The trap is writing the cash row as an equation: "what came in is spent or carried". With a cap on carry-over, an equation forces the plan to spend at least 90% of each year's money, whether there is anything worth funding or not. Under the window cap that cannot be done:



The model is not wrong about the money; it is wrong about the world. Budget that is neither spent nor carried returns to the utility at year end, so the row is $\le$, and the gap between the two sides is the lapsed amount. An "infeasible" answer from a model that has a perfectly good plan is one of the most common bugs in this kind of work. Section 9 shows how to find which rows are responsible; here, the habit is to ask of every equality "does the world really force this?".

Problem statement


A water utility's board approves a new rule for its capital fund. A year may spend some of the next year's budget early, up to a share max_advance of that next year's budget, and whatever it spends early is no longer there to spend in the year it came from. A year may do both in one plan: lose part of its budget to the year before and spend early from the year after. The plan's last year has no next year to draw on. Money a year neither spends nor sends anywhere still lapses at year end. The model in advance.py still holds every year to its own budget, so it never uses the new rule.

Each Pot of Money Gets Its Own Row per Year, and the Pooled Total Cannot Stand In for It


GridCap's finance team has one more condition. The money does not come from one pot. Capital work is paid from the CapEx budget, maintenance work from the OpEx (operating) budget, and the regulator has set up a dedicated resilience fund for undergrounding. Each pot has its own amount each year, and money in one pot cannot pay for work charged to another. So a plan has to fit every pot in every year, not only the total.


The rule needs two new pieces of data. A project's cost profile is split by pot: $c_{i,k,\tau}$ is what project $i$ draws from pot $k$ in its $\tau$-th year of work, and the pots' shares add up to the project's cost, $\sum_k c_{i,k,\tau} = c_{i,\tau}$. Each pot has a budget per year, $B_{k,y}$. The row for pot $k$ in year $y$ is the spend row of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years with the pot's profile in place of the whole one:



GridCap's data has no pots, so the program categories.py invents a split for this lesson (GridCap itself is fictional): vegetation work is all OpEx, a repair is 40% OpEx, the resilience fund pays half of every undergrounding project, and everything else is CapEx. Each year's budget is split 65% CapEx, 25% OpEx and 10% fund, so the three pots add up to the year's budget $B_y$:






P039 works for two years, so it has a profile per pot over both: it draws $580k and then $440k from the fund. The pot rows are added to the timing model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years, one per pot and year, each built from that pot's spend expressions (draw_by_year is spend_by_year with the pot's profile):




The pooled rows $\text{spend}_y \le B_y$ stay in the model, but they can no longer refuse anything: the pots add up to $B_y$, so a plan that fits every pot fits the total. The reverse is false. The optimum of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years, solved with one pooled budget, fits every budget_y row and breaks seven pot rows:



In 2027, for example, the pooled plan spends $2,990k of maintenance work against an OpEx pot of $2,220k. The total has room, but that room is CapEx money, which cannot pay for maintenance. With the pot rows the best plan is worth $20,613k, $3,647k less. A pooled budget is a relaxation of the pots: it allows every plan the pots allow and more, so its NPV is never lower, and a model that pools money the utility keeps apart reports value it cannot deliver, as the lump model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years did.


Which pot row costs the most? In a linear program a row binds when it holds with equality at the optimum. In an integer program that test misleads, because a row can have slack and still keep out a whole project. The direct test is to leave one row out, solve again, and see what the NPV gains:



The fund's 2027 row costs the most, and it has $310k of slack at the optimum. The reason is P034, an undergrounding project that may start only in 2027: half its cost, $1,060k, falls on the fund, whose 2027 budget is $890k. With that row in the model P034 can never be funded, however the rest of the plan is arranged. Without it P034 starts and the NPV rises by $1,682k, less than P034's own $2,123k because P034 also draws $1,070k from 2027's CapEx pot, and the rest of the plan gives way. The table above shows the four costliest rows of the fifteen; each gain is measured with every other row in place, so the gains do not add up.


The program solves the pots without carry-over or the window cap ($\alpha = 0$, no $W$), so the cash rows are the budget rows of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years and only the pots differ. The model below keeps both rules together. One consequence follows from the rows: when the pots add up to $B_y$ and none of them carries money, the pooled carry-over $r_y$ buys nothing, because the pot rows already hold each year's spend to $B_y$. A utility whose pots do carry over gives each pot its own carry variable $r_{k,y}$ and its own cash row (Challenge 4).


The Model


The model with carry-over and budget pots, whole. New are the parameters $\alpha$ (the share of a year's budget that may roll over), $W$ and $C_W$ (the capped window and its cap), the continuous variables $r_y \ge 0$, the money carried out of year $y$ (with $r_{y_0 - 1} = 0$ before the first year), and the pots $K$ with their profiles $c_{i,k,\tau}$ and budgets $B_{k,y}$. The budget rows are replaced by the marked rows. The year of work is written $\tau$ here, so that $k$ can name a pot.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use (its window, cut so the work ends inside $Y$); $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years; $K$ the budget pots (here CapEx, OpEx and the resilience fund).


Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,\tau}$, what project $i$ spends in its $\tau$-th year of work, $\tau = 0, 1, \dots$ ($k); $c_{i,k,\tau}$, the part of it drawn from pot $k$, with $\sum_{k \in K} c_{i,k,\tau} = c_{i,\tau}$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $B_{k,y}$, the budget of pot $k$ in year $y$, with $\sum_{k \in K} B_{k,y} = B_y$ ($k); $\alpha$, the share of a year's budget that may be carried out of it; $W \subseteq Y$, the capped window of years, and $C_W$, its cap ($k).


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$; and $r_y \ge 0$ for every $y \in Y$, the money carried out of year $y$, continuous, with $r_{y_0 - 1} = 0$ before the first year.


Objective.



Constraints.



In the code the row $\text{pot}_{k,y}$ is named {k}_{y}, as in opex_2027 or fund_2027.


Summary


  • Carry-over is a variable linking each year to the next. Cash rows replace budget rows, and with every carry at zero they are the old model.
  • A cap on carry-over and a cap on a window are more rows on the same expressions. Their cost is visible only by solving each scenario.
  • Unspent money lapses, so the cash row is an inequality. An equation here turns a feasible plan into an infeasible model.
  • Each pot of money gets its own row per year, and the pooled total cannot stand in for it. One pooled budget allows plans the pots refuse; to find which pot row costs the most in an integer program, drop it and solve again, since a row with slack can still keep out a whole project.

Design Note: Spend Stays an Expression, Carry-Over Is a Variable


Spend is fully decided by the start variables, so lesson Cash-flow spreading across years keeps it as an expression. Carry-over is not decided by anything else: the same schedule can carry different amounts, and the model has to choose. That is the test for adding a variable: a variable for something the model must choose, an expression for something it only computes.
