GridCap medium's Ridge region has fourteen large transmission jobs to finish, each needing between 9 and 14 months of one line crew's work, and four line crews to do them. The crews are interchangeable: same skills, same depot, same equipment. Each job goes to one crew, a crew works its jobs one after another, and the planners want every job done as early as possible, so they minimize the month the last crew finishes. It is a small model, 57 variables, and HiGHS with its symmetry handling switched off needs 3,913 nodes to prove its answer. The reason is not the size of the model. It is that every plan appears in it 24 times.

This lesson explains why identical crews (or trucks, or transformers, or any identical assets) make a MILP slow, and four ways to deal with it, each measured on the same crews: rows that order the crews, counting identical jobs instead of naming them, and the solver's own symmetry handling.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. Reading a project's crew_months as the months one crew needs for it, and the four identical crews, are this lesson's own scenario.

The Model

Sets. $J$ the jobs (Ridge's 14 largest transmission projects, in order of crew-months, largest first, ties by project id); $K = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ the crews.

Parameters. $m_j$, the months of work job $j$ needs (14, 14, 13, 13, 13, 13, 12, 12, 11, 11, 10, 10, 10, 9).

Variables. $x_{j,k} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $j \in J$, $k \in K$: job $j$ goes to crew $k$. $0 \le C \le \sum_j m_j$: the makespan, the month by which every crew has finished.

Objective.

Constraints. Write $L_k = \sum_{j \in J} m_j\, x_{j,k}$ for crew $k$'s load, the month it finishes.

where $n(j)$ is job $j$'s position in the list, counting from 0. A model uses at most one of the two optional families; the third rule shows why never both.

Identical Crews Make Every Plan Appear Once per Numbering of the Crews

A symmetry of a model is a way of renaming its variables that turns every allowed point into an allowed point with the same objective value. Here, renumbering the crews is one: if crews 0 and 2 swap all their jobs, every row still holds (each job still has one crew, and the two loads swap places under the same makespan), and the makespan is unchanged. With 4 crews there are $4! = 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24$ numberings, so a plan that uses all four crews with different jobs appears as 24 different points $x$:

To the business these 24 points are one plan: "these four groups of jobs, one group per crew". To the MILP they are 24 different optimal solutions. The same is true of every other plan, good or bad, so the whole space of 0/1 points is 24 copies of the space of real plans.

The question to ask of any model is whether it has identical things that the model names apart: crews, trucks, warehouses, transformers bought in a batch, time slots of the same length. Lesson Multiple knapsack and generalized assignment's design note found that GridCap's own crews differ by region and capacity, so its model there had no such symmetry. These four do not differ in anything the model reads.

Symmetry Costs Nodes Because Each Copy Must Be Ruled Out Separately

The LP relaxation's bound is $165 / 4 = 41.25$ months: it can split every job into fractions and spread the 165 months evenly. The best plan finishes in month 43, so branch-and-bound must prove that no split finishes by month 42 (the loads are whole months, so the makespan is too).

Proving that something is impossible means ruling out every way it could happen. Suppose the search branches on "job P324 goes to crew 0" and, deep in that branch, rules out every split with P324 on crew 0. The other branch, "P324 is not on crew 0", still contains the same splits with P324 on crew 1, 2 or 3, and the LP relaxation cannot tell them apart from the ones just ruled out. They are ruled out again, three more times, and the same happens at every level below. A pruned node prunes one copy, not the plan. With HiGHS's own symmetry handling switched off (the last rule shows the switch), the plain model takes 3,913 nodes:

(the first line of the next rule's table). symmetry_rows.py builds and solves that plain model twice more at its end, and gets the same count each time:

HiGHS repeats its node count when the model, the HiGHS version and the settings are the same, which is what makes the comparisons within this lesson and the next valid. A count does not carry over: lesson Incremental and logarithmic formulations warned that it depends on the instance, the solver and its version, even on the order of the rows, so another OR-Tools release, or the same rows written in another order, may give a different number.

An Ordering Row Keeps at Least One Copy of Every Plan and Removes the Rest

The cure is to choose one numbering of the crews in advance and add rows that refuse every other. Such symmetry-breaking rows are not valid inequalities in the sense of lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities: they do cut off allowed plans, the other numberings. What they must keep is at least one copy of every plan, so that the optimum value cannot change. Two classic choices:

Order by load. Number the crews from busiest to least busy: $L_0 \ge L_1 \ge L_2 \ge L_3$. Any plan can be renumbered this way (sort its crews by load), so every plan keeps at least one copy. When two crews have equal loads, both orders survive, so a plan can keep more than one copy.

Order by first job. Number the crews by the first job (in the list $J$) each one holds. Then crew 0 holds job 0; crew 1's first job comes after crew 0's; and so on. A consequence that is easy to write as rows: job number $n$ can only be on crews $0, \dots, n$, because the crews before its own each hold an earlier job. So

Any plan can be renumbered by first job, and that copy satisfies these rows, so again every plan keeps a copy. The rows do not refuse every other copy: they only restrict jobs 0, 1 and 2, the jobs whose position is below the last crew's number, so some other numberings pass too. What they do refuse, they refuse by fixing variables, the cheapest kind of row there is.

Never both. Each family picks a numbering, and they pick by different rules. Take a plan whose busiest crew does not hold job 0, like the one above: P324, job 0, is on a crew with 40 months, and the busiest crew has 43. The first-job rows put P324's crew first; the load rows put the 43-month crew first. No numbering does both, so that plan loses every copy. If it was the only optimal plan, the optimum changes. Two symmetry-breaking families are safe together only when one numbering satisfies both, which must be proved, not hoped. Nor does the second family buy a smaller search for sure: removing copies is not a promise of fewer nodes, as the table below shows, and the extra rows cannot loosen the LP bound either, since a row can only shrink the LP region. The one thing the combination adds for certain is the risk of losing the optimum.

All three models on Ridge, with HiGHS's symmetry detection off and on:

The makespan is 43 in every row: no copy of the best plan was lost. With detection off, the load rows cut the search from 3,913 nodes to 791 and the first-job rows to 688.

Problem statement

A storm-response team (not GridCap) rents identical mobile units for the season. Each outage site needs one unit for a number of days, a unit serves its sites one after another, and each unit is rented for 30 days. build in fleet.py already has the model: $x_{s,k} = 1$ if site $s$ is served by unit $k$, $y_k = 1$ if unit $k$ is rented, and

Renumbering the units gives the same plan, so every plan appears many times. Write add_symmetry_breaking(fleet, days, units): rows that refuse every numbering that breaks one of these two rules:

  • the rented units are the first ones (no unit is rented while a lower-numbered one is not), and
  • the sites, taken in the order the dict days lists them (its key order, as given, not sorted by their number of days), fill the units in order: site number $n$ (from 0) may only use units $0, \dots, n$. fleet.py's own 14 sites happen to be listed longest first; one test's sites are not.

Unlike the lesson's crews, here a unit can stay unrented, so decide what the first rule means as a row on the $y$. Before coding, write both families with their labels and argue on paper that every plan can be renumbered to satisfy both at once, so the fewest units cannot change. Check the second family on the first site: which units may it use?

Then run fleet.py: it solves the same 14 sites with and without your rows and prints HiGHS's node counts. Write down both. The lesson found that ordering rows can cost nodes as well as save them; see which happens here.

The tests fix whole plans and ask whether each is allowed, so any rows that keep this numbering pass. About six lines.

Identical Jobs Are Symmetric Too: Count Them Instead of Naming Them


The crews are not the only identical things here. Four jobs need 13 months, and nothing else about them matters to this model. Swapping two of them between crews changes $x$ and changes nothing else, another symmetry. Ordering rows could break it too, but there is a more direct remedy: stop naming the identical things. Group the jobs by length, let $a_s$ be the number of jobs of $s$ months, and decide only how many of each length each crew gets:



with $S$ the set of lengths. A plan of this model, "crew 0 gets two 13-month jobs and one 14-month job", stands for every assignment of named jobs with those counts; turning it back into named jobs is a matter of handing them out in any order. The model never sees the difference between two 13-month jobs, so it cannot explore both versions.




Ordering rows and the solver's symmetry detection both keep every named variable, so neither makes the model smaller; only counting does. The count has to be a number, not a yes/no: a binary per period saying whether any identical job runs then would lose how many run, which the crew limit and the cost depend on. Counting removes the jobs' symmetry and shrinks the model from 57 variables to 25, and the search from 3,913 nodes to 178, with no ordering rows at all. The crews are still identical in the counted model, yet adding load-order rows to it changed little (182 nodes), and switching HiGHS's detection on changed nothing. This is the strongest form of symmetry breaking when it applies: if a group of things is truly identical, give the model a count, not a list of names. It applies when the identical things are the decisions' subjects (which jobs, how many poles); the crews here receive work, and their loads are what the objective reads, so they keep their numbers.

Problem statement


A distribution company (not GridCap) must replace a feeder's wooden poles within four years. The poles are identical: same age, same type, same risk. Replacing one in year $y$ costs $c_y$; at the end of each year, every pole not yet replaced adds a risk cost $r_y$; and the crew can replace at most $K_y$ poles in year $y$. Every pole must be replaced by the end of the last year.


A first draft, build_named in poles.py, gives each pole a name: $x_{p,y} = 1$ if pole $p$ is replaced in year $y$. With 30 poles and four years that is 120 binaries, and every plan appears once for each way of handing its yearly numbers to the 30 named poles.


Write build_counted(poles, cost, risk, crew): the same plans, with the poles counted instead of named. It returns (model, n), where n maps each year to its one variable. Before coding, write the formulation: each variable's domain and bounds, the row that says every pole is replaced, and the objective, in which the number of poles still waiting at the end of year $y$ is an expression in your variables. Check on paper that a plan of the named model and its counts have the same cost.


The tests fix the counts of whole plans and ask whether each is allowed and what it costs, and compare your best plan with the named model's. About six lines.

Solvers Break Symmetry Too, So Measure Your Rows Against Theirs


MILP solvers look for symmetry themselves. HiGHS has an option mip_detect_symmetry, on by default, and MathOpt passes HiGHS options through SolveParameters(highs=...):



CP-SAT has its own parameter for the same purpose, symmetry_level (default 2: detect symmetries in presolve and also break some during the search, according to its parameter file). The table of the previous rule already showed the effect on Ridge: detection alone took the plain model from 3,913 nodes to 305, and with ordering rows on top it went lower still (196 and 139).


One instance is one data point. The same six setups on twelve instances, the four regions' largest transmission jobs with three, four and five crews:



"Plain" is the first line: no ordering rows, detection off. Three things to read:


  • The solver's detection did the most for the least effort. On its own it cut the total from 10,334 nodes to 1,175 and never made an instance worse.
  • First-job rows helped more than load rows, with detection off (2,351 against 5,875) and with it on (645 against 1,757). First-job rows only fix variables; load rows add rows over all of the crews' variables.
  • Rows can hurt. Load rows took more nodes than the plain model on 4 of the 12 instances with detection off, and on 2 with detection on. A row changes the LP at every node, and with it the path the search takes; nothing guarantees that the new path is shorter.


So the rule is the course's usual one: try the solver's own handling first, add ordering rows that fix variables if the model is still slow, prove that every plan keeps a copy, and measure on several instances, not one.


Summary


  • Identical crews make every plan appear once per numbering of the crews: 24 copies of each plan with 4 crews, all with the same makespan.
  • Symmetry costs nodes because each copy must be ruled out separately. The plain Ridge model took 3,913 nodes with HiGHS's symmetry detection off.
  • An ordering row keeps at least one copy of every plan and removes the rest. Order by load or by first job, never both; 791 and 688 nodes.
  • Identical jobs are symmetric too: count them instead of naming them. 25 variables instead of 57 and 178 nodes.
  • Solvers break symmetry too, so measure your rows against theirs. Detection alone: 10,334 nodes to 1,175 on twelve instances; first-job rows with detection: 645; load rows sometimes hurt.

Design Note: Break Symmetry Where It Is Born


The cheapest symmetry is the one never written. Before adding ordering rows, ask whether the model needs the names at all: identical jobs became counts here, and identical crews could become a count in a model where only the number of crews used matters. When the names are needed, put the ordering rows in the builder next to the variables they order (add_order here), with the argument that every plan keeps a copy written as its comment, so the next person who adds a family of rows can check that the two agree.
