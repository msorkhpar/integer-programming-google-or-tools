GridCap's planning team inherits a model of the medium portfolio (400 projects over ten years) from an analyst who has moved on. It is correct: it allows exactly the plans the business rules allow. It also uses two forms section 6 has called weak, the aggregated work-front row of lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound and the aggregated, big-M precedence row of lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms. HiGHS needs 3,856 nodes to solve it to its default gap. The team's lead asks the question every inherited model raises: "Can we make this solve faster by writing it better?"

This lesson answers it the way a careful engineer would, in five rules: measure first, let the LP point show the weak rows, try each lever of this module and record what it finds, then measure the solver again, and only then decide what to keep. The answer turns out to be more interesting than "yes".

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. The work fronts and their fees are lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound's own addition.

The Model

The model of lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound on GridCap medium: 400 projects, 10 years, 12 work fronts, 39 must-do projects, 18 parent-child pairs and 18 alternative groups. Its two inherited rows, marked, are the weak forms; the reformulated model replaces them with the forms below them.

Sets. $P$, $Y$, $T_i$, $M$, $\mathcal{G}$, $C$ with $p(c)$ and $L_c$, $F$ and $P_f$, as in lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, $c_{i,k}$, $B_y$ and the fees $\phi_f$ ($k), as in lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$ (887 of them); $y_f \in \{0, 1\}$ for $f \in F$ (12). With $s_i = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$.

Objective.

Constraints.

with $M_c = \max T_{p(c)} + L_c$, the smallest valid constant (lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms).

Measure Before You Reformulate: Size, LP Bound and Root Bound

A reformulation is a change made to improve a number, so the numbers come first. Lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound named three that a formulation decides or strongly influences, and the audit records all three for the model as inherited and after each change, one change at a time:

  • size: variables and rows;
  • LP bound: the formulation's own bound, solved with GLOP;
  • root bound: HiGHS's bound after presolve and its root cuts, read with node_limit=1.

The fourth number, the solver's search, is the expensive one, and it comes last (the fourth rule). The order is deliberate: each line costs seconds, and it already tells you where the formulation differs from what the solver works with.

The LP Point Shows Where a Formulation Is Weak

The inherited LP bound, $190,640.68k, is far above every other number in the table. Where does the excess come from? The LP's optimal point says so: look for variables that should be 0 or 1 and are not.

All twelve fronts sit between 0.38 and 0.64, while only 13 of the 887 start variables are fractional. The fractions come from the rows, not the costs: the objective only picks which allowed point is best, and only the rows decide which fractional points are allowed. That is the signature lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound explained: with the aggregated row, a front mobilized at 0.5 can run up to half its projects in full while paying half its fee. The remedy is the disaggregated row, one per project, and the second line of the table shows its effect: the LP bound falls by $31,043k, to $159,597.31k.

The precedence rows are the other inherited weakness. Lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms found the cumulative form never weaker, and tighter in some scenarios. On GridCap medium it changes nothing: the third line's LP bound is the second's to the cent. With five more rows. A change that does not move the bound here can still be worth keeping (its rows need no big-M, and they read as plain English), but it is not a speed-up, and the audit says so.

How much of the inherited gap did the reformulation close? The best plan is worth about $159,435k (the fourth rule's solve), so by lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound's measure $(190{,}640.68 - 159{,}597.31) / (190{,}640.68 - 159{,}435) \approx 99.5\%$. Almost the whole distance from the inherited LP bound to the best plan was the front rows.

Problem statement

A transmission operator (not GridCap) keeps spare parts in depots. Each substation draws $d_i$ parts a year and is served by exactly one open depot; depot $j$ holds at most $U_j$ parts and costs $\phi_j$ a year to keep open; serving substation $i$ from depot $j$ costs $h_{i,j}$ a year in hauling. build in depots.py has the variables ($x_{i,j} \in \{0,1\}$: $i$ is served by $j$; $y_j \in \{0,1\}$: $j$ is open), the "served once" rows and the objective

The model you inherit (add_inherited_rules) states the rules "only an open depot serves" and "a depot holds at most its capacity" as

It allows exactly the right plans, and its LP bound is $42.13k against a best plan of $77k.

Audit it as the lesson did: solve its LP relaxation and look at the $y_j$. Then write add_rules(model, x, y, d) with the same two rules in their strongest form. Write your rows with labels and quantifiers first, and for each one show on paper (a) that with whole numbers it allows the same plans as the colleague's, and (b) which fractional point of the colleague's LP it refuses.

The tests fix whole plans and ask whether each is allowed, and compare your LP bound with what the strongest rows give. Since this model minimizes, a higher LP bound is a tighter one. About six lines.

Try Each Lever, and Record What It Finds, Even Nothing


A case study is also a checklist. The other levers of this module have nothing to do with the front rows, so each is run on the medium model as it is, and its finding is recorded:


  • Bound tightening (lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities): starts that no plan can use, from the budget rows and the must-dos' floors.
  • Valid inequalities (lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities): pairs of starts that no plan can use together and that no row already says, the raw material of clique rows.
  • Symmetry (lesson Symmetry and how to break it): projects identical in every number the model reads, which would make plans appear several times.



Nothing, three times. That is a real finding, not a failed step. GridCap medium's yearly budgets are $34,000k to $43,000k against projects of at most a few thousand, so no single start and no pair of starts can overflow a year (the room argument of lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities needs a project that is large next to the row); and no two of medium's projects are identical. On GridCap small with hard crew rows (lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities), the same code found eight fixings and 70 new conflicts, because crew limits of 2 to 34 crew-months are small next to the projects. The levers depend on the data's shape, which is why the audit runs them rather than assuming.


The Solver's Own Work Decides Whether a Reformulation Solves Faster


Now the measurement that answers the lead's question: solve both models with HiGHS, as the team would in production, to HiGHS's default relative gap of $10^{-4}$ (0.01%):






The reformulated model did not solve faster. It took 7,823 nodes against the inherited model's 3,856. The audit table had already given the reason it could not be much faster: the root bounds were equal, $159,535k and $159,536k. Whatever the inherited front rows lost, HiGHS's presolve and root cuts had recovered before the first branch, exactly as lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound measured on GridCap small. From there the two searches start level and go different ways, and the inherited one happened to be shorter. The counts are not noise: HiGHS's node counts repeat from run to run on the same model (the module's tests solve twice and compare), so one measurement of each is enough here.


The two plans differ in 14 starts and by $1k in value. Both runs stopped as soon as their plan was proved within 0.01% of the best possible, which is what OPTIMAL means under a gap tolerance (lesson What "optimal at 0.01%" means on a $2B portfolio); a gap of $15k on a portfolio of $159,000k is inside it. Since both models allow exactly the same plans, either plan is valid in either model.

Problem statement


A modeling team wants the audit of the lesson as a reusable function, so that every reformulation proposal arrives with the same table. Each formulation is given as a function build(integer) that returns the model as a MILP (integer=True) or as its LP relaxation (integer=False); the first one given is the model as inherited.


Write audit(variants, optimum) in audit.py. It returns one dictionary per formulation, in the order given, with the keys


  • name, variables and rows: the formulation's name and size;
  • lp: its LP bound (GLOP is fine);
  • root: HiGHS's bound after its root node, read the way the lesson read it;
  • closed: the share of the inherited formulation's gap to optimum that this formulation's LP bound closes. If the inherited LP bound already equals optimum, there is no gap to close: the reference then reports closed as 1.0 on every line, instead of dividing by zero.


Before coding, write the formula for closed with the inherited LP bound, this formulation's LP bound and the optimum, and work out its value for the inherited line itself and for a formulation whose LP bound equals the optimum.


The tests audit three formulations of a three-item rule, small enough to check every number by hand. About fifteen lines.

Keep the Strong Form for What It Is Worth, and Say What It Is Not


So what does the team keep? The audit gives an answer with its reasons, which is what a lead can act on:


  • Keep the disaggregated fronts. The model's own LP bound moves from $31,206k above the best plan to $162k above it. That bound is the number the team can report without trusting a solver's internal cuts; it holds for any solver or version; and any method of section 11 that works with the LP relaxation directly (decomposition, rounding heuristics, the LP bounds of a rolling horizon) inherits it. It costs 388 rows.
  • Keep the cumulative precedence, for its readability and its lack of a big-M, and record that on this data it changes no bound.
  • Do not claim a speed-up. On this model and this solver it did not happen. A reformulation's effect on run time is measured, per solver, on the data that matters, and HiGHS's own presolve and cuts are part of what is being measured.
  • Look elsewhere for speed. The root bound sits about $100k above the best plan, and the levers of this module found nothing more to cut there: no fixings, no conflicts, no symmetry. Section 11 takes it from there, with warm starts, parameter tuning and decomposition.


The lesson for the next inherited model is the order of the work: measure size, LP bound and root bound; let the LP point point at weak rows; run every lever and record what it finds; measure the solve; and report the result you got, including when it is not the one you expected.


Summary


  • Measure before you reformulate: size, LP bound and root bound. Each costs seconds and is recorded for every step.
  • The LP point shows where a formulation is weak. All twelve fronts fractional pointed at the front rows; disaggregating them closed about 99.5% of the inherited LP gap.
  • Try each lever, and record what it finds, even nothing. No fixings, no conflicts and no identical projects on GridCap medium, because its budgets are large next to its projects.
  • The solver's own work decides whether a reformulation solves faster. Equal root bounds; 3,856 nodes inherited against 7,823 reformulated.
  • Keep the strong form for what it is worth, and say what it is not. A bound that does not depend on the solver, at a cost of 388 rows; not a speed-up here.

Design Note: Write the Audit as a Program


Every number in this lesson came from two short programs that build each variant of the model from one builder with switches (fronts=, precedence=). That makes the audit repeatable: when the data change, or the solver is upgraded, the same programs say whether the advice still holds. An audit that lives in a slide deck says what was true once.
