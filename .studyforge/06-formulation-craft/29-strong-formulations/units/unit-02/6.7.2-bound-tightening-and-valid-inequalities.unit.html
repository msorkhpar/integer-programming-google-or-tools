A contractor freeze reaches GridCap: next plan, every project on the small portfolio must be done by in-house crews. Lesson Deriving big-M from bounds let a program hire a contractor when its own crews ran out; now each program's crew-months per year are a hard limit, a row like any budget row. The planners rebuild the model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows with those crew rows and get a plan worth $22,741k, with an LP bound of $25,262.67k above it.

Lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound compared two formulations someone had already written. This lesson starts from one formulation and makes it stronger without changing a single plan it allows, using only facts that its own rows imply. There are two kinds of such facts. A bound fact says a variable can take fewer values than its domain allows (here: a start year no plan can use, fixed at 0). A valid inequality is a new row that every allowed plan already satisfies (lesson Cover inequalities that tighten budget rows), here a row that says "at most one of these starts". Lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first showed how presolve derives facts of the first kind from one row at a time, and lessons At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k and Cover inequalities that tighten budget rows wrote "at most one" rows and cover rows. This lesson derives both kinds from the whole model, in four rules, and checks every fact it adds.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. Reading crew_months as the crew-months a project needs in every year it is worked on is the reading of lessons Deriving big-M from bounds to Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull.

A Row Leaves Each Variable Only What the Others Must Use

Take one capacity row, $\sum_v a_v\, x_v \le b$ with every $a_v \ge 0$, and one start $x_u$ in it. If $x_u = 1$, the row must hold its $a_u$ plus whatever the other variables are forced to use. Lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first called that forced part the row's least activity, computed from the bounds. In this model the bounds alone force nothing (every $x$ may be 0), but the must-do rows do: a must-do project starts in one of its years, so it uses at least the smallest of its starts' uses of the row. Call the total over the must-dos the row's floor, and $b$ minus the floor its room:

If $a_u$ is more than the room, no allowed plan can set $x_u = 1$: the bound of $x_u$ tightens from $[0, 1]$ to $[0, 0]$. For a must-do's own start, its floor is part of the room already, so it is given back before the comparison:

One fixing can lead to another. Fixing a must-do's start removes it from the must-do's choices, so the must-do's smallest use of some other row can rise, that row's room falls, and more starts may no longer fit. So the search runs in rounds, each using the fixings of the rounds before, until a round finds nothing. This repeated use of bounds to tighten other bounds is called bound propagation. On GridCap small, with budget and crew rows:

Vegetation has 2 crew-months in 2031, and the must-do P003, whose only start is 2031, takes 1 of them. So any start that needs 2 or more vegetation crew-months in 2031 cannot be used, including the must-do P010's: P010 must therefore start in 2030, its other year. P008, P012 and P018 would not fit even with no must-do at all. One round found all eight here; a second round looked at the rows again with P010 pinned to 2030 and found nothing new.

The MILP optimum does not move, as it cannot: only starts no plan could use were removed. The LP bound falls by $92.16k, because the relaxation had been using fractions of those starts. Fixing a variable is the strongest bound tightening there is, and it shrinks the model too.

Problem statement

A regulator (not GridCap) chooses which field inspections to run next quarter. Inspection $j$ costs $c_j$ and gives $h_{d,j}$ hours of inspection to district $d$; each district must get at least $r_d$ hours, and the total cost is capped by $B$:

build in inspections.py writes this model and can apply fixings to the variables' bounds.

Before solving, the planners want the facts the rows force on their own: inspections every allowed plan includes (fixed at 1) and inspections no allowed plan includes (fixed at 0). Write tighten(cost, budget, hours, need), returning the pair (ones, zeros) of sets.

The lesson fixed starts at 0 from "$\le$" rows. Here the coverage rows are "$\ge$" rows, so work out on paper what they can force, and how. Use one row at a time: a coverage row, given what is already fixed at 0, and the budget row, given what is already fixed at 1. A fixing found from one row can make another row force something new, so repeat until a whole pass finds nothing. Write the condition for each kind of fixing as an inequality before coding it.

The tests compare your sets with the ones this one-row-at-a-time rule reaches, and check every fixing against all allowed plans, found by brute force. About fifteen lines.

Two Starts That Cannot Both Be Used Are a Conflict, Whatever Row Says So


The same reasoning applies to pairs. Two starts $u$ and $v$ of different projects conflict if together they need more of some row than its room: $a_u + a_v > \text{room}$ (with must-do floors given back, as above). No allowed plan uses both, so $x_u + x_v \le 1$ is a valid inequality. Conflicts also come straight from the model's other rows: two starts of the same project conflict (the once row), and so do starts of two projects in one alternative group (the alt row).



Draw every start as a point and every conflict as a line between two points: that drawing is the conflict graph of the model. On GridCap small there are 123 conflicting pairs, of which 70 are not already stated by a once or alt row.


Each of those 70 could be added as a pair row. Lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k showed why that is a weak way to say "at most one": with a pair row for every two of six projects, its LP set five of them to 0.5, which every pair row allows.


A Clique of Conflicts Is One Valid Row, Stronger Than Its Pairs


A clique of the conflict graph is a set of starts that conflict two by two. For a clique $Q$, any plan uses at most one of its starts, so



is valid. It implies every pair row inside $Q$ (drop the other terms, which are $\ge 0$), and the pair rows do not imply it: the point with every start of a five-start clique at 0.5 satisfies all ten pair rows and gives the clique row 2.5. So the clique row is stronger than its pairs, and it is one row instead of $|Q|(|Q| - 1)/2$.


Lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k had the clique handed to it by the business rule. Here the cliques must be found. Finding the largest clique of a graph is itself a hard problem, so the program grows them greedily: each conflict not yet covered starts a clique, which takes in, in a fixed order, every further start that conflicts with all of its members:




Of the 39 cliques that cover the conflicts, 20 lie inside one project's starts or one alternative group, rows the model already has; 19 are new. The one printed shows why a clique can say more than any row of the model. Its ten pairs conflict for three different reasons: two once rows, the transmission crew row of 2028 (P022, P032 and P036 are all worked in 2028 from those starts, and any two of them need more than 2028's 19 crew-months), and the 2028 budget row. No single row of the model says "at most one of these five starts"; the clique row does.


The bounds line up as the argument says they must: 25,170.51 with fixings alone, 24,719.96 with the 70 pair rows, 24,580.01 with the 19 clique rows, fewer rows and a lower bound. The optimum is $22,741k in all three.


HiGHS's root bound tells a different story: 23,148, 23,107 and 23,187, all within 0.4% of each other and in no order the LP bounds would predict. Whatever HiGHS's presolve and root cuts did with each model, they reached nearly the same bound from all three, so on this model the derived rows told the solver little it did not work out itself. That is the measurement to make before adding derived rows to a production model: they help most where the solver cannot derive them, for example from data the model's rows do not contain, and in a model you solve only as an LP.

A Derived Row Is Trusted Only After It Is Checked Against Whole Plans


A wrong bound or a wrong valid inequality does not raise an error. It removes plans the rules allow, and the solver reports a worse optimum as if it were true (lesson Cover inequalities that tighten budget rows's design note). So every fact derived here is checked twice. On GridCap small, the MILP with all of them added must keep its optimum, which the outputs above show. On GridCap tiny, where every 0/1 point can be listed, each fixing and each clique row must hold for every allowed plan:



The program lists all $2^{13} = 8{,}192$ points, keeps the 15 that satisfy every row, and finds no allowed plan that breaks a derived fact. Then it plants a wrong row, a "conflict" between two starts that some allowed plan uses together, and the same check catches it: 6 allowed plans break it. A check that cannot fail proves nothing, so test the checker with a planted error as well.


Why is the brute force on tiny worth having when the small MILP already kept its optimum? The optimum only tests the plans near the top. A wrong row that cuts off a plan worth less than the best one would leave the optimum unchanged and still be wrong, and it would bite on next year's data.


Summary


  • A row leaves each variable only what the others must use. Capacity less the must-dos' floor is the room; a start that needs more is fixed at 0, and fixings propagate in rounds. Eight starts of GridCap small go, and the LP bound falls from 25,262.67 to 25,170.51.
  • Two starts that cannot both be used are a conflict, whatever row says so. Once rows, alt rows and capacity rows all give conflicts; together they form the conflict graph.
  • A clique of conflicts is one valid row, stronger than its pairs. 19 clique rows give a lower LP bound than 70 pair rows; HiGHS's root bound was within 0.4% across all three models.
  • A derived row is trusted only after it is checked against whole plans. The MILP optimum must stay; on tiny, every allowed plan must satisfy every derived row, and a planted error must be caught.

Design Note: Keep Derived Rows Apart From Business Rules


build writes the business rules; derived_rows.py computes fixings and cliques from them, and the programs add those afterwards, with their own names. Keeping them apart has two uses. A planner who reads the model sees the rules as they were agreed, not a mix of rules and consequences. And when the data change, the derived rows are recomputed from the new data and checked again, rather than being carried over from a run where they were true.
