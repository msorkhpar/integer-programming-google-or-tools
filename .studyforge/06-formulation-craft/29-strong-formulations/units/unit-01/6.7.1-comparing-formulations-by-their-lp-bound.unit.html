GridCap's planners add a cost the model has ignored so far. Before any project of a region's program can start, for example any Ridge transmission project, GridCap must mobilize a work front there: set up a materials yard, bring in a supervisor, sign local contracts. It is paid once for the whole plan, whatever the number of projects the front then runs. Two analysts add the rule to the multi-period model of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows. Both models allow exactly the same plans and both return the same best plan. One of them is far closer to that plan before the solver has branched even once.

Lessons The integrality gap, measured on GridCap, At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k, Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull and Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms each compared two ways of writing one rule. This lesson turns those comparisons into a method you can apply to any pair of formulations, in five rules: what "stronger" means, how to prove it, how to measure it with one number, what the solver itself makes of the difference, and what it costs in size.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. The mobilization fee is this lesson's own addition: 10% of the front's total capex, rounded down to a multiple of $10k.

The Model

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; $F$ the work fronts, one per region and program that has projects (12 on GridCap small); $P_f \subseteq P$ the projects of front $f$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $\phi_f$, the mobilization fee of front $f$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. $y_f \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $f \in F$: front $f$ is mobilized.

Objective. NPV of the projects started, less the fees of the fronts mobilized:

Constraints. The rows of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, then the front rule in one of two forms. Write $s_i = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ for "project $i$ is started" (an expression, not a variable).

A model uses one of the two front rows, never both. The code names the rows front_Ridge_transmission (aggregated) and front_Ridge_transmission_P028 (disaggregated, one per project).

Check both forms on whole numbers before anything else. With $y_f = 0$, the aggregated row reads $\sum s_i \le 0$ and each disaggregated row $s_i \le 0$: no project of the front starts. With $y_f = 1$, the aggregated row reads $\sum s_i \le |P_f|$, which the once rows already guarantee, and each disaggregated row $s_i \le 1$, also guaranteed. So with binary values the two forms allow exactly the same plans, and the two MILPs have the same optimum.

A Formulation Is Judged by the Region Its LP Relaxation Allows

A formulation of a planning problem is one choice of variables and rows whose 0/1 solutions are exactly the plans the rules allow. Call that set of allowed plans $S$. Two formulations of the same problem have the same $S$; what differs is everything else.

Drop the whole-number requirement and each formulation becomes an LP, its LP relaxation (lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails). The points the relaxation allows form a region, written $R_A$ for formulation $A$. Every plan of $S$ lies in $R_A$, plus many fractional points that no plan matches. The relaxation's optimal value is the LP bound: when maximizing, no plan is worth more (lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap).

Formulation $A$ is at least as strong as $B$ when $R_A \subseteq R_B$: every point $A$'s relaxation allows, $B$'s allows too. It is stronger when, in addition, $R_B$ has a point $R_A$ does not. Why the region and not the bound? Because the region decides the bound for every objective at once. Maximizing any objective over a smaller region can never give more than over a larger one that contains it, so

and the reverse inequality when minimizing. A stronger formulation's bound is never looser, whatever the NPVs and fees turn out to be next year.

There is a smallest possible region. Every relaxation contains all the plans of $S$, and with them every weighted average of plans (an LP region has no holes, lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull). The set of all such averages is the convex hull of $S$, written $\text{conv}(S)$. So

A formulation whose relaxation is exactly $\text{conv}(S)$ gives the MILP's optimum as its LP bound, for every objective. Vielma's survey (see Further reading) calls it sharp; it calls a formulation ideal when, on top of that, every corner point of its relaxation (the points a simplex solver returns) is a whole-number plan, so the LP returns a plan, not only its value. Real models are rarely either. The question in practice is which of the formulations you can write sits closer to $\text{conv}(S)$.

GridCap small, both forms, as LP and as MILP:

Same optimum, $17,748k, as the whole-number check promised. The LP bounds are $2,819k apart. Read the Ridge transmission line of the aggregated LP: the front is mobilized at 0.50, yet P028 is started in full. The aggregated row for a front of two projects reads $s_{P007} + s_{P028} \le 2\, y$, and $0 + 1 \le 2 \times 0.5$ holds. So the LP runs a whole project while paying half the fee, $395k instead of $790k. The disaggregated row $s_{P028} \le y$ refuses that point: $1 \le 0.5$ is false. That one point is the proof that the disaggregated region is strictly smaller than the aggregated one; the next rule proves it is contained in it.

The disaggregated LP is not ideal either: it mobilizes Plains transmission at 0.50. Its rows say a front is at least as mobilized as its most-started project, and some Plains project is half started. A stronger formulation removes fractional points; it does not remove them all.

    A bonus for a fully commissioned substation

    • a formulation is stronger when its LP relaxation allows fewer points with the same 0/1 plans
    • one fractional point the weak relaxation allows and the strong one refuses shows the regions differ

Problem statement

A transmission operator (not GridCap) funds upgrade jobs under one budget. Each substation has a list of jobs, and the regulator pays a commissioning bonus for a substation only when every one of its jobs is funded, so that the substation can be put back in service fully upgraded. A job belongs to one substation.

build in bonus.py already has the variables, the budget row and the objective: $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$ (fund job $j$), $z_s \in \{0, 1\}$ (substation $s$ is commissioned and earns its bonus $R_s$), and

A colleague wrote the rule as one row per substation, $|J_s|\, z_s \le \sum_{j \in J_s} x_j$ (add_colleague_rows, given). It allows exactly the right plans, but its LP relaxation lets a substation count as two-thirds commissioned when two of its three jobs are funded, and collect two-thirds of the bonus.

Write add_commissioning(model, x, z, jobs_of): the same rule in the strongest form you can find. Before coding, write your rows with their labels and quantifiers, check on paper that with whole numbers they allow exactly the plans the colleague's row allows, and show that the colleague's row is a sum of yours, so your relaxation's region lies inside the colleague's.

The tests fix whole plans and ask the solver whether each is allowed, so any correct formulation passes them; one more test checks that your LP bound on a budget of 9 is no looser than the best rows give. About three lines.

Containment Is Proved Row by Row: Each Weak Row Is a Sum of Strong Ones


A bound comparison on one objective is evidence. A proof that $R_{\text{strong}} \subseteq R_{\text{weak}}$ settles it for every objective, and for the work fronts it takes one line. Add the disaggregated rows of front $f$, one per project, $s_i - y_f \le 0$:



The sum is exactly the aggregated row. Any point that satisfies every disaggregated row satisfies their sum, so it satisfies the aggregated row; every other row is common to both models. Hence every point of the disaggregated relaxation lies in the aggregated one. This is the general test: if each row of formulation $B$ is a sum of rows of $A$, each multiplied by a nonnegative number, then $R_A \subseteq R_B$ (when both formulations use the same variables). Multiplying a "$\le$" row by a negative number would turn it around, which is why the multipliers must be nonnegative.


The program checks the proof on the models it builds, row by row, and then does what a skeptic would do: it draws 30 other objectives (every NPV scaled by a random factor between 0.5 and 1.5, every fee by one between 0 and 3) and solves both relaxations for each:






Never higher, as the proof says it cannot be, and strictly lower in all 30 here.


When neither region contains the other, the bounds can rank the two formulations either way, depending on the objective. Picture formulation $A$ whose relaxation lets $u$ reach 0.7 and $v$ reach 0.2, and $B$ whose relaxation lets $u$ reach 0.5 and $v$ reach 0.6: on "maximize $u$", $B$ has the tighter bound, and on "maximize $v$", $A$ does. One objective cannot tell you which is stronger in general; a row-by-row proof can, and many objectives that all agree are good evidence when no proof is at hand.

Problem statement


A modeling team keeps several formulations of the same rules and wants a quick, honest report of which is stronger before anyone writes the row-by-row proof. You have, for each formulation, a function that builds its LP relaxation with a given objective, and a list of objectives to try.


Write compare(build_a, build_b, objectives, sense="max") in compare.py. It solves both relaxations for every objective (GLOP is fine; lp_value is given) and returns:


  • "a" if $A$'s bound is at least as tight as $B$'s on every objective and strictly tighter on at least one, and "b" the other way round;
  • "neither" if each is strictly tighter on some objective, which shows that neither region contains the other;
  • "same" if the bounds agree on every objective.


Decide first what "tighter" means for a maximization and for a minimization, using the rule $z_{IP} \le z_{LP}$ (maximizing) and its mirror. Compare with the tolerance TOLERANCE, since a solver's values carry rounding noise.


The tests use small formulations of "at most one" and "at least one" of two decisions, so you can check each answer by hand. About fifteen lines.

The Share of the Gap Closed Puts Two Bounds on One Scale


The $2,819k between the two bounds means little on its own: it depends on the size of the portfolio and of the fees. Lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap measured one formulation by its integrality gap, the distance from its LP bound to the best plan. Two formulations are compared by how much of the weaker one's gap the stronger one removes:




On GridCap small, $(22{,}893.58 - 20{,}074.76) / (22{,}893.58 - 17{,}748) = 54.8\%$, the last line of the output above. A share of 0% means the stronger form's bound is no better; 100% means it reaches the optimum. The same formula serves when minimizing, because both differences change sign together. The measure needs $z_{IP}$, the best plan's value; when the MILP is too hard to solve to the end, use the best plan found so far, which makes the share an estimate.


The Solver's Root Bound Is the Second Measurement


An LP bound is what the formulation says by itself. A MILP solver does not branch on that LP directly: it first presolves the model (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first) and adds cuts of its own (lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand), and only then branches. The bound it holds after that work, at the root node of its search, is a second measurement. MathOpt reads it by stopping HiGHS after one node:



node_limit is the MathOpt parameter that caps the number of nodes of the search; best_objective_bound() is the best bound the solver has proved when it stops (lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count). Both forms on GridCap small:



HiGHS's root bound for the aggregated model, $18,112k, is no worse than for the disaggregated one, $18,127k, although the two LP bounds were $2,819k apart. Since the root bound is the bound after presolve and cuts, those steps recovered at least everything the aggregated rows had lost. The search that follows took 72 nodes with the weak form and 82 with the strong one.


This is a measured fact about one solver on one model, and it carries two lessons. First, a stronger formulation is not a promise of a smaller search: after the solver's own work the two models may start level, and the rest of the search depends on choices the solver makes along the way. Second, the strong form still has value the node count does not show. Its LP bound is the number you can report without running a MILP solver, it does not depend on a solver's cuts (another solver, or another version, may find fewer), and it is the form every later model builds on. Lesson Case study: reformulating GridCap medium makes the same measurements on the medium portfolio.


Strength Has a Price in Size


The disaggregated model has 89 rows against 61: one row per project (40) instead of one per front (12). That price grows with the portfolio: on GridCap medium, with lesson Case study: reformulating GridCap medium's other rows, the two forms have 864 and 476 rows. Rows cost LP time at every node. Vielma's survey opens with exactly this trade-off: a formulation can be too weak or too large, and the best one balances the two. The habit that follows is to measure both columns before choosing: the bound or the share of the gap it closes, and the size.


Sometimes strength costs nothing. A front of one project has the same row in both forms. And a stronger form can let you drop something else: with the disaggregated rows, each $y_f$ is pushed down by its fee and held up by $y_f \ge s_i$, so at a whole-number $x$ the best $y_f$ is already 0 or 1. Those $y_f$ could be declared continuous without changing the optimum; with the aggregated row they could not, since $y_f = (\text{started}) / |P_f|$ would satisfy it with a fraction.


Summary


  • A formulation is judged by the region its LP relaxation allows. Same 0/1 plans, smaller region: stronger, with a bound no looser for any objective. The smallest region is the convex hull of the plans.
  • Containment is proved row by row. If every weak row is a nonnegative sum of strong rows, the strong region lies inside the weak one; the aggregated front row is the sum of its disaggregated rows. Many objectives that agree are evidence; one objective is not.
  • The share of the gap closed puts two bounds on one scale. On GridCap small, 54.8%.
  • The solver's root bound is the second measurement. HiGHS's presolve and cuts brought both forms to about the same root bound, and the weak form's search was not larger.
  • Strength has a price in size. One row per project instead of one per front: 89 rows against 61.

Design Note: Prove the Containment, Then Measure the Rest


Two formulations compared on one GridCap seed tell you about that seed. A row-by-row argument tells you about every seed and every objective, and it is usually short, as the sum above was. So the course's habit is: prove containment when you can, measure the bound and the share of the gap it closes on the data you have, and then measure what the solver does with each form, because that last number is the one that decides run time and the one no proof can give.
