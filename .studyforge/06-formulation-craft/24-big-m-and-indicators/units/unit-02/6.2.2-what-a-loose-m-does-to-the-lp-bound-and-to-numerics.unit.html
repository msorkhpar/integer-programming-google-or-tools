  15. What a loose M does

A colleague reviews the crew model of lesson Deriving big-M from bounds and asks why it computes an $M$ for every row. "Put $10^6$ everywhere. It is bigger than any crew use GridCap will ever have, so it is valid, and the answer is the same." Both halves of that sentence are true for the best plan on GridCap small: $10^6$ is valid, and the MILP returns the same plan. This lesson measures what the colleague's model costs anyway, in four rules: it gives the solver a weaker bound, it makes the search longer, and with a larger $M$ still, it lets the solver return an "optimal" plan that breaks the crew rule.

The Model

The model is lesson Deriving big-M from bounds's, unchanged except for the value of $M_{g,y}$, which this lesson varies. It is restated here in full.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ project $i$'s start years; $P^{\text{must}}$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; $G$ the programs, $P_g$ the projects of program $g$; $A_{g,y}$ the pairs $(i, t)$, $i \in P_g$, $t \in T_i$, with project $i$ started in $t$ still being worked on in year $y$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, project $i$'s spend in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$ the budget ($k); $\kappa_i$ the crew-months project $i$ needs per year of work; $K_{g,y}$ the in-house crew-months; $F = 300$ the contractor fee ($k); $M_{g,y} \ge 0$, the big-M of crew row $(g, y)$, in crew-months.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$; $h_{g,y} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $g \in G$, $y \in Y$. In the LP relaxation both are relaxed to $[0, 1]$.

Objective.

Constraints.

The pieces from lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows to Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps read as in lesson Deriving big-M from bounds: $x_{i,t} = 1$ says project $i$ starts in year $t$; $T_i$ is its window, and a year outside it gets no variable; started(i), the sum $\sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$, is 1 when the project is funded; row after$_{c,t}$ says the child can have started by year $t$ only if its parent had started by year $t - L_c$; and $A_{g,y}$ lists the start choices that keep a crew of program $g$ busy in year $y$. Lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows and Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms, later in this section, treat them in full.

The values of $M_{g,y}$ compared are lesson Deriving big-M from bounds's: $10^6$ for every row, and the four per-row values from variable bounds, once rows, the LP maximum and the MILP maximum.

With a Loose M the LP Hires a Sliver of a Contractor

In the MILP, $h_{g,y}$ is 0 or 1. In the LP relaxation it may be any number in between, and the LP picks the smallest one the crew row allows. If a plan uses $v$ crew-months over the in-house $K_{g,y}$, the row needs

In the LP relaxation, then, the crew rule is a price: $F / M$ for every crew-month over the limit. The real rule charges the whole $300k fee for the first crew-month over. The LP charges $300/M$ per crew-month, and the larger $M$ is, the cheaper breaking the rule looks. Here is one row, transmission in 2029, in the LP with the colleague's $M$ and with the tightest:

With $M = 10^6$, 3.1 crew-months over cost the LP a fee of less than $0.01k. With $M = 26$ the LP pays 300/26, about $11.5k, per crew-month: 3.9 over cost $45.36k. Neither is the real $300k, because a relaxation is always more optimistic than the rule it relaxes, but the tight $M$ keeps the LP's price as close to the rule as a big-M row can.

A Loose M Weakens the LP Bound; the MILP Optimum Does Not Move

Every allowed plan is a point of the LP relaxation, so the LP's optimal value is an upper bound on every plan's value (lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap). The cheaper the relaxation makes it to break a rule, the higher that bound sits above the best plan. Lesson Bounds, incumbents and pruning showed why that matters: branch and bound throws away a part of its search tree only when that part's LP bound falls below the best plan found so far, so a higher bound means less is thrown away.

The whole model, solved as an LP and as a MILP for each choice of $M$:

Four things to read from it:

  • The MILP optimum is the same, $23,341k, for every valid $M$, with the same two contractors. A valid $M$ never changes which plans are allowed, so it cannot change the best one. The colleague was right about that.
  • The LP bound with $M = 10^6$ is exactly the bound of a model with no crew rule and no fee, $26,587.4k. At $0.0003k per crew-month the crew rule has vanished from the relaxation.
  • Each tighter $M$ lowers the bound. The gap between the LP bound and the best plan is $3,246.4k with $10^6$ and $2,993.5k with the tightest $M$: the per-row $M$ closes about 8% of it, on a model where only 15 of the rows are crew rows. Lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms, later in this section, shows the same kind of effect for precedence rows; section 6.7 compares whole formulations this way.
  • The search is longer with the loose $M$ when the solver works on the model as written. The column "presolve off" solves each model with HiGHS's presolve turned off. Presolve is the pass a MILP solver makes over the model before the search, simplifying what it can prove (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first). Without it, $M = 10^6$ takes 662 nodes of branch and bound against 32 for the tightest $M$. With presolve on (the default), the counts are close, from 27 to 33: on this model presolve undid most of the damage the loose $M$ did. You cannot plan on that, as the next rule shows.

Valid $M$s all give the same plan; tight ones give it with a better bound and, measured here, a shorter search when presolve cannot repair the row.

The OR-Tools guide The Bin Packing Problem has a big-M row in it, though it does not use the name. Eleven items weighing 370 in all go into bins of capacity 100, and the goal is the fewest bins. A binary $y_j$ says bin $j$ is used, and the guide writes the bin's row as $\sum_i w_i x_{i,j} \le 100\, y_j$: with $y_j = 0$ the bin holds nothing, with $y_j = 1$ it holds up to its capacity. That is a switched row whose $M$ is the capacity, and by lesson Deriving big-M from bounds's second rule it is the tightest one: the capacity row already caps the load at 100, so no allowed plan needs more. bin_packing_m.py is the guide's model in MathOpt, as written and with the switch split from the capacity row and given a looser $M$: 370 from the bound rule (every item in one bin), and $10^6$:

All three need 4 bins, the answer the guide reports. The LP bounds tell the story of this section in one column. With the guide's row the LP must open $370 / 100 = 3.7$ bins' worth of $y$, a bound that rounds up to the true 4. With $M = 370$ it opens one bin's worth, and with $10^6$ almost nothing: the relaxation has forgotten that bins cost anything. Here the capacity is the right $M$, and the guide's one row states both rules at once.

    Mobile generators and their hookups

    • a loose M raises the LP bound while every valid M gives the same MILP optimum
    • in the LP relaxation a fee-switched rule costs the fee divided by M per unit over the limit

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

During a storm, a utility places mobile generators at critical sites (a hospital feeder, a water plant, a shelter). The fleet has fleet generator-hours in all. Site $s$ is worth a value per generator-hour and can use at most a number of hours (its demand). A site gets generator-hours only if a hookup is built there first, which costs a fixed fee. The planners maximize value less fees.

build(sites, fleet, big_m, integer) in generators.py already creates the hours $g_s \ge 0$, the hookup binaries $h_s$, the demand and fleet rows and the objective. Write three things:

  • the hookup row in build, one per site, switched by $h_s$, using big_m[s];
  • tight_m(sites, fleet): each site's smallest valid $M$, from the rows the model already has (no solve);
  • bounds(sites, fleet): the LP relaxation's value with $M = 10^6$ for every site ("loose"), the LP relaxation's value with tight_m ("tight"), and the MILP optimum with tight_m ("milp"). Use GLOP for the LPs and HiGHS for the MILP.

The model, with $v_s$ the value per hour, $D_s$ the demand, $f_s$ the fee and $H$ the fleet:

Write the hookup row and the formula for $M_s$ in this notation first. Before running anything, predict which of the two LP values is higher, and why.

The tests fix a plan by its variables' bounds and ask the solver whether it is allowed, so any correct row passes; two more tests check tight_m's values and the three numbers bounds returns. About ten lines.

A Huge M Lets the Integrality Tolerance Switch a Rule Off


A MILP solver works in floating-point arithmetic, so it cannot demand that a binary be exactly 0 or 1. It accepts a value within a small integrality tolerance of a whole number as that whole number (lesson Branching on a fractional variable met the same idea). For most variables that is harmless: $x = 0.0000003$ is a project not started. For the switch of a big-M row, it is not harmless, because the row multiplies the switch by $M$. A switch of $3 \times 10^{-7}$ counts as "no contractor", yet gives the row $3 \times 10^{-7} \cdot M$ of extra room: $0.0000078$ crew-months with $M = 26$, 3 crew-months with $M = 10^7$.


Here is the model with presolve off, solved by HiGHS for four values of $M$. For each solution, every row that uses more than the in-house crews while its switch counts as 0 is listed:



With $M = 10^7$ HiGHS reports OPTIMAL with a value of $24,130k. The true best plan is worth $23,341k, so this "optimal" plan is worth more than any plan the rules allow: it hires no contractor and still runs four program-years over their crews, each through a switch of a few times $10^{-7}$. Status, objective and the list of hired contractors all look normal. Only checking the plan against the rule shows the problem.


The line "M = 1e9 with presolve on" solves the same model with HiGHS's default settings, and gets the right plan: on this model, presolve repaired the rows before the search could exploit them. That is luck you cannot plan on. Presolve simplifies what it happens to prove about the model in front of it, and one changed setting was enough here to turn the same model into a wrong "optimal" answer.


The last line is the general warning. Without the loose $M$, the crew rows' coefficients run from 1 to 26; with it, from 1 to $10^9$. A tolerance that is harmless on numbers near 1 becomes a whole crew-month when it is multiplied by $10^9$. Section 10 treats numerics in general; for big-M rows, the rule is short: the smallest valid $M$ is also the safest one.


Check the Plan Against the Rule, Not Against the Solver's Status


A solver's OPTIMAL means "optimal for the model, within my tolerances". It does not mean "the plan obeys the business rules". So a model that uses big-M rows, or any switched row, gets a check after every solve that reads the plan the way a planner would: round every binary to 0 or 1 (> 0.5; lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows reads start variables the same way), then recompute each rule from the data, with no $M$ in it. The loop that printed the rows above is that check for the crew rule:




mathopt.evaluate_expression computes a linear expression's value at the solution, so over is the program's crew use less its in-house crews. A row is broken when its switch rounds to 0 and the use is over the crews by more than a small tolerance. On the runs with a valid, tight $M$ it found nothing; on the $10^7$ and $10^9$ runs it found every leak.


Two habits follow:


  • Write the check once per rule, in plain Python over the data, and run it after every solve in development and in production. It is the same kind of check lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy applied with $Ax \le b$.
  • When the check finds a leak, the fix is a smaller $M$ (lesson Deriving big-M from bounds) or a formulation that needs none (lesson Indicator constraints vs. big-M), never a tighter tolerance alone: a tolerance of $10^{-9}$ would have been beaten by $M = 10^{12}$ in the same way.

Problem statement


A port authority plans which ships use its berths. Each berth has a load limit that may be exceeded only if a tug is assigned to it, and the planning model writes every such rule as a big-M row, switched by the tug's binary. The authority's operations team does not trust a solver status: after every solve, it wants a list of the berth rules that the returned values break while their tug counts as not assigned.


Write leaks(values, rows, tol) in audit.py. values maps each variable's name to the value the solver returned. Each row is a dict: its name, its terms (variable name to coefficient), its limit, and the name of its switch variable. Read the rule each row states, "the sum of the terms stays within the limit unless the switch is on", the way a planner would: decide whether the switch is on as a planner reads a binary, and allow the sum to exceed the limit by at most tol, since the values come from floating-point arithmetic. Return the names of the broken rows, in the order the rows are given.

Summary


  • With a loose M the LP hires a sliver of a contractor. In the relaxation a big-M rule with a fee costs $F/M$ per unit over the limit; with $M = 10^6$ that is nothing.
  • A loose M weakens the LP bound; the MILP optimum does not move. The bound with $10^6$ equalled the bound with no crew rule at all; the tight $M$ closed about 8% of the gap and, with presolve off, cut the search from 662 nodes to 32.
  • A huge M lets the integrality tolerance switch a rule off. With $M = 10^7$ HiGHS reported OPTIMAL for a plan worth more than any allowed plan, through switches of about $10^{-7}$.
  • Check the plan against the rule, not against the solver's status. Round the binaries, recompute every rule from the data, and report any row that is broken.

Design Note: Why the Course Never Writes 1e6


An $M$ of $10^6$ in a model has the same shape as a magic number in any code: it carries an unstated assumption ("no row ever exceeds a million") that nobody checks when the data grow, and it is wrong in both directions at once: too large for the LP bound, and possibly too small for tomorrow's data. A function that computes $M$ from the data, as crew_model.py does, is easier to review, and a post-solve check catches whatever the review missed.
