Three projects in GridCap small's Coast region work on the same substation: P011 (vegetation clearing around it), P016 and P021 (transmission work in it). Each one needs the substation out of service while its crew works, and the operator allows only one outage at a time, so the three jobs cannot overlap within the year. The planners want an order that gets the assets back in service as early as possible. "Job A finishes before job B starts, or job B finishes before job A starts" is not a single linear row: it is a choice between two groups of rows, a disjunction. This lesson writes it two ways, as big-M rows and as the hull formulation, and compares them by the measure lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics used, the LP bound, in four rules. (Lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms, later in this section, uses the same measure on precedence rows.)

As in lesson Deriving big-M from bounds, GridCap's data is fictional, and one reading of it is this lesson's own: each project's crew_months is taken as the months its single crew works with the outage on.

The Model

Sets. $J$ the jobs (P011, P016, P021); $\Pi = \{(i, j) \in J \times J : i \text{ before } j \text{ in the list}\}$ the pairs that share the outage (every pair here).

Parameters. $d_j$, job $j$'s duration in months (2, 3 and 5); $H = 12$, the months in the year.

Variables. $s_j \in [0,\, H - d_j]$, the month job $j$ starts (continuous, so a job may start mid-month), for $j \in J$. $y_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $(i, j) \in \Pi$: 1 if $i$ goes first, 0 if $j$ does.

Objective. Every month a job is unfinished, its asset waits; minimize the sum of finish months:

Constraints, as a disjunction. For every pair, one of two rows holds:

The symbol $\vee$ reads "or". A disjunction is not yet something a MILP solver accepts; the next two rules turn it into linear rows with the binary $y_{ij}$ choosing the side. Each side of the "or", here a single row, is called a disjunct; in general a disjunct can be a whole group of rows.

A Disjunction Written as Big-M Switches Each Disjunct With Its Own Binary

The big-M form gives each disjunct a switch, and ties the switches together so exactly one disjunct is on. With one binary per pair, $y_{ij}$ switches on the first disjunct and $1 - y_{ij}$ the second:

$M$ follows from lesson Deriving big-M from bounds's bound rule. With the switch off, the first row must allow every pair of starts the bounds allow, so $M$ must cover the largest value of $s_i + d_i - s_j$: $s_i$ at its upper bound $H - d_i$ and $s_j$ at its lower bound 0, which gives $(H - d_i) + d_i - 0 = H$. The same holds for the second row, so $M = H = 12$ months is the smallest valid value from bounds. (With these durations it is also the exact $M^\ast$: $s_i = H - d_i$ and $s_j = 0$ is a schedule the other disjunct allows.)

Solved as a MILP and as its LP relaxation:

The best order is shortest job first: P011 in months 0 to 2, P016 in 2 to 5, P021 in 5 to 10, so the finish months add up to 2 + 5 + 10 = 17. The LP relaxation is useless here. It starts all three jobs in month 0, all at once, and sets each $y_{ij}$ to a fraction that keeps both rows of the pair loose: with $y_{P011,P016} = 0.25$, the first row has $12 \times 0.75 = 9$ months of room and the second $12 \times 0.25 = 3$, enough for P011's 2 months and P016's 3. The LP bound, 10, is just the sum of the durations: the bound of a model with no outage rule at all, as with $M = 10^6$ in lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics, except that here even the tightest $M$ cannot do better.

    Two ways to upgrade a feeder, as big-M rows

    • each disjunct of an either-or rule gets a switch, and each row's M comes from the bounds
    • a disjunct's row that the variables' bounds already imply needs no switched row

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A feeder that keeps tripping is upgraded in exactly one of two ways. A line crew reconductors the worst spans: it spends between 10 and 30 ($k), and the capacity it adds is at most half of what it spends. A rebuild replaces the feeder: it spends between 60 and 100, and adds at most 40 of capacity, whatever it spends.

mode_variables (given) creates the spend $s \in [10, 100]$, the capacity gain $g \in [0, 40]$ and the binary $r$ (1 = rebuild, 0 = line crew). Write add_bigm_modes(model, s, g, r) in upgrade.py: the disjunction as big-M rows, each with the smallest $M$ the variables' bounds justify, computed in code from s.lower_bound, g.upper_bound and the module's constants.

"From the bounds" means from $10 \le s \le 100$ and $0 \le g \le 40$ alone, as in the variable-bounds rule of lesson Deriving big-M from bounds, and not from the other rows of the disjunct. For the line crew's gain row, the reference takes the largest value $g - 0.5\,s$ can reach over those bounds, $40 - 0.5 \cdot 10 = 35$. It does not use the rebuild's own floor $s \ge 60$, which would bring that $M$ down to 10; that smaller value is also valid, and the tests accept it, but it is a step beyond the bounds.

The model so far:

Some rows of each disjunct are already implied by the variables' bounds, and need no row of their own. Decide which, write the remaining rows with their $M$s in this notation first, then code them.

The tests fix a plan by its variables' bounds and ask the solver whether it is allowed, so any correct set of rows passes; one more test checks that the LP relaxation of your rows is at least as tight as rows with $M$s from the bounds. About six lines.

The Hull Gives Each Disjunct Its Own Copy of the Variables


The big-M rows are weak because both disjuncts talk about the same $s_i$ and $s_j$, and a fractional switch only loosens each row; it never forces the starts to be a mix of two orders that actually work. The hull formulation (also called the convex-hull or Balas formulation, after the disjunctive programming it comes from) fixes that by giving each disjunct its own copy of every variable it mentions:


  • Split each start into one copy per disjunct: $s_i = s_i^a + s_i^b$, where $a$ is the disjunct "$i$ first" (switched by $y_{ij}$) and $b$ is "$j$ first" (switched by $1 - y_{ij}$).
  • Scale each copy's bounds by its disjunct's switch: $0 \le s_i^a \le (H - d_i)\, y_{ij}$, and so on. When a disjunct is off, its copies are forced to 0.
  • Write each disjunct's row on its own copies, with every constant also multiplied by the switch: $s_i^a + d_i\, y_{ij} \le s_j^a$.


For one pair:



Why does this work? When $y_{ij} = 1$, every $b$ copy is 0, so $s_i = s_i^a$ and $s_j = s_j^a$, and the $a$ row is the real "$i$ first" row; the $b$ row reads $0 \le 0$. When $y_{ij}$ is a fraction $\lambda$, the $a$ copies describe a valid "$i$ first" schedule scaled by $\lambda$, the $b$ copies a valid "$j$ first" schedule scaled by $1 - \lambda$, and the starts are their sum: a weighted average of two real schedules. That is exactly the convex hull of the disjunction, the smallest set without holes that contains every point either disjunct allows. No formulation of a single disjunction can have a tighter LP relaxation, because every point of the convex hull is an average of allowed points, and an LP cannot rule out an average of allowed points.



The same three jobs:



The MILP is the same, 17, with the same order: both forms allow the same whole-number schedules. The LP bound rose from 10.00 to 13.98, closing $3.98 / 7 \approx 57\%$ of the gap to 17. The LP's starts now spread out (0.25, 1.83, 1.9) instead of sitting at month 0, because each pair's starts must be an average of two schedules in which the jobs do not overlap.


Why is 13.98 still below 17? The hull is the convex hull of each disjunction on its own. The model has three of them, and the hull of each one separately is not the hull of all three together. Formulating the whole model as one hull would need a disjunct for every complete order of the jobs, which grows too fast to write (lesson Why MILP is hard: combinatorial growth in numbers).


Note the hull needed bounds too: each copy's cap $(H - d_k)\, y_{ij}$ is the variable's upper bound times the switch. A disjunction over unbounded variables has no hull formulation of this kind, for the same reason it has no valid big-M.

The Hull Is Tighter but Bigger, and for One Variable a Tight Big-M Already Is the Hull


The price is in the first two columns of the outputs: the hull model has 18 variables and 24 rows against the big-M model's 6 and 6. Every disjunction adds a copy of each variable it mentions, per disjunct, and a cap row for each copy. On a model with thousands of pairs, that is real size. A bigger model with a tighter bound is often the better model, but measure it; lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms, later in this section, makes the same point for precedence rows.


There is also a common case where the hull buys nothing: a disjunction over one variable's intervals, such as a contract that is either small, 100 to 300 $k, or large, 600 to 900 $k. With the tight Ms from lesson Deriving big-M from bounds, the big-M rows are $s \le 300 + 600\,\ell$ and $s \ge 600 - 500\,(1 - \ell)$, with $\ell$ the "large" binary. Solving the LP relaxation of each form for four different objectives:



The tight big-M and the hull agree on every objective: in the plane of $(s, \ell)$, the two big-M rows are exactly the two slanted edges of the convex hull of the two intervals, so the forms describe the same LP region. The loose big-M is far off on three of the four. So the rule for choosing is:


  • One variable, intervals: a tight big-M is already the hull. Use it; it is smaller.
  • Disjuncts that tie several variables together (sequencing, two modes with different costs): the hull can be much tighter, as on the outage pairs. Try both and compare LP bounds.
  • Either way, every variable needs a finite bound, and the big-M needs it to be tight.


Lesson Pyomo.GDP: disjunctions, big-M and hull transformations writes disjunctions in Pyomo's GDP language, which can build either form from one statement of the disjunction.


The same disjunction is the heart of the OR-Tools guide The Job Shop Problem. Three jobs of two or three tasks run on three machines; a job's tasks run in order, a machine runs one task at a time, and the goal is the earliest time the last job finishes, the makespan. The guide states the machine rule for one pair of tasks exactly as this lesson does: one task finishes before the other starts, or the other way round. CP-SAT then takes it for a whole machine at once: each task is an interval variable (a start, a duration and an end tied together), and add_no_overlap forbids any two intervals of a machine to overlap. job_shop.py solves the guide's example that way, and as a MILP with this lesson's add_big_m and add_hull on every pair of tasks that share a machine:








All three find the makespan of 11 the guide reports. The $M$ is the horizon, 21, the sum of all durations, which is also the bound the guide puts on every start and end. The LP bounds repeat the outage result: 7 for big-M, which is only the longest job (each job's three or two tasks take 7 in a row), and 8.20 for the hull. Both are below 10, the work machine 1 must do in any schedule. Each hull describes one pair of tasks, and no pair sees a whole machine's load; add_no_overlap states the rule for all of a machine's intervals in one constraint.


Summary


  • A disjunction written as big-M switches each disjunct with its own binary. For sequencing, the smallest $M$ from bounds is the horizon; even so the LP starts every job at month 0, and its bound is that of a model with no outage rule.
  • The hull gives each disjunct its own copy of the variables. Copies scaled by the switch make the LP relaxation of each disjunction its convex hull; on three outage jobs the bound rose from 10 to 13.98 against an optimum of 17.
  • The hull is tighter but bigger, and for one variable a tight big-M already is the hull. 18 variables and 24 rows against 6 and 6; on one variable's intervals the two LPs were identical.

Design Note: Name the Disjunct, Then Choose Its Form


outage_model.build(..., form=...) takes the form as a parameter, like crew_model.build in lesson Indicator constraints vs. big-M. The disjunction itself, "these two jobs do not overlap", is the business rule; big-M and hull are two ways to hand it to a MILP solver. Keeping the rule in one place (the loop over pairs) and the form in another (add_big_m, add_hull) lets you switch forms with one argument and measure, which is how the two LP bounds above were obtained.
