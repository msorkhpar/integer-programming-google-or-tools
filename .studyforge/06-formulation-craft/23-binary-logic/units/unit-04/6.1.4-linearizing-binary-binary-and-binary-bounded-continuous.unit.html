  15. Linearizing binary products

Two refinements reach GridCap's planners for the small portfolio. Field operations points out that two distribution projects in the same region, funded together, share one crew mobilization, and values that at $150k a pair; and that two transmission projects in the same region, funded together, need their outages coordinated, at a cost of $400k a pair. Both say "for each pair funded together", which is a product: $x_a\,x_b$ is 1 exactly when both are funded. And the vegetation contract now lets GridCap choose one clearance width $q$, between 3 and 6 metres, for every funded vegetation project; a project's cost and value depend on the width only if it is funded, which is the product $x_i\,q$ of a binary and a continuous variable. A MILP allows neither product. This lesson replaces each by a new variable and linear rows that give exactly the same plans, in four rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course; the savings, costs and the clearance-width contract are invented for this lesson.

A Product of Two Binaries Is Their AND, Written as Three Rows

Multiply two 0/1 numbers and you get 1 only when both are 1:

$x_a$$x_b$$x_a x_b$
000
010
100
111

That is the truth table of AND, so $x_a x_b$ is "$a$ and $b$", and lesson AND, OR, NOT, XOR and implication as linear constraints already wrote AND as a value: a new binary $z_{ab}$ with

Linearizing a product means exactly this: replace the nonlinear term by a new variable, and add linear rows that force the variable to equal the term at every 0/1 point. The model is then linear again, and every plan it allows is a plan the product model allowed, with the same value.

Why not leave the product in? The objective $\sum_i \text{npv}_i x_i + 150 \sum_{(a,b)} x_a x_b$ is quadratic (it has products of two variables), and a MILP solver is not obliged to accept that. The program writes the bonus exactly that way, then hands the model to HiGHS and to SCIP, the solver MathOpt reaches as GSCIP:

Then it replaces each product by its $z$ and solves the linear model with HiGHS:

HiGHS, the course's default solver, refuses the quadratic objective through MathOpt. (OR-Tools 9.15 raises an AttributeError that hides the solver's message, as lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP noted for other refusals; the real reason is on the exception's __context__.) SCIP accepts it and finds $26,064k. The linearized model gives HiGHS a plain MILP and reaches the same $26,064k, with 12 pairs funded together. Linearizing is what makes the model portable: the result is a plain MILP, the kind of model every MILP solver of section 5 is built for.

The cost is one variable and three rows per pair. 22 pairs is small; all pairs of 400 projects would be about 80,000, so in practice the pairs come from data (the same region, the same feeder), never from every combination.

The Sign of the Product's Term Decides Which Rows It Needs

Lesson AND, OR, NOT, XOR and implication as linear constraints showed that a variable pinned from one side may be enough, depending on which way the solver wants to move it. Products are the most common place this matters. The program solves both refinements three ways: with all three rows per pair, with only the two rows that push $z$ down ($z \le x_a$, $z \le x_b$), and with only the row that pushes it up ($z \ge x_a + x_b - 1$). For each, it compares what the solver claims (its objective, which trusts $z$) with what the plan is really worth (NPV plus the bonus or cost of the pairs the plan actually funds):

  • A reward on $z$ (a positive term in a maximization) makes the solver push $z$ up, so the rows that push it down are the ones that hold it to the truth. With them alone the answer is exact. Without them, the solver sets all 22 $z$ to 1, claims $28,636k, and returns a plan that funds only 2 real pairs and is worth $25,636k, $428k less than the true optimum.
  • A cost on $z$ (a negative term in a maximization) makes the solver push $z$ down, so the row that pushes it up is the one that matters. Without it, the solver sets every $z$ to 0, pays no coordination cost, and returns the plan that ignores it: 7 transmission pairs, really worth $22,536k, reported as $25,336k.

The same holds when the product sits in a row instead of the objective: a product in a $\le$ row with a positive coefficient (a cost in a budget) is pushed down and needs the "up" row; a product whose coefficient lowers the spend (a discount) is pushed up and needs the "down" rows. Writing all three rows is always correct. Dropping a side is an optimization that is only safe while every use of $z$ pushes the same way.

A product of more than two binaries is the AND of all of them: $x_a x_b x_c$ becomes $z$ with $z \le x_a$, $z \le x_b$, $z \le x_c$ and $z \ge x_a + x_b + x_c - 2$, from the table in lesson AND, OR, NOT, XOR and implication as linear constraints.

    Detours and corridor fees for street works

    • the product of binaries is replaced by a new binary tied to its factors by linear rows
    • whether a product is rewarded or charged decides which of its rows are essential

Problem statement

A city's roads department is choosing which streets to rebuild next summer. Rebuilding streets at the same time causes extra traffic costs that a street-by-street price list misses:

  • Detours. For each pair of adjacent streets in detours (a dict from (a, b) to a cost in $k), rebuilding both in the same summer needs a temporary detour route, which costs that amount. Rebuilding only one of them needs no detour.
  • Corridor fees. Each (streets, fee) in corridor_fees is a corridor of several streets. If every street of the corridor is rebuilt, the police charge a corridor-wide traffic-management fee. If only some of them are, there is no fee. A corridor may have any number of streets.

The plan's spend (street costs plus detours plus corridor fees) must not exceed budget. The model in streets.py has a binary x[sid] per street (1 if rebuilt) and no budget row yet. Write add_traffic_costs(model, x, streets, detours, corridor_fees, budget) so that the model allows exactly the plans whose real spend fits the budget. Add whatever variables you need.

With $S$ the streets and $c_s$ the cost of street $s$ ($k), the model so far is only

The real spend has products of binaries in it. Write the spend with those products first, then replace each by a new variable with its domain and the rows that make it equal to the product wherever it matters. Decide for each new variable which way the solver would like to move it.

The tests fix which streets are rebuilt and ask the solver whether the model accepts the plan, so any correct formulation passes. About ten lines.

  • $x_i = 0$. Row (1) says $w_i \le 0$ and row (3) says $w_i \ge 0$, so $w_i = 0$. Rows (2) and (4) become $w_i \le q - L$ and $w_i \ge q - U$; since $L \le q \le U$, the first right side is at least 0 and the second at most 0, so both hold for $w_i = 0$ and cut nothing.
  • $x_i = 1$. Rows (2) and (4) say $w_i \le q$ and $w_i \ge q$, so $w_i = q$. Rows (1) and (3) become $w_i \le U$ and $w_i \ge L$, which $w_i = q$ already satisfies.


In each case two rows do the pinning and two are switched off by the bounds, the same trick as the switch rows of lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k. These four rows are known as the McCormick rows of the product; for a binary $x$ they are exact. Rows (1) and (2) push $w$ down, rows (3) and (4) push it up:




The program fixes $x$ and $q$, then asks HiGHS for the smallest and the largest $w$ the rows allow ($w$ is declared between 0 and 6):



With all four rows, $w$ has one value and it is $x\,q$. As with binaries, each side alone leaves $w$ free in one direction, and the same reasoning picks the side to keep: here $w_i$ earns value in the objective and spends budget, so the solver would like it larger for one reason and smaller for the other, and the model needs all four.


The GridCap model puts each funded vegetation project's cost and value on $w_i$:




and solves it with $q$ fixed at each whole width, then with $q$ left to the solver:



At $q = 4$ every cost and value equals GridCap's own, and the model returns the familiar $25,336k: a check that the rows change nothing when the width is the standard one. Left free, the solver picks 5.897 metres, a width no one would have tried by hand, and funds 10 vegetation projects at it, $568.9k more than the standard width.


When the continuous variable belongs to one project only (each project has its own amount $q_i$, used nowhere else), there is nothing to multiply: define the amount as already switched off, $L\,x_i \le q_i \le U\,x_i$, and use $q_i$ directly. The four rows are for a continuous variable that exists whether or not the project is funded and is shared, like the one width of a contract.


CP-SAT, OR-Tools' constraint solver (lessons Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers to When CP-SAT beats MIP, and when it doesn't), writes both products of this lesson as logic instead of rows. Two OR-Tools samples show how. boolean_product_sample_sat.py writes $p = x\,y$ for Booleans as one clause and two implications: "$x$ and $y$ implies $p$" is the clause "not $x$, or not $y$, or $p$", the row that pushes $p$ up, and "$p$ implies $x$", "$p$ implies $y$" are the two rows that push it down. bool_and_int_var_product_sample_sat.py writes $p = b\,x$ for a Boolean $b$ and an integer $x$ as two equalities, each enforced by one value of $b$. cpsat_products.py builds both, with $x$ from 1 to 4 in the second, and lists every solution:








The first list is the AND table, so $p$ is pinned on every line. The second has $p = 0$ whenever $b = 0$ and $p = x$ whenever $b = 1$: the product, with no $L$ or $U$ written in any row. The bounds have not gone away, though. CP-SAT gives every variable a finite domain, and $p$'s domain, 0 to 4, is $x$'s domain with 0 added, as the sample builds it. In a MILP solver the same knowledge goes into the four rows; in CP-SAT it goes into the domains.


The Bounds in the Rows Are Part of the Formulation


The four rows are exact only because $q$ has a lower bound $L$ and an upper bound $U$. With no finite upper bound there is no number to write in row (1), and no linear form exists. The bound in the rows does not have to be the tightest one to be correct: any $U' \ge U$ still gives $w = x\,q$ at every 0/1 point. But the rows are also what the LP relaxation sees, and there the number matters. The program solves the width model's LP relaxation (GLOP) and MILP (HiGHS) with the true bound 6 written in the rows, with looser bounds, and with 5, a bound below the true one:



  • A looser bound keeps the MILP and weakens the LP. With 60, the MILP is unchanged, but the LP relaxation funds 0.053 of five projects at a width of 3.16 metres each: row (1) allows $w \le 60\,x$, about 3.16 at $x = 0.053$, so a twentieth of a project is cleared at more than half the full width. The LP bound rises from $25,983.20k to $26,043.95k, further from the true $25,904.9k, and with 600 further still. Lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap explained why a looser bound means a harder search.
  • A bound below the true one is wrong, not loose. With 5, row (4) for a funded project reads $w \ge q$ while row (1) reads $w \le 5$, so $q$ can no longer exceed 5. The model silently loses every plan with a wider clearance, and the "optimum" drops to $25,657.0k, the value of the best plan at $q = 5$.


So take the bound from the data (here the contract's 6 metres), never a large round number "to be safe", and never a guess that might be too small. Summary


  • A product of two binaries is their AND, written as three rows. Replace $x_a x_b$ by a binary $z$ with $z \le x_a$, $z \le x_b$, $z \ge x_a + x_b - 1$; HiGHS refuses the product itself.
  • The sign of the product's term decides which rows it needs. A reward needs the rows that push $z$ down, a cost the row that pushes it up; without the right side the solver claims value the plan does not have.
  • A binary times a bounded continuous variable takes four rows. With $L \le q \le U$, $w = x\,q$ is exact through the four McCormick rows; a per-project amount needs only $L\,x \le q \le U\,x$.
  • The bounds in the rows are part of the formulation. A loose bound weakens the LP bound; a bound below the true one cuts off real plans.

Design Note: Linearize Where the Model Is Built, Not in the Data


A product could be avoided by precomputing: list every width the contract allows (3, 3.5, 4, …) and give each project one binary per width. That trades a continuous decision for a grid and many more binaries, and it misses 5.897 metres. The opposite mistake is to compute $x_i q$ after the solve ("we'll multiply later"), which leaves the solver choosing $q$ without seeing what it costs. Keep the products in the model, written as the rows of this lesson, in one helper such as product_rows, so every use of a product gets the same exact rows and the same bounds, and a reviewer has one place to check them.
