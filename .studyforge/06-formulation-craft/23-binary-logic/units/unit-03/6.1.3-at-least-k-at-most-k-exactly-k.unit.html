GridCap's rate-case team brings three numbers to the planning meeting. The Valley transmission yard can stage at most 2 projects in the plan. The rate case promises every region at least 4 funded projects. And the Coast vegetation crew is under contract for exactly 2 jobs, no more and no fewer. Each is a statement about how many projects of a group the plan funds, which lesson Binary selection and counting constraints showed is a linear expression. This lesson writes the three kinds of row, shows how each can make the model impossible to solve and how to catch that before solving, compares two ways of writing "at most one", and handles a rule that a count must be either zero or at least $k$, in four rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course, and the numbers above are invented for this lesson.

At Most k, At Least k and Exactly k Are One Row Each on the Count

For a group of projects $S \subseteq P$ and a whole number $k$, the three rules are

"Exactly" is "at most" and "at least" at once, which is why it is an equality. With $k = 1$ the three have names you will meet in the literature: at most one is a packing row (the alternative-group row $\sum_{i \in g} x_i \le 1$ of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model is one), at least one is a covering row, and exactly one is a partitioning row, "choose one of these".

The three rate-case rules, on GridCap small:

at_least writes one row per region, with the quantifier $\forall r \in R$; the other two are single rows. Each rule is solved on its own, and the program prints the counts the rules talk about:

The best plan without the rules funds 4 Valley transmission projects, 3 projects each in Ridge and Plains, and 3 Coast vegetation jobs, so each rule cuts that plan off and the solver has to find another:

  • At most 2 costs $543k: two Valley transmission projects leave, and the money goes to cheaper projects in other regions (Ridge rises from 3 to 4, Plains from 3 to 5).
  • At least 4 per region costs only $19k: Ridge and Plains each gain a project, and Coast gives one up. A minimum is cheap when cheap projects can meet it.
  • Exactly 2 costs $31k. The Coast crew had 3 jobs in the best plan; "exactly" pushes the count down here. With a plan that had only 1 Coast vegetation job, the same row would push it up. An equality row cuts both ways, which is the reason to use it only when the business means both bounds.

Check k Against What the Plan Must and Can Fund Before You Solve

A model none of whose plans satisfies every row is infeasible: the solver returns the status INFEASIBLE and no plan at all. A counting row is the easiest way to make a model infeasible, and it does so in two ways:

  • An at-least row asks for more than a plan can fund. If a group has fewer candidates than $k$, no plan reaches $k$. Candidates that exclude each other count once: two projects in the same alternative group can never both be funded.
  • An at-most row allows fewer than the plan must fund. If a group holds more must-do projects than $k$, every plan already exceeds $k$.

Both are checks on the data alone, so you can run them before building the model and report which group and which number is wrong, something the solver's single word INFEASIBLE does not say. The program tries three invented rules on GridCap small, "at least 2 vegetation projects per region", "at least 2 transmission projects per region" and "at most 1 vegetation project per region", with these checks:

The second case is the one a quick look gets wrong. Plains has two transmission candidates, P001 and P002, so "at least 2" looks possible; but they are the replace and repair options for the same asset, alternative group G001, and a plan may fund only one of them.

The checks are necessary, not sufficient. A group can pass both and the model can still be infeasible, because the projects the minimum needs do not fit the budget together with the must-dos, or because two counting rows on overlapping groups pull against each other. When the checks pass and the solver still says INFEASIBLE, the tools of section 9 find the smallest set of rows that conflict.

The OR-Tools guide Employee Scheduling builds a nurse schedule from these three kinds of row and nothing else. Four nurses cover three shifts a day for three days. Every shift gets exactly one nurse, a partitioning row; no nurse works more than one shift a day, a packing row; and the nine shifts are spread so that each nurse works at least $\lfloor 9/4 \rfloor = 2$ of them and at most 3, an at-least and an at-most row on each nurse's count. CP-SAT has the first two as constraints of their own, add_exactly_one and add_at_most_one. nurses.py is the guide's model, with every schedule counted instead of the first five printed:

The at-most row changes nothing here, as the guide says: four nurses with at least 2 of 9 shifts each leave one shift over, so no nurse can reach 4. The at-least rows cut the schedules from 13,824 to 5,184. And "at least 3 each" is infeasible for the reason the first check above gives, counted over a group of rows instead of one: the four minimums ask for 12 shifts and only 9 exist, which a line of arithmetic shows before any solve.

Problem statement

A regional cellular carrier is upgrading towers under a state broadband grant. The model in grant.py has a binary x[tid] per candidate tower (1 if upgraded this cycle) and a binary y[oid] per backhaul option, the link that carries a hub tower's traffic (fiber, radio or satellite). The grant and the carrier's own limits set three rules:

  • Coverage. Each county c in need must end the cycle with at least need[c] upgraded towers. Towers upgraded in earlier cycles count: already[c] of them exist (missing means 0).
  • Crews. A county in crew_limit can take at most crew_limit[c] upgrades this cycle; a county not in it has no limit.
  • Backhaul. backhaul maps each hub tower to its backhaul options. An upgraded hub gets exactly one of its options. A backhaul link is only built for a hub that is upgraded.

Write add_grant_rules(model, x, y, towers, need, already, crew_limit, backhaul) so that the model allows exactly the plans that keep all three rules.

The model so far, with $T$ the candidate towers, $H \subseteq T$ the hubs and $O_h$ the backhaul options of hub $h$:

Before you write a coverage row, ask what it should be for a county that already meets its target. (The reference writes no coverage row for such a county: its row would ask for 0 or fewer new upgrades, which every plan keeps, so skipping it allows the same plans.)

The tests fix every tower and every backhaul option through the variables' bounds and ask the solver whether the model accepts the plan, so any correct formulation passes. About ten lines.

At Most One of Many Is One Row, Not One Row per Pair


Suppose the Valley transmission line can be taken out of service only once in the plan, so at most one of the six Valley transmission projects may be funded. One way to write it says it pair by pair, $x_a + x_b \le 1$ for every two projects $a, b$ of the group, which is the "not both" row of lesson AND, OR, NOT, XOR and implication as linear constraints repeated. Six projects make $6 \cdot 5 / 2 = 15$ pairs. The other way is the single count row $\sum_{i \in S} x_i \le 1$:




Both allow exactly the same 0/1 plans: if two projects were funded, the pair row for those two would fail, and so would the count row. So both MILPs have the same optimum. Their LP relaxations (the same rows with each $x_i$ allowed anywhere between 0 and 1, lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails) are not the same. The program solves each relaxation with GLOP and each MILP with HiGHS:



With pair rows, the relaxation sets five projects to 0.5. No pair sums to more than 1, so every pair row holds, and the group as a whole is "two and a half projects", which the business rule forbids and the count row refuses. The pair form's LP bound is $387.10k above the true optimum; the count row's is $69.20k above it. Lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap called that distance the integrality gap and showed why a smaller one helps: the solver's search (lessons Branching on a fractional variable to A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines) prunes with the LP bound, so a bound closer to the optimum lets it discard more of the tree.


The count row is also one row instead of $n(n-1)/2$, which matters when a group has 50 members (1,225 pairs). Merge pair rows into one count row whenever every pair in a group conflicts. When the conflicts do not cover every pair (A conflicts with B and B with C, but A and C may go together), the group is not a single "at most one", and only the pairs that really conflict can be written.


None, or at Least k, Needs a Switch Variable


The vegetation contractor makes one last condition: it will go to a region only if it gets at least 2 vegetation jobs there. A region may have no vegetation work at all, or 2 or more, but not exactly 1. That is not a single counting row, because the allowed counts, $\{0, 2, 3, \dots\}$, have a hole in them. A new binary per region, $g_r$ ("the contractor goes to region $r$"), switches between the two cases:



with $V_r$ the vegetation projects of region $r$ and $|V_r|$ how many there are. Read the two rows for each value of the switch:


  • $g_r = 0$: the first row says the count is at least 0, which always holds; the second says it is at most 0. No vegetation work in $r$.
  • $g_r = 1$: the first row says the count is at least $k$; the second says it is at most $|V_r|$, which always holds. At least $k$ jobs in $r$.


So each row is "switched off" in one case by being made trivially true, and the switch is the solver's choice. $|V_r|$ in the second row is the largest the count can ever be; any smaller number would cut off real plans, and lesson Deriving big-M from bounds makes that idea, choosing the smallest number that is still safe, into a method.




The best plan had exactly 1 vegetation job in Ridge, the case the contractor refuses. The solver could drop P035 (to 0) or add a second Ridge job (to 2); adding P008 cost less, $19k in all after the plan rebalanced elsewhere. Plains has only one vegetation candidate, so it can never reach 2, and the rule forces it to 0 for good; a precheck like the one above would flag that before the solve.


The same two rows work with any upper limit: "none, or between $k$ and $K$" is $k\,g \le \text{count} \le K\,g$. And they work with a weighted count, a total of kilometres or units in place of a number of projects, as long as the second row uses the largest value that total can take.

Summary


  • At most k, at least k and exactly k are one row each on the count. Exactly is both bounds; use it only when the business means both.
  • Check k against what the plan must and can fund before you solve. Count candidates (each alternative group once) against an at-least $k$, and must-dos against an at-most $k$; the checks are necessary, not sufficient.
  • At most one of many is one row, not one row per pair. The same 0/1 plans, a tighter LP bound ($69.20k against $387.10k of gap here), and far fewer rows.
  • None, or at least k, needs a switch variable. $k\,g \le \text{count} \le |S|\,g$ with $g$ binary.

Design Note: Name the Group, Not the Pairs


Rules arrive from the business as pairs ("these two can't share an outage"), because that is how engineers find them. Before writing them, gather the pairs into groups: if every two projects of a group conflict, the group is one "at most one" row, and the model is smaller and its LP bound tighter. Keep the pair list as the source the planners maintain, and let the model builder find the groups. Lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities treats this and other ways of strengthening rows as a topic of its own.
