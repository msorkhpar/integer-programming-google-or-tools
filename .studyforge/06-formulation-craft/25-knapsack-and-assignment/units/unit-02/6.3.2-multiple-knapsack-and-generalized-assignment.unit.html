GridCap small's best plan (lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model's selection model) funds 20 projects that need 100 crew-months of field work over the five-year plan. The work-planning team has four regional crews, one per region, with 30, 30, 20 and 20 crew-months free, and a contractor who can take up to 20 crew-months more at a higher rate. Planning asked "which projects?"; work planning asks "which crew does each one?" A crew that works outside its own region loses a crew-month to travel and set-up, and only some crews are qualified for transmission work.

Each crew is a knapsack: it has a capacity, and the projects it takes must fit in it. With several crews sharing one list of projects the model becomes a multiple knapsack, and when every project must be placed and its size depends on the crew, a generalized assignment problem (GAP). This lesson builds both, adds the qualification rule, and looks at what the LP relaxation does with them.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. Its crews, their capacities, rates and qualifications are invented for this lesson.

Several Knapsacks Share the Items: One Binary per Item and Knapsack

In lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack there was one bag. Now there are several, $K$ of them, each with its own capacity $b_k$, and each item goes into at most one of them. A binary per item is no longer enough, because the model must say which knapsack an item is in. So there is one binary per item and knapsack:

Two families of rows complete the multiple knapsack: each item is used at most once, and each knapsack holds no more than its capacity.

The OR-Tools guide Solving a Multiple Knapsacks Problem uses this model with 15 items and five bins of capacity 100. The program below is that example's data and rows, written in MathOpt and solved with HiGHS (the OR-Tools sample uses the older pywraplp wrapper, which lesson Migrating a pywraplp model to MathOpt maps to MathOpt):

Value 395 and weight 438, the numbers the OR-Tools guide reports. Now the tempting shortcut: five bins of 100 hold 500 in all, so why not one binary per item and one row, "total weight at most 500"? The same program solves that too:

The pooled row promises 410, and no packing into the five bins achieves it: the multiple knapsack's optimum is 395. Pooling forgets that items are indivisible and bins are separate. A set of items can fit in the total and still not split into five groups of at most 100 each. Every packing into the bins obeys the pooled row (add the five bin rows up and you get it), so the pooled model is a relaxation of the multiple knapsack: it allows every plan the bins allow, and more. Its optimum is a bound, not a plan.

On GridCap the knapsacks are the crews. The selection model keeps its binary $y_i$ (fund project $i$) and its rows, and one row per project ties the two: a funded project gets exactly one crew, an unfunded one none.

The row $\sum_k x_{i,k} = y_i$ is the "at most once" row with the selection decision on its right: when $y_i = 0$ it forces every $x_{i,k}$ to 0, and when $y_i = 1$ exactly one of them is 1. With every crew taking any project at its own crew-months:

The plan is unchanged ($25,336k), and its 100 crew-months pack exactly into the four crews. Many other packings would do as well (the crews could swap projects of equal size), so the lists above are one optimal answer among several, not the answer. And this model ignores something the work planners care about: here the Ridge crew does P030, a Valley project, at no extra cost.

Problem statement

A utility's central warehouse (not GridCap) is loading tomorrow's trucks to the field depots. Each material order $o$ has a priority $v_o$, a weight $w_o$ (kg) and a volume $u_o$ (m³). Each truck $t$ can carry at most $W_t$ kg and $U_t$ m³. The dispatcher's rules:

  • An order travels on one truck at most; an order left off every truck waits for the next day.
  • Every truck must stay within both its weight and its volume limit.
  • Orders for the same job site (the same site value, when it is not None) are unloaded by one crew, so they travel on the same truck, or all wait.

Every Item Goes to Exactly One Agent, at a Size That Depends on the Agent


Two things change when the plan is fixed and only the crews are left to choose. First, every project of the plan must be done, so "at most one crew" becomes "exactly one". Second, a project's size depends on who does it: a regional crew working away from home needs one more crew-month for travel and set-up, and the contractor needs none. The labour cost depends on the crew too, $20k per crew-month for a GridCap crew and $35k for the contractor.


That is the generalized assignment problem (GAP): agents $k$ (the crews), each with a capacity $b_k$; tasks $i$ (the projects), each of which must go to exactly one agent; a size $a_{i,k}$ and a cost $c_{i,k}$ that depend on the pair. "Generalized" because the classic assignment problem is the special case where every agent takes exactly one task of size 1.

Check the cost by hand. All 100 crew-months at the in-house rate would cost $2,000k. Three projects are done away from home, three extra crew-months at $20k: $60k. The contractor does 5 crew-months at $35k instead of $20k: $75k more. $2,000k + $60k + $75k = $2,135k. The model moved the small projects to the contractor (3 projects, 5 crew-months) and the big Valley project P030 to Ridge, where one travel month on a 14-month project costs less than paying contractor rates on it.


The OR-Tools guide Assignment with Task Sizes solves a GAP of this shape in which the size of a task is the same for every worker; only the cost depends on the pair. Ten workers share eight tasks, each task goes to exactly one worker, and the sizes of a worker's tasks may add up to at most 15. task_sizes.py is the guide's example (assignment_task_sizes_mip.py in the OR-Tools repository) in MathOpt, row for row the GAP above:






The minimum, 326, is the guide's. The assignment is not quite the guide's: HiGHS gives task 4 to worker 2 and the guide's run gives it to worker 4, both at a cost of 59. Two optimal answers that differ only in a tie are both correct, which is one more reason to compare costs, never assignment lists, when you check a model against a reference.


An Agent That May Not Take an Item Gets No Variable


Only the Ridge and Valley crews and the contractor hold transmission qualifications. The simplest way to forbid a pair is to leave its variable out of the model: a variable that does not exist cannot be 1, and the model is smaller too. A crew that may not do a project gets no $x_{i,k}$ for it, so no row can ever assign it (lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, later in this section, uses the same idea to enforce a project's timing window: it creates no start variable for a year outside the window):



That is the only_eligible switch above: the dictionary comprehension skips the pairs that are not in $E$, and every row sums only over the pairs that exist (if (p.project_id, k) in x). The alternative, creating every variable and adding a row $x_{i,k} = 0$ for each forbidden pair, gives the same plans with more variables and rows for the solver to read and presolve away. Note what the switch does not touch: every crew keeps its capacity, because Coast and Plains may still do distribution and vegetation work; only the forbidden pairs disappear.


Fewer pairs can make the model infeasible, and a GAP's infeasibility is expensive to discover by solving. Two quick checks catch the obvious cases first: every task must have at least one eligible agent with enough capacity for it, and the qualified agents together must have room for the qualified work at its smallest size:




Both are necessary conditions, not sufficient ones: passing them does not prove a packing exists, as the pooled row showed in the first rule. Here every project has a crew, and:



The letter after each project is its program (t, d or v). The unrestricted answer had the Coast crew on P017 and the Plains crew on P021, two transmission projects they are not qualified for. With the 16 forbidden pairs gone (100 variables down to 84), the transmission work squeezes into the three line crews: Ridge and Valley are full of it, the contractor takes 14 crew-months, and Coast and Plains pick up the vegetation work the line crews had to drop. The qualification rule costs $155k.

Problem statement


A water utility (not GridCap) has approved a set of water-main replacements for the next three years and must now decide which contractor crew does each job, and in which year. Each job $j$ is done once, in one year, by one crew:


  • Job $j$ may be done in any year from earliest to latest, both included, and in no other year.
  • Crews work at different paces: crew $c$ needs $d_{j,c}$ weeks for job $j$ (job.weeks[crew]).
  • Crew $c$ has $F_{c,t}$ free weeks in year $t$ (crew.weeks[year]), and the jobs it does that year must fit in them.
  • A large-diameter main (job.large) may only be given to a certified crew (crew.certified).
  • Crew $c$ charges $r_c$ ($k per week), so job $j$ done by crew $c$ costs $r_c\, d_{j,c}$. The utility wants the cheapest schedule.


Write build(jobs, crews, years) in schedule.py. It returns the model and a dictionary x of binary variables keyed (job id, crew id, year), where x[j, c, t] = 1 means crew $c$ does job $j$ in year $t$. You decide which keys exist.


Each agent of this assignment is a crew in a year, with its own capacity. Write the formulation first: the sets (including the set of allowed job, crew and year triples), the parameters, the variable with its domain, the objective and each labelled row. Then code it.


The tests fix a schedule and ask the solver whether the model allows it; a schedule that needs a key your x does not have counts as refused. So any correct formulation passes, whether a forbidden triple has no variable or a row holding it at 0. About fifteen lines.

The LP Relaxation Splits Items Between Agents


Relax every $x_{i,k}$ to $[0, 1]$ and a project may be shared, three quarters done by one crew and a quarter by another. The assignment rows still add each project's shares up to 1, and the capacity rows still charge each share its crew's size:



The LP bound is $2,258.75k, so no assignment can cost less, and the whole-crew answer, $2,290k, is within 1.4% of it. Only 2 of 20 projects are split, and a counting argument like lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack's says a vertex can never split many. Say $s$ projects are split. Their fractional variables, at least two per split project, are not at a bound, so at a vertex they must be pinned down by rows that hold with equality, and the only rows they appear in are the $s$ assignment rows of the split projects and the $|K|$ capacity rows. A vertex needs at least as many such rows as fractional variables, so $2s \le s + |K|$, that is $s \le |K|$: at most one split project per agent. With 5 agents that is 5, whether the plan has 20 projects or 2,000.


The guide's task-size example shows the same bound at work. Its LP relaxation, solved by GLOP:



Three of eight tasks are split, against a limit of ten, and the LP bound, 310.80, is 4.7% below the whole-number 326.


The split itself is what branch-and-bound works on: branching on $x_{P017,\text{Ridge}}$ sends P017 wholly to Ridge in one child and away from Ridge in the other (lessons Branching on a fractional variable to Node and variable selection).


Why is a split cheaper? P017 is a 13-month Coast transmission project. The Coast crew may not do it, so it costs 14 crew-months with Ridge or Valley, or 13 contractor months at $35k. The LP gives three quarters of it to Ridge and the rest to the contractor, a mix no real crew can work.


The classic assignment problem, one task per agent and every size 1, is the exception: its LP relaxation always has a whole optimal vertex, which is why it has fast special algorithms; lesson Special-purpose solvers: LinearSumAssignment, KnapsackSolver and bin packing covers OR-Tools' solver for it.


The Model


The crew assignment of GridCap small's plan: a generalized assignment problem with an eligibility set. The multiple knapsack of the first rule is the same shape with "at most one" rows, one size per item, and a value to maximize.


Sets. $I$, the projects of the plan (20); $I_{\text{trans}} \subseteq I$, its transmission projects; $K$, the agents: the four regional crews and the contractor; $K_{\text{line}} \subseteq K$, the agents qualified for transmission (Ridge, Valley, contractor); $E \subseteq I \times K$, the allowed pairs: $(i, k) \in E$ unless $i \in I_{\text{trans}}$ and $k \notin K_{\text{line}}$.


Parameters. $m_i$, the crew-months project $i$ needs at home; $a_{i,k} = m_i + 1$ for a regional crew outside its region, else $m_i$ (crew-months); $r_k$, agent $k$'s rate ($20k per crew-month for a regional crew, $35k for the contractor); $c_{i,k} = r_k\, a_{i,k}$ ($k); $b_k$, agent $k$'s capacity (30, 30, 20, 20 and 20 crew-months).

Summary


  • Several knapsacks share the items: one binary per item and knapsack. $x_{i,k}$, at most one knapsack per item, one capacity row per knapsack. One pooled row is a relaxation: on the OR-Tools example it promised 410 where the bins allow 395.
  • In a generalized assignment, every item goes to exactly one agent, at an agent-dependent size. Equality assignment rows, capacity rows with sizes $a_{i,k}$, costs $c_{i,k}$; GridCap's crew plan costs $2,135k with travel and a contractor.
  • An agent that may not take an item gets no variable. Build the variables over the allowed pairs only, and check before solving that every task has a capable agent; qualifications raised the cost to $2,290k.
  • The LP relaxation splits items between agents. Its bound was $2,258.75k with 2 projects split; branching on a split variable sends the project wholly to one side.

Design Note: Identical Agents Make Identical Plans Look Different


Two crews with the same capacity, the same home region and the same rate are interchangeable: swap everything they do and the cost is the same, yet the $x_{i,k}$ are different. A model with $n$ identical crews has up to $n!$ copies of every assignment, and branch-and-bound may explore them all. Lesson Symmetry and how to break it, later in this section, treats this; the usual cure is a row that orders the copies, for example "crew 1's load is at least crew 2's". GridCap's crews differ in region and capacity, which is one reason this lesson's model needed no such row.
