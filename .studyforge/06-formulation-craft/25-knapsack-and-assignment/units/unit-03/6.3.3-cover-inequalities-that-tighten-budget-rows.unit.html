Lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack left GridCap small's six-row knapsack with an LP bound of $21,149.21k against a best whole plan of $20,183k. The gap, $966k, is the price of letting projects be funded in part: the LP funds 34% of P024 and 34% of P030 because each budget row only measures money, and money can be split. Branch-and-bound closes that gap by splitting the search (lesson Bounds, incumbents and pruning); this lesson closes most of it before any branching, by adding rows that every whole plan already obeys but the fractional LP points do not.

The rows come from a simple observation about one budget row: some sets of projects cannot all be funded, because together they cost more than the budget. Saying so as a row, "at most all but one of these", is a cover inequality. Lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand introduces cutting planes in general; this lesson works with covers on GridCap's budget rows, from the definition to an automatic loop that closes 90% of the gap.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. None of its numbers describe a real company.

A Cover Is a Set of Items That Cannot All Fit

Take one knapsack row with whole-number weights, $\sum_{i \in I} a_i\, x_i \le b$, with every $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and every $a_i \ge 0$. A set $C \subseteq I$ is a cover of the row when its weights add up to more than the capacity:

Then no whole plan takes every item of $C$: that plan would break the row. So every whole plan takes at most $|C| - 1$ of them ($|C|$ is the number of items in $C$):

This row is a valid inequality: every whole plan the model allows also satisfies it, so adding it to the model removes no whole plan and cannot change the MILP's optimum. It is not implied by the budget row for fractional points, and that is the whole point, as the next rule shows.

GridCap tiny is small enough to list them all. Its must-do project is paid for first, as in lesson A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines, which leaves seven projects and $4,660k:

Of the $2^7 = 128$ sets of projects, 82 are covers. A cover is minimal when removing any one item leaves a set that fits; only 11 are. Every other cover contains a minimal one, and its row is weaker, as the third rule shows, so the listing keeps the minimal ones.

A Cover Row Cuts Off LP Points the Budget Row Allows

The LP relaxation of tiny's knapsack is the fill of lesson A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines: P007, P004 and 64% of P001. Check the first cover row at that point: $x_{P001} + x_{P004} = 0.637 + 1 = 1.637$, more than 1. The budget row allows this point and the cover row does not. A valid inequality that some LP point breaks is a cut (or cutting plane): it cuts that point off the LP's feasible region and leaves every whole plan in it.

The program solves the LP, finds the minimal cover whose row the LP point breaks by the most, adds that row, and solves again, until no minimal cover row is broken:

Two cuts, and the LP's answer is a whole plan worth 3,021, the optimum lesson A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines's search proved with 9 nodes. No branching was needed. Read the two cuts as rules a planner would recognise: "P004 (a $3,000k project) cannot share the budget with P001, nor with P006." The budget row says the same about whole projects, but it lets the LP take all of P004 and a bit of P001; the cover rows do not.

Why can't the budget row say it by itself? A row is a straight cut through the space of plans. The budget row is placed so that it allows every plan whose cost fits, including fractional ones. The cover rows are placed using the extra fact that plans are whole. The tightest possible formulation, the convex hull of the whole plans (the smallest region with straight sides that contains them all), would give the optimum straight from the LP; covers are one family of rows that carve the LP region toward it. Lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound, later in this section, measures formulations by exactly this distance.

    Jobs too big to share a resource

    • a cover row removes fractional LP points the knapsack row allows and keeps every whole plan
    • a set of items whose weights exceed the capacity gives a row that every whole plan obeys

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A transmission operator (not GridCap) plans its substation jobs for next season. Several limited resources are shared: the mobile crane's days, the outage hours the grid operator grants, the budget. Each is a knapsack row,

with $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$ (do job $j$), $u_{\ell,j}$ the whole amount of resource $\ell$ that job $j$ uses and $U_\ell$ what is available. build in jobs.py already writes these rows and the objective.

The planners noticed that some jobs are so big that no two of them fit under the same limit, and that the LP relaxation ignores this: it happily takes one big job and part of another. Write add_big_item_rows(model, x, limits), which adds rows that

  • every whole plan that fits all the limits still satisfies (so no plan the limits allow is lost), and
  • stop the LP from combining big jobs: for each limit, the jobs no two of which fit together under it may be funded at most one at a time, and a job that does not fit under a limit even on its own is never funded, not even in part.

Each such row comes from a cover of one limit's row, a cover of two jobs or of one. Decide on paper which jobs belong to a limit's row, why "no two of them fit" is a simple test on each job's own use, and write the rows with their labels before coding. When a limit has only one big job, the reference writes no "at most one" row for it: that row would read $x_j \le 1$, which the variable's bound already says (a job too big even on its own still gets its "never" row).

The tests try every whole plan of five jobs against the limits, and compare the LP bound with the value the right rows give, so any correct set of valid rows passes. About seven lines.

Shrink a Cover to Minimal, Then Extend It With Heavier Items


Two ways to make a cover row stronger, each a few lines of code.


Minimal first. If $C$ is a cover and some $j \in C$ can be dropped with the rest still a cover, drop it. The row for $C \setminus \{j\}$ has one item fewer on the left and a right side lower by 1:



Add $x_j \le 1$ to it and you get the row for $C$, so the smaller cover's row implies the larger one's, never the other way round. At an LP point it is broken by at least as much: the left side falls by $x_j^*$, at most 1, and the right side falls by exactly 1. The code drops items with the smallest LP value first, since they lower the left side the least:



Then extend. Let $a_{\max}$ be the largest weight in a cover $C$. Any item $j$ outside $C$ with $a_j \ge a_{\max}$ can join the left side with the right side unchanged:



$E(C)$ is the extended cover. Why is the row still valid? Take any $|C|$ items from $E(C)$. Swap each one that is not in $C$ for a different item of $C$ that is missing: the swap can only lower the weight, because the item swapped out weighs at least $a_{\max}$ and the one swapped in at most $a_{\max}$. After the swaps you hold exactly $C$, which is over the capacity, so the $|C|$ items you started with were over it too. Hence no whole plan takes $|C|$ items of $E(C)$.



An extended row has the same right side and more items on the left, so it is at least as strong as the plain one at every point where the extra items are positive. It is still not the strongest row the cover can give: lifting computes a separate coefficient for each extra item (some may get more than 1), at the price of solving a small knapsack per item. Lesson Bound tightening and valid inequalities, later in this section, goes further in that direction.


Finding the Most Violated Cover Is a Knapsack of Its Own


Tiny had 11 minimal covers to try. GridCap small's capital row has 36 items and $2^{36}$ sets of them, far too many to list. The search for a broken cover, called separation, must find one without listing them.


Rewrite the cover row as $\sum_{i \in C} (1 - x_i) \ge 1$: subtract each side from $|C|$. An LP point $x^*$ breaks it exactly when $\sum_{i \in C} (1 - x^*_i) < 1$. So the most broken cover is the set $C$ that makes $\sum_{i \in C} (1 - x^*_i)$ as small as possible while still being a cover. With a binary $z_i$ for "item $i$ is in the cover", that is



a knapsack problem again: pick items whose weights exceed the capacity, at least "cost". "$> b$" becomes "$\ge b + 1$" because the weights are whole numbers. If the optimum is below 1, its cover is broken; if it is 1 or more, no cover of this row is broken at $x^*$. Items with $x^*_i = 1$ cost nothing to include, and items at 0 cost a full unit, so the cheapest covers are built from the items the LP likes.



Solving a MILP to find each cut is affordable here because each separation knapsack is small; with thousands of rows, a greedy fill by $(1 - x^*_i) / a_i$ would find good covers faster, without the guarantee of finding the most broken one. Rounds of Cover Cuts Close Most of the Gap and Remove No Whole Plan


Put the pieces in a loop: solve the LP, separate one cover per row, shrink it, optionally extend it, add every row the LP point breaks, and repeat until a round adds nothing.






Four things to read in it.


  • The first round does most of the work. Five cuts take the bound from 21,149 to 20,454, 72% of the gap. Later rounds add one to four cuts each and gain less and less.
  • Extended covers close more with fewer cuts: 93.6% of the gap with 22 cuts, against 90.2% with 27. The extra items on the left make each row bite harder.
  • The loop stops short of the optimum. When no cover of any single row is broken, the remaining $95k (or $62k) of gap comes from the rows together, and no cover of one row sees it. Branch-and-bound finishes the job from a much tighter start.
  • No whole plan is lost. The MILP with every cut added still has the optimum $20,183k, as a valid inequality guarantees. The check is cheap and catches a wrong cut, which would show up as a lower optimum, not as an error.


Lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand shows that MILP solvers add cuts of their own before branching, and lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line shows where a log reports them. Writing covers into the model yourself is worth it when you know a structure the solver cannot see, or when you want a tighter LP for its own sake, for example to report a better bound.

Summary


  • A cover is a set of items that cannot all fit. If $\sum_{i \in C} a_i > b$, every whole plan obeys $\sum_{i \in C} x_i \le |C| - 1$: a valid inequality.
  • A cover row cuts off LP points the budget row allows. On GridCap tiny two cover cuts turned the LP bound 3,268.17 into the optimum 3,021 with no branching.
  • Shrink a cover to minimal, then extend it with heavier items. A minimal cover's row implies the larger cover's; items at least as heavy as the cover's heaviest join the left side for free.
  • Finding the most violated cover is a knapsack of its own: minimize $\sum (1 - x^*_i) z_i$ over covers; a value below 1 means a broken cover.
  • Rounds of cover cuts close most of the gap and remove no whole plan: 90.2% of GridCap small's gap with plain covers, 93.6% with extended ones, and the same $20,183k optimum.

Design Note: Test a Cut on the Whole Plans


A wrong cut does not crash anything. It removes a whole plan the model should allow, and the solver reports a worse optimum as if it were the truth. So every cut generator in this lesson is checked two ways: by brute force on tiny, where all 128 plans can be tried against each row, and by the MILP with all cuts added having the same optimum as without. Keep both checks next to any cut code you write; the second one costs one more solve.
