  15. Convex piecewise costs

GridCap's treasury offers the small portfolio's planners a credit line. The plan may spend past its $38,390k capital budget by drawing on it, and each $k drawn costs money: the first $1,500k at 0.10 per $k (the present value of the interest and fees), the next $1,500k at 0.25, and the last $2,000k at 0.45. Nothing above $5,000k may be drawn. Is it worth drawing, and how much? The cost of the loan is not one line through the origin: it bends at $1,500k and again at $3,000k. This lesson writes that bent cost into the selection model with no new binary variables, and shows exactly when that is allowed, in four rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course; the credit line is invented for this lesson.

A Piecewise-Linear Function Is Its Breakpoints

Add up the tiers to get the total cost $f(b)$ of drawing $b$ thousand dollars:

Drawn $b$ ($k)01,5003,0005,000
Total cost $f(b)$ ($k)01505251,425

Between two neighbouring columns the cost grows at the tier's rate, so $f$ is made of straight pieces joined at the columns. Such a function is piecewise linear. The columns are its breakpoints $(X_0, F_0), (X_1, F_1), \dots, (X_K, F_K)$ with $X_0 < X_1 < \dots < X_K$, and the $K$ straight pieces between them are its segments. The slope of segment $k$ (numbered from 1) is

here 0.10, 0.25 and 0.45, the three rates. Between $X_{k-1}$ and $X_k$ the function is $f(b) = F_{k-1} + s_k\,(b - X_{k-1})$. So the list of breakpoints says everything about $f$, and the programs of this module store a curve as exactly that: two tuples, xs and ys.

A MILP cannot contain $f(b)$ itself: "if $b \le 1500$ use this line, otherwise that one" is not a linear expression in $b$. The question of this module is how to say $y = f(b)$ with linear rows, and this lesson's answer is that for some curves it costs nothing at all.

A function is convex when its slopes never decrease from left to right: each segment is at least as steep as the one before. The credit line is convex (0.10, then 0.25, then 0.45), as is every cost that gets dearer per unit the more you buy; MIT's Applied Mathematical Programming (chapter 9) calls this case diseconomies of scale. A function whose slopes never increase is concave: each unit is cheaper, or worth less, than the one before. A straight line is both.

A Convex Cost Can Be Split Into Segments the Solver Fills in Order

Give each tier its own continuous variable: $\delta_k$, the amount drawn from tier $k$, between 0 and the tier's width $X_k - X_{k-1}$. Then

This is a linear model. It describes the credit line correctly only if the tiers are used in order: tier 2 only once tier 1 is full. Nothing in the rows says so. A plan with $\delta_1 = 0$ and $\delta_2 = 1{,}000$ is allowed by the rows and charged at 0.25 per $k, which no tier of the real line does for the first $1,000k drawn.

The order holds anyway, because of where the cost sits. The objective subtracts the cost, so the solver wants it as small as possible for whatever it draws. To draw $b$, it takes the cheapest $k first: all of tier 1 at 0.10 before any of tier 2 at 0.25. Filling in order is exactly what the real line charges, so the cheapest way the rows allow is the true cost.

The Same Cost Is the Largest of Its Lines

There is a second way to write a convex cost, with one variable instead of one per tier. Extend each segment into a whole straight line: line $k$ is $F_{k-1} + s_k\,(b - X_{k-1})$. For a convex function, every line lies on or below the curve, and at each $b$ the line of the segment that contains $b$ touches it. So the curve is the largest of its lines:

A maximum of linear terms is easy to bound from below: "$y$ is at least the largest line" is the same as "$y$ is at least every line", one row per segment.

These rows allow every $y \ge f(b)$. As before, the objective decides the rest: it subtracts $y$, so the solver lowers $y$ until a row stops it, and the row that stops it is the largest line, $f(b)$. The region above a function's graph is called its epigraph, so this is the epigraph form:

The bounds on $x$ carry the domain: nothing below $X_0$, nothing above $X_K = 5{,}000$. Without the upper bound the last line would happily price a $9,000k draw that the treasury never offered. Solved:

The same plan and the same $795k, with two continuous variables and three rows. The segment form uses one variable per segment and no extra rows (the widths are bounds); the epigraph form uses two variables and one row per segment. Both are exact for a convex cost the solver wants small, and neither needs a binary.

The cost does not have to sit in the objective. In a row such as $\text{spend} + y \le B$, a larger $y$ only makes the row harder to meet, so any plan the rows allow with some $y \ge f(b)$ is also allowed with $y = f(b)$: the row sees the true cost. What matters is the direction in which the rest of the model presses on $y$, which is the next rule.

Problem statement

A water utility plans next month's work for its contracted main-repair crew. Each job in jobs needs a known number of crew hours. The contract prices the crew's hours in tiers, given in tiers as (hours up to, $ per hour), in order:

Hours in the monthRate
up to 160$60 per hour
from 160 up to 200$90 per hour
from 200 up to 240$120 per hour

So a month of 170 hours costs 160 × 60 + 10 × 90. The contract allows no more hours than the last tier's limit. The month's labor cost must not exceed budget dollars.

The model in crew_month.py has a binary x[jid] per job (1 if the job is done this month). Write add_labor_rules(model, x, jobs, tiers, budget) so that the model allows exactly the months (which jobs) whose real labor cost fits the budget and whose hours stay within the contract. Add whatever variables you need. Work from tiers, not from the numbers in the table: the tests use other tiers too.

Write the month's hours as an expression first. Then write its labor cost as a function of the hours: what shape is it, and does the budget row press that cost up or down? That decides whether you need anything more than continuous variables.

The tests fix which jobs are done and ask the solver whether the model accepts the month, so any correct formulation passes. About eight lines.

Without Binaries, the Push Must Match the Shape


Now the treasury proposes the opposite schedule: the first $1,500k at 0.45 per $k, the next $1,500k at 0.25 and the last $2,000k at 0.10, as a lender does that charges a large arrangement fee and then little. The slopes fall, so this cost is concave. Solved with the same segment rows:



The solver drew $2,000k from the last tier and $1,500k from the middle one, and only $100k from the first, the dear one. The real line would have charged the first $1,500k at 0.45, so the plan's true cost is $1,110k, and the model charged $620k. It claims $26,861k for a plan really worth $26,371k. The cheapest way the rows allow is no longer the true cost, because filling the cheap tiers first now breaks the order.


Whether the rows are exact depends on two things together: the curve's shape, and which way the model pushes $y$. The program fixes $b$ at four values, lets the solver move $y$ down (as a cost in a maximization, or in a $\le$ row, does) or up (as a value in a maximization does), and compares the result with $f(b)$:



  • A convex curve pushed down is exact: the cheap segments come first, so filling them first is the true order. This is a convex cost being minimized, or kept within a budget.
  • A concave curve pushed up is exact for the mirror reason: the most valuable segments come first. This is a concave value being maximized, such as a benefit with diminishing returns.
  • The other two are wrong. A concave cost pushed down (economies of scale) and a convex value pushed up (increasing returns) both reward skipping to a later segment, and the rows cannot stop it.


For the epigraph form, the mirror of a convex cost is a concave value written as the smallest of its lines: $y \le$ every line, which is exact when $y$ is pushed up.


So before writing a curve without binaries, check two things: the slopes (rising or falling) and the push (is the solver trying to make $y$ smaller or larger?). When they match, the curve is free. When they do not, the solver must be told which segment it is on, and that takes binaries: lesson The lambda (SOS2-style) formulation built from binaries.


Two warnings about the push. First, it must be the same everywhere $y$ appears: a cost that is subtracted in the objective and also limited in a budget row is pushed down in both places, which is fine, but a quantity that is a cost in one row and a reward in another is pushed both ways and needs the exact forms of lesson The lambda (SOS2-style) formulation built from binaries. Second, an equality row ($y = \dots$ pinned to something else) pushes both ways too.

The Model


The selection model of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model on GridCap small with the credit line in segment form, marked. credit_line.py solves it with the rising tiers (exact) and with the falling tiers (not exact, as the last rule showed).

The model is exact because $s_1 \le s_2 \le s_3$ and the objective subtracts the cost. The epigraph version replaces the $\delta_k$ by $0 \le b \le X_K$ and a cost variable $y$, with rows $y \ge F_{k-1} + s_k(b - X_{k-1})$ for each $k$ (loan_line0 to loan_line2 in the code), the budget row reading $\le B + b$ and the objective subtracting $y$.


Summary


  • A piecewise-linear function is its breakpoints. Between neighbouring breakpoints it is a straight segment; a convex function's slopes never decrease, a concave function's never increase.
  • A convex cost can be split into segments the solver fills in order. One bounded variable per segment; the solver takes the cheap segments first, which is the true order. On GridCap small the credit line added $1,350k of value with no new binary.
  • The same cost is the largest of its lines. One row per segment, $y \ge$ each line, and $y$ pushed down lands on the curve.
  • Without binaries, the push must match the shape. Convex pushed down and concave pushed up are exact; the other two let the solver skip segments, as the falling tiers did ($620k charged, $1,110k real).

Design Note: Check the Shape in Code, Not by Eye


A curve that arrives as data (a tariff, a contract, a fitted curve) can change shape when someone edits one breakpoint. The library's Curve has convex() and concave(); a model builder that writes the cheap form should assert the shape it relies on, and fall back to the forms of lesson The lambda (SOS2-style) formulation built from binaries (or refuse) when the check fails, rather than return a plan that claims value it does not have. The falling-tier run above passed every solver check: optimal, feasible, no warning. Only the shape check catches it.
