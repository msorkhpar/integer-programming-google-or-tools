Two aging assets in GridCap's small portfolio each come with two candidate projects. Asset A001, a 45-year-old Plains transmission asset, can be replaced (P001, $2,640k) or repaired (P002, $460k). Asset A030, a Valley transmission asset, can be replaced (P030, $5,840k) or repaired (P031, $1,020k). The planners' question for each is the one asset managers ask every cycle: repair, replace, or leave it alone, and if replace, now or later? The options are mutually exclusive alternatives: the plan picks at most one of them for each asset. This lesson writes that as rows, makes "leave it alone" an option with a price, and handles the case where two options are allowed in sequence, in three rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. Its data puts each pair in an alternative group (G001 and G030); the two-year gap below is invented for the lesson.

Alternatives for One Asset Share One At-Most-One Row

Repairing an asset and replacing it in the same plan pays for the same risk reduction twice. The model stops that with one row per alternative group $g$, the "multiple-choice" row of capital budgeting (MIT 15.053, section 9.1):

with $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ as in lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows. Because it sums every start variable of every member, the row also says that no two years of the group are chosen: one member, in one year, or nothing. alternative_rows.py solves GridCap small with and without the two rows:

Without the row, the plan replaces A001 and repairs it, both in 2028, and reports $137k more NPV. That NPV is not real: GridCap values each option as if it were the only thing done to the asset, so the second option's value is counted on risk the first one already removed. The model has no way to know that; only the alternative row tells it.

The row is one row per group, not one per pair of members. When a group has three or more options (repair, refurbish, replace), every pair conflicts, and lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k showed that one row on the whole group allows the same 0/1 plans as the pair rows and gives the tighter LP bound.

Doing Nothing Is an Alternative Too, and It Has a Price

The alternative row allows a plan that does nothing to the asset: the sum is 0. That is often the right answer, but it is an answer the model gives silently, and it hides a cost the NPV does not carry: the risk of leaving an aging asset in service. Make the option explicit. Give each group a binary $k_g$, "keep the asset as it is", and turn the row into an equality:

Now every asset gets exactly one of its options, "keep" included, and the plan names the choice for each. With $k_g$ in the model, its price can go in the objective. Lesson Predictive vs. prescriptive: where PoF/RUL predictions stop and optimization starts measured an asset's risk as its chance of failure times its consequence of failure. Here the chance is that of failing within the plan's five years, given that the asset has survived to its present age (the Weibull curve's conditional form), and the price of keeping is

do_nothing.py solves with the generated budgets and with 2027's budget cut to $6,000k, each time with NPV alone and with the risk of keeping subtracted:

With the generated budgets both assets are worked on either way, so the price changes nothing. After the cut, the NPV-only plan leaves A001 alone and spends the money elsewhere. Priced, keeping A001 costs $547k of risk: the NPV-only plan is then worth $22,649k − $547k = $22,102k, and the solver prefers to repair A001 in 2027 for $460k, giving up $43k of NPV to remove $547k of risk. Where the two models differ, the second one's report reads "repair A001" and the first one's reads nothing at all, because the first never wrote down that leaving A001 alone was a decision.

keep is binary, but it need not be declared so: the equality forces it to $1 - \sum$ of binaries that sum to at most 1, so it is 0 or 1 whenever they are. Declaring it binary costs nothing and states the intent.

Problem statement

A transit agency reviews its bus fleet once a year. For each bus the plan picks one of four options: "keep" it as it is, "refurbish" it, replace it with a "diesel" bus, or replace it with an "electric" one. The model in fleet.py has a binary y[bid, option] for every bus and every option. The rules:

  • One option per bus. Every bus gets exactly one of the four options; "keep" is a decision like the others.
  • Chargers. The depot has chargers charging bays, so at most that many buses can be replaced with electric ones.
  • Age. A bus older than max_age years may not be kept: it must be refurbished or replaced. A bus exactly max_age years old may still be kept.

Write add_fleet_rules(model, y, buses, chargers, max_age) so that the model allows exactly the plans that keep the three rules.

Repair Now, Replace Later Is a Sequence: Exclusion Can Depend on the Years


"Replace now or replace later" needs no new row: the replace project's start variables are already alternatives to each other, through its once row. "Repair now, then replace later" is different: it is two members of the group in one plan, which the alternative row forbids. Suppose the asset engineers say a repair buys at least two years, so a replacement may follow a repair if it starts $G = 2$ or more years after it, and a repair never follows a replacement.


The exclusion now depends on the years. A replacement in year $t$ conflicts with a repair in year $s$ when $s > t - G$: a repair too close before it, in the same year, or after it. For each replacement year that is a set of variables of which at most one may be 1:



with "rep" the replace project and "fix" the repair. The repair's own variables already exclude each other (its once row), so each gap row is again a group in which every pair conflicts, written as one row. The alternative row on the whole group is dropped, because it would forbid the sequence.




repair_then_replace.py applies it to A001 (repair P002, replace P001). It lists the conflicts, solves four versions, and then fixes six pairs of years to see which each rule accepts:



The last six lines are the rule itself: the gap rows accept exactly the three sequences with a replacement two or more years after the repair, and "never both" accepts none. The solves show that allowing a sequence does not mean the plan uses it. With these values the best plan replaces A001 in 2028 under both rules; even with the repair fixed in 2027, the best plan adds no replacement, because a later replacement is worth less (P001's NPV falls from $1,401k in 2028 to $1,189k in 2029). "No rule" funds a repair and a replacement in the same year, the double count of the first section.


A model that allows a sequence needs one more thing from its data: the value of the replacement after a repair. GridCap gives each option's value as if it were done alone, so a plan that does both counts both in full. When you allow a sequence, ask for its value, or model it as its own option ("repair 2027, replace 2030") with its own NPV, which keeps the plain alternative row.

A university plans roof work on its buildings for 2027 to 2036. Each roof has two candidate projects, a patch and a replacement, and the model in roofs.py has a binary patch[r, s] for each year s the roof may be patched and replace[r, t] for each year t it may be replaced. Each project happens at most once (that row is given). The engineers' rules:


  • A roof may be patched, replaced, both, or neither.
  • A patch buys time: a replacement may follow a patch only if it starts at least gap years after the patch (a patch in 2028 and a replacement in 2028 + gap is fine).
  • A new roof comes with a warranty: the roof may not be patched in its replacement year, nor in the warranty years after it. A patch later than that is allowed (a replacement in 2029 with a warranty of 3 rules out patches in 2029 to 2032).


Write add_sequence_rules(model, patch, replace, roofs, gap, warranty), where roofs maps each roof to a pair (patch_years, replace_years), so that the model allows exactly the plans that keep the rules.

The Model


The planning model with this lesson's rows marked. do_nothing.py adds the keep variables and choose rows; repair_then_replace.py replaces the alternative row of G001 by its gap rows.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years of project $i$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; for a group allowed in sequence, its repair project "fix" and replace project "rep".


Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $\text{risk}_g = \text{PoF}_g \times \text{CoF}_g$, the risk of keeping group $g$'s asset as it is ($k); $G = 2$, the fewest years from a repair to a replacement.

Without the keep variables, choose$_g$ is the alternative row alt$_g$: $\sum_{i \in g} \text{started}(i) \le 1$. A group allowed in sequence has gap rows and no choose or alt row.

Summary


  • Alternatives for one asset share one at-most-one row. Without it the plan repaired and replaced A001 in the same year and claimed $137k of NPV that does not exist.
  • Doing nothing is an alternative too, and it has a price. A keep variable makes the row an equality, names the choice in the report, and lets the risk of keeping enter the objective; after a budget cut it turned "leave A001 alone" into "repair A001".
  • Repair now, replace later is a sequence: exclusion can depend on the years. One row per replacement year, over the repair years too close to it, replaces the group row. Value the sequence, not each option alone.

Design Note: Keep Is a Decision, So Report It


Planners and auditors ask about the assets a plan did not touch as often as about those it did. A model with an explicit keep variable per asset answers from the solution itself: every asset has exactly one chosen option. Without it, the report has to infer "kept" from an absence, and an asset that was never in a group at all looks the same as one the optimizer decided to leave. Lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out explains such decisions to planners by forcing an option in and out.
