GridCap's planners split the small portfolio's 40 projects into two kinds before they plan anything. Four are must-do projects: P003 and P010 (Valley vegetation clearing), P017 (a Coast transmission repair) and P039 (a Coast undergrounding job) are owed to a regulator or a safety commitment, and the plan has to fund them whatever they are worth. The other 36 are discretionary: each is funded only if it earns its place against the others. A third kind of rule sits between the two: "every aging asset with a repair and a replace option must get one of them", or "at least one Ridge circuit goes underground", where the group is mandated but no single project is.

A planning group is a named set of projects that the plan treats by one rule. This lesson writes the must-do group, the discretionary group and the mandated groups as rows of the multi-period model of lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows to Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps, checks before the solve that the mandates can fit, and measures what each mandate costs, in four rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course; the budget cuts and the mandates on groups below are invented for the lesson.

A Must-Do Is an Equality Row, Not a Large Weight

The model is the one of lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows: a binary $x_{i,t}$ for each project $i$ and each year $t$ it may start in, and $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$, which is 1 if project $i$ is funded in some year and 0 if it is not. The two groups are told apart by one character of their row:

with $M$ the set of must-do projects. The discretionary row says "at most once": the project may be dropped. The must-do row says "exactly once": every plan the model accepts funds it, in one of its start years, and the model chooses which.

There is a second way people write a mandate, and it is common in inherited models: leave every project discretionary and add a large bonus to each must-do's value, so that the solver "wants" it badly enough. must_do_rows.py builds both, with a bonus of $100,000k per must-do:

It solves both with the budgets as generated, then again after cutting the 2030 and 2031 budgets to $4,000k each. The NPV column is computed from the plan itself, without the bonus:

With the generated budgets the two agree on the plan, and only the objective differs, by $400,000k of bonus that no one can spend. After the cut they disagree on the one thing that matters. P017 costs $5,310k in a single year, 2030 or 2031, and both years now have $4,000k. No plan can fund it, so no plan keeps every rule of the equality model, and the solver says so with the status INFEASIBLE (lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k defined it: no plan satisfies every row). The weighted model has no rule to break. It returns OPTIMAL and a plan that quietly drops P017, with an objective of $321,216k that is neither the plan's value nor anything else a planner can use.

INFEASIBLE is the right answer here. The planner needs to hear "the mandates do not fit the budget", and a plan that drops a regulatory commitment without saying so is worse than no plan. A weight also has to be chosen: too small, and the solver drops the must-do whenever discretionary work is worth more; large, and the objective is mostly bonus, which makes the solver's gap (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap) a percentage of a number that means nothing. The rule to keep is simple: what the plan must do is a constraint, and what the plan would like to do is a term in the objective.

Must-Dos Claim Their Money First, and You Can Check That Before Solving

Every plan funds the must-dos, so their spend comes out of each year's budget before any discretionary project is considered. Part of that is known from the data alone, before a model is built. A must-do with one start year spends a fixed amount in fixed years. A must-do with a window of several start years can move, so in a given year it is certain to spend only the least it spends there over all its start years, which is often 0. Summing that least spend over the must-dos gives a floor on each year's must-do spend:

Only P003 has a single start year (2031), so only its $140k is certain. The others can move, and the yearly floor does not see them. Two more checks see more. One asks whether each must-do, on its own, has a start year whose spend fits every year's budget; if not, no plan can fund it, whatever else the plan does. The other compares the must-dos' total cost with the budgets' total.

After the solve, the same split can be read off the plan: what the must-dos spent in each year, and what was left to the discretionary group. Then the program tries the precheck on three budget cuts and solves each:

In the plan, P017 takes $5,310k of 2030's $8,610k, which leaves $3,250k for discretionary work there (the other $50k is unspent). The first cut is the one from the previous section, and the precheck names the project at fault, which the solver's single word INFEASIBLE does not. The second cut leaves room for every must-do. The third shows the limit of the checks. Each must-do fits on its own: P017 cannot start in 2030 ($5,310k is more than $5,000k), but it fits in 2031 ($5,310k is at most $5,400k). Together they do not: in 2031, P017's $5,310k and P003's $140k make $5,450k, which is $50k over. No check that looks at one project, or at one year's floor, sees that.

So the checks are necessary, not sufficient: when one fails, the model is infeasible, and when all pass, it may still be. They are cheap and they name the culprit, so run them first; when they pass and the solver still says INFEASIBLE, lessons Validating inputs before the solve and IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo find the rows that conflict.

A Group Can Be Mandated When No Single Project Is

Some mandates name a group. Two are common in asset planning:

  • Every asset that is failing gets addressed, by whichever of its options the plan picks. In GridCap small, assets A001 and A030 each have a replace and a repair project, in alternative groups G001 (P001, P002) and G030 (P030, P031). "Address each asset" is exactly one member of each group: $\sum_{i \in g} \text{started}(i) = 1$.
  • At least one of a set gets done. "At least one Ridge circuit is put underground in the plan", with P024 and P037 the Ridge undergrounding projects, is $\text{started}(P024) + \text{started}(P037) \ge 1$.

Neither is a must-do on a project: no single project in either group has to be funded. Each is one row on a sum of started terms, the counting rows of lesson At-least-k, at-most-k, exactly-k written over start variables:

The program also writes the mistake that turns a group mandate into project mandates: every member made a must-do. It solves each version and prints, for the six projects involved, the year each starts in (- for not funded):

  • Address each asset costs nothing on these budgets: the best plan already replaces A001 (P001) and repairs A030 (P031). The row still belongs in the model. It is what stops a later plan, with other budgets, from leaving a failing asset untouched.
  • Underground one costs $64k. The best plan funded neither project, and the cheapest way to meet the rule is P037 in 2031.
  • Underground both is the mistake: it costs $273k, more than four times as much, because it demands a project the regulator never asked for.
  • Both G030 members is the same mistake on an alternative group, and here it is fatal. The alternative row says $\text{started}(P030) + \text{started}(P031) \le 1$, the two must-do rows say each is 1, and no plan keeps all three.

Write a mandate at the level the regulator wrote it. "One of these" is one row on the group; "each of these" is one row per project.

Problem statement

A school district plans its building work for 2027 to 2030. The model in schools.py has a binary x[pid, t] for each project and each year t in its starts (1 if the project starts in year t). Three rules come from the state and the district's own policy:

  • Once. No project starts more than once.
  • Required work. required maps a project to a deadline year. A required project must be done, and it must start in its deadline year or earlier.
  • Accessibility. access maps a school to its accessibility projects (ramps, lifts, doors), and targets maps a school to a pair (k, year): by the end of year, at least k of that school's accessibility projects must have started. Which ones is up to the plan.

Write add_mandates(model, x, projects, required, access, targets) so that the model allows exactly the plans that keep all three rules.

The model so far, with $P$ the projects and $T_i$ the start years of project $i$:

Name the remaining sets and parameters (the required projects and their deadlines, the schools, each school's access projects, its count and its year), then write your rows, each with its quantifier and a label, and only then code them. Before you write the access row, ask which of a project's start variables should count toward a target year.

The tests fix every x[pid, t] through its bounds and ask the solver whether the model accepts the plan, so any correct formulation passes. About ten lines.

The Cost of a Mandate Is the NPV You Give Up for It


A planner will ask what the mandates cost. The question has an exact answer: the NPV of the best plan without a mandate, less the NPV of the best plan with it. Removing a must-do row does not remove the project; it becomes discretionary (its row turns into "at most once"), and the solver keeps it if it is worth its money:






Three things in this table are worth saying out loud to a planner.


  • A mandate the plan would meet anyway costs nothing. P003 and P010, small vegetation jobs, stay in the plan when their own row is lifted.
  • P017 is the expensive one. Without its row the plan drops it and spends its $5,310k on work worth $1,843k more. That number is the price of the Coast repair commitment, in the model's own units.
  • The costs do not add up. One at a time they sum to $1,843k + $524k = $2,367k; lifted together they cost $2,386k. Each cost is measured against the same plan, but when several rows are lifted at once the solver rearranges around all of them together, through the budgets they share. Even P010, which costs nothing on its own, leaves the plan when every mandate is lifted. Report each mandate's cost and the joint cost, and never the sum as the joint cost.


This is the "forcing in and out" idea that section 9 turns into a report (lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out): the value of a rule is measured by solving with and without it.

Problem statement


A city parks department picks this year's projects under one budget. Each project has a cost and a value (both in $k), and some are mandated: a court order or a safety inspection requires them. The council asks the question every planner hears: what do the mandates cost us?


Write two functions in parks.py:


  • best_value(projects, budget, enforced): the value of the best selection that fits the budget, in which every project in enforced is funded. A mandated project that is not in enforced is treated like any other project: the selection may fund it or not.
  • mandate_costs(projects, budget): a dict with one entry per mandated project, its cost, and one entry "all", the cost of every mandate together. A mandate's cost is how much more the best selection is worth when that mandate (or, for "all", every mandate) is lifted.


Every instance in the tests can fund all its mandates within the budget.


Write the model of best_value first, with $P$ the projects, $E \subseteq P$ the enforced ones, $c_i$ and $v_i$ the cost and value, and $B$ the budget: the variables with their domains, the objective, and each row with its label. Then decide, for each entry of mandate_costs, which enforced sets it compares. Before you code "all", ask whether the single costs can tell you its value.


The tests compare your numbers with values worked out by hand on small instances. About fifteen lines.

The Model


The planning model of this lesson, in one place. planning_model.build writes the GridCap rules; the group mandates are added by group_mandates.py, one at a time.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ the groups whose asset must be addressed; $U \subseteq P$ a set of which at least one must be funded (here P024 and P037); $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.


Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work, $k = 0, 1, \dots$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. We write $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$.

The weighted version in must_do_rows.py drops every must$_i$ row, writes once$_i$ for all of $P$, and maximizes $\sum_{i,t} \text{npv}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t} + W \sum_{i \in M} \text{started}(i)$ with $W = 100{,}000$; this lesson shows why not to.


Summary


  • A must-do is an equality row, not a large weight. The weight hides an infeasible mandate behind an OPTIMAL plan that drops it; the equality reports INFEASIBLE.
  • Must-dos claim their money first, and you can check that before solving. Check each must-do alone, each year's least must-do spend, and the total. The checks are necessary, not sufficient.
  • A group can be mandated when no single project is. "One of these" is one row on the group's sum; making each member a must-do over-mandates, and on an alternative group is infeasible.
  • The cost of a mandate is the NPV you give up for it. Solve with and without the row. Costs of several mandates do not add up.

Design Note: Keep Mandates in the Data, Not in the Code


GridCap stores the must-do flag on each project, and build turns it into rows. A mandate on a group (address this asset, underground one of these) deserves the same treatment: a table of groups with a kind ("exactly one", "at least one") and a list of members, read by one function that writes the rows. Then a planner can see every mandate in one place, a test can check that each one is in the model (lesson Unit-testing one constraint at a time), and lifting a mandate to price it is a change of data, not of code.
