GridCap's capital plan runs every night, and the job keeps each solve's log next to its result (lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line's design note). Most nights nobody reads it. This lesson is about the nights someone should: what in a log says that a plan rests on bad numbers, before a planner finds out from a budget that does not add up. Lessons Reading an LP solver log and Reading a full MIP log line by line read an LP log and a MIP log from top to bottom. This one reads only the lines that carry numerical warnings, on the models of lessons Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters and Coefficient ranges and scaling, and ends with the check no log line replaces: solve again a different way and compare. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

The Model

The bridge-loan model of lessons Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters and Coefficient ranges and scaling, restated whole. $o$ and $r$ are the units of the objective and of the money rows per $k, and $\bar M_y$ is the switch row's big-M.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y = \{2027, \dots, 2031\}$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$.

Parameters. $v_i$ the NPV of project $i$ ($k); $c_{i,y}$ its spend in year $y$ ($k); $B_y$ the budget ($k); $L_y = B_y/10$ the credit line ($k); $F = 300$ the fee ($k); $\rho = 0.05$ the interest per $k borrowed; $o$, $r$ and $\bar M_y$ as above.

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$; $h_y \in \{0, 1\}$ and $\ell'_y \ge 0$ (the loan, in the rows' unit) for $y \in Y$.

The three variants: TIGHT ($k, $\bar M_y = L_y$), HUGE_M ($k, $\bar M_y = 10^9$) and MIXED (objective in dollars, rows in $M, $\bar M_y = 10^9$). All three allow the same plans, and the best of them is worth $23,161.5k.

HiGHS Warns About Large Costs and Bounds, but Not About the Matrix, So Read the Ranges Yourself

A HiGHS log opens with the model's size and its four coefficient ranges (lesson Coefficient ranges and scaling), then any WARNING lines about them, then presolve. Which numbers draw a warning? warnings_grid.py builds one tiny model per test, with all numbers 1 except one, and keeps the warnings:

The HiGHS in OR-Tools 9.15 warned about costs, right-hand sides and bounds at $10^7$ and above and at $10^{-7}$, and not at $10^5$ or $10^6$. It printed no warning for any matrix coefficient, not even $10^{11}$, though the matrix is where a big-M lives. That is why lesson Coefficient ranges and scaling's model with the objective in $k and $10^9$ in its matrix ran without a single warning.

So the first lines to read are the ranges, not the warnings. Read the Matrix line as a span: a largest value more than $10^9$ times the smallest is past the limit Gurobi's numerical guidelines give, and anything above $10^6$ is worth a look. Then read each extreme as a question: which data gave a coefficient this large, and is it in the unit you meant?

A Nonzero Count Lower Than You Built Means a Coefficient Was Dropped

The first line of the log describes the model as HiGHS received it, before presolve (lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line), and counts its nonzeros. HiGHS's option small_matrix_value, default $10^{-9}$, is documented as a lower limit: matrix entries at or below it are treated as zero. dropped.py builds the same one-row model with $a = 10^{-8}$ and $a = 10^{-9}$, and compares the nonzeros it built with the count in HiGHS's first line:

With $a = 10^{-8}$, $x = 1000$ uses $10^{-5}$ of the row, so $y$ cannot be 1 as well, and HiGHS sets it to 0. With $a = 10^{-9}$ HiGHS counts one nonzero, not two: the coefficient never reached the solver, there was no warning, and the plan breaks the row as written by $10^{-6}$. A coefficient this small usually means a unit gone wrong (a cost per dollar in a row of billions, say), and the only trace in the log is a count one lower than the model you built. Compare it with sum(len(list(model.row_nonzeros(r))) for r in model.linear_constraints()), as dropped.py does. Large coefficients are not dropped the same way: the $10^9$ of lesson Coefficient ranges and scaling's models reached HiGHS, and its Matrix line printed it.

The Report's Violation Lines Are the Solver's Audit of Its Own Plan

At the end of a MIP log, HiGHS's solving report checks the plan it returns against the model it solved, and prints the largest violation of three kinds: of a variable's bounds, of integrality, and of a row. report.py prints that block for lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters's two runs, the tight model with defaults and the huge $M$ with presolve off:

Both say feasible, and both are, within HiGHS's tolerances: $8.89 \times 10^{-7}$ is below its integrality tolerance of $10^{-6}$. The difference is in the numbers. A model whose binaries multiply ordinary numbers lands its binaries on 0 and 1, and reports zeros. A nonzero integrality violation says a binary was left between whole numbers, and lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters showed that this one is a $8.9 \times 10^{-7}$ switch worth an $889k loan. The violation line does not say that it matters; it says where to look: find the binaries that are not whole, and the coefficients they meet.

A useful rule for a nightly job is to flag any nonzero violation on a model with switched rows, and any violation near the tolerance on any model, and to keep the flag with the plan. Rerunning with a tighter tolerance is not the fix: lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters uses it only to diagnose, and the fix is to make the model's numbers fit the tolerance.

Problem statement

A regional airline re-optimizes its maintenance schedule every night with HiGHS and keeps each solve's log as a list of lines. Nobody reads the logs, so the operations team wants a scanner that returns the lines worth reading, as short findings stored with each plan.

Write scan(lines, max_span=1e6). It returns a list of findings, in the order their lines appear:

  • a line that starts with WARNING: (after leading spaces) gives "warning: <text>", where <text> is the rest of the line after WARNING:, with spaces trimmed at both ends;
  • the range line that starts with Matrix, such as Matrix [1e-01, 1e+09], gives "matrix span <ratio>" when the largest value divided by the smallest is above max_span, with the ratio written as f"{ratio:.0e}" (so 1e+10);
  • a report line such as 8.89e-07 (int. viol.) gives "<kind> violation <value>" when its value is above 0, with <kind> bound, integrality or row for (bound viol.), (int. viol.) and (row viol.), and <value> the number exactly as the line writes it.

Every other line gives nothing. The tests use their own logs, in HiGHS's format. About fifteen lines.

SCIP Says When Its Best Solution Breaks a Row of Your Model


SCIP works on a transformed copy of the model, the result of its presolve, and translates its best solution back at the end. When the translated solution breaks a row of the original model, it says so after its report. report.py solves the HUGE_M model with SCIP's defaults:






The last four lines are the warning. They print the row (switch_2027, written as $\ell_{2027} - 10^9 h_{2027} \le 0$), each variable's value in brackets, and the size of the violation. The values are rounding noise: a loan of $-1.1 \times 10^{-12}$ and a switch of $-1.3 \times 10^{-15}$, both zero in any sense that matters. But the switch is multiplied by $10^9$, and $-10^9 \times -1.28 \times 10^{-15} = 1.28 \times 10^{-6}$ is above SCIP's tolerance for a row whose right-hand side is 0. Lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters measured that noise at about $10^{-16}$ of a number; $10^9$ brought it up to the tolerance.


The plan itself is right: $23,161.5k, the true optimum. What the warning says is that the model cannot be checked reliably, because any value near zero on that binary is magnified a billion times. And MathOpt's result carries none of it: the status is OPTIMAL and the objective is right. Only the log has the warning, which is one more reason to keep and scan it.

Problem statement


A freight company runs its depot-opening model with HiGHS some nights and SCIP others. HiGHS's report prints the largest bound, integrality and row violation of the plan it returns; SCIP's does not. The team wants the same three numbers for every plan, whichever solver produced it.


Write audit(model, values) for a MathOpt model and a solution's values (a dict from each variable to its value, as result.variable_values() returns it). It returns a dict with three keys, each the largest violation of its kind, or 0.0 when there is none:


  • "bound": how far a variable's value is outside its bounds, below the lower or above the upper;
  • "integrality": for integer variables only, how far the value is from the nearest whole number;
  • "row": how far a row's activity (the sum of its coefficients times the values) is outside its bounds, below the lower side or above the upper.


model.variables() and model.linear_constraints() yield the variables and rows; a variable has lower_bound, upper_bound and integer; a row has lower_bound and upper_bound, and model.row_nonzeros(row) with row.get_coefficient(variable) gives its terms. Infinite bounds never count as broken. The tests build their own models and values, with no solve. About twelve lines.

Two Settings That Should Agree and Do Not Are a Numerical Symptom


The warnings above are the solver's. The strongest test is your own: solve the same model in ways that must give the same optimum, and compare. Presolve on or off, HiGHS or SCIP: none of these changes which plans the model allows, so with a gap tolerance of 0 every run must report the same best value. Gurobi's numerical guidelines give the same test: re-solve with another setting, and treat a changed objective or status as a strong sign of numerical trouble. cross_check.py runs all three variants four ways:






The tight model gives one answer four times. Each model with $M = 10^9$ gives a wrong one in one of the four ways: HiGHS without presolve on HUGE_M (the free loans of lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters), and SCIP without presolve on MIXED, $507.4k too high. Both wrong runs ended OPTIMAL, with nothing in MathOpt's result to set them apart. With the defaults alone, all three models gave the right value, so a team that only ever ran the defaults would never have seen it. The trouble was in the models all along; the second run is what showed it.


Which settings to vary is a choice: presolve and the solver are cheap to change. Lesson Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver adds seeds and thread counts to the settings a run can vary. Run the comparison when a model changes, not every night: it doubles the solve time, and a model that passed it keeps passing until its numbers change.


Summary


  • HiGHS warns about large costs and bounds, but not about the matrix, so read the ranges yourself. A $10^{11}$ matrix coefficient drew no warning.
  • A nonzero count lower than you built means a coefficient was dropped. HiGHS treats entries at or below $10^{-9}$ as zero, silently.
  • The report's violation lines are the solver's audit of its own plan. A nonzero integrality violation, even below the tolerance, points at a binary between whole numbers.
  • SCIP says when its best solution breaks a row of your model. "best solution is not feasible in original problem" appeared in the log while MathOpt reported OPTIMAL.
  • Two settings that should agree and do not are a numerical symptom. The tight model agreed four ways; each model with $M = 10^9$ disagreed once.

Design Note: Scan the Log Like a Test Report


A log is output nobody reads until something breaks. Treat its numerical lines the way a build treats test failures: a short function that reads the lines kept with each solve and returns a list of findings (warnings, a matrix span above a limit, nonzero violations, SCIP's not-feasible-in-original line), stored with the plan and shown to whoever approves it. The function is cheap; the night it finds something, it is the difference between a planner trusting a plan and a planner finding a $980k overspend.
