A colleague hands over a version of the bridge-loan model from lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters. The NPVs came from the finance system in dollars, so the objective is in dollars. The budgets came from the board pack in millions, so the budget rows and the loan are in $M. And the switch row got $M = 10^9$, "big enough for anything". It solves, and HiGHS returns the right plan. It also prints two warnings, and its log shows matrix coefficients from about 0.1 to $10^9$.

Lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters showed why such numbers are risky: tolerances are absolute, and a huge coefficient on a binary turns the integrality tolerance into room. This lesson shows how to measure a model's numbers, what the solver's own warnings say about them, and how to rescale the model, one unit at a time, until its numbers fit the tolerances, without changing the plan. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

Four Ranges Describe a Model's Numbers: Matrix, Cost, Bound and RHS

Lesson Reading an LP solver log read the four coefficient ranges at the top of a HiGHS log: for each kind of number in the model, the smallest and the largest absolute value. The kinds are the matrix (the coefficients in the rows), the cost (the objective's coefficients), the bounds (variables' finite bounds) and the RHS (the rows' finite right-hand sides). Zeros and infinite values are left out, and so are bounds of 0, so a binary's bounds $[0, 1]$ add only the 1.

The same numbers can be computed from a MathOpt model, which a test can then check:

model.row_nonzeros(row) lists the variables with a nonzero coefficient in a row, and row.get_coefficient(v) reads one. ranges.py prints the function's result for the TIGHT model next to HiGHS's log:

The two agree; HiGHS rounds each value to one significant digit. The span of a range is its largest value divided by its smallest, the number of times the largest is bigger. Each number can be traced to the model:

  • Matrix, 1 to 5,680: the 1s are the loan's coefficient in a budget row, the switch's and credit's coefficients, and the must and after rows; 5,680 is the largest one-year spend of a project, in $k. With the tight $M$, the switch coefficients are the credit lines, at most 889.
  • Cost, 0.05 to 4,374: 0.05 is the interest on one $k of loan; 4,374 is the largest NPV.
  • Bound, 1: only the binaries have a finite nonzero bound. The loans have none above.
  • RHS, 1 to 8,890: the must rows' 1s, and the budgets.

Every span is below $10^5$. The rows' numbers sit between 1 and $10^4$, where lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters's tolerances are a tenth of a cent and floats keep twelve digits to spare.

Problem statement

A hospital network's staffing model prints Matrix [1e-04, 1e+08] at the top of its HiGHS log. The span is far too wide, but the log does not say which rows and variables hold the extremes, and the model has thousands of rows. The team wants a function that names them.

Write matrix_extremes(model) for a MathOpt model. It returns two triples, the smallest and the largest matrix entry by absolute value, each as (row name, variable name, coefficient), with the coefficient as written in the row, sign included. Only the rows' coefficients count: the objective's coefficients and the variables' bounds are other ranges. Every row in the tests has at least one nonzero coefficient.

model.linear_constraints() yields the rows, model.row_nonzeros(row) yields the variables with a nonzero coefficient in a row, and row.get_coefficient(variable) reads one coefficient. About five lines.

A Model Written in Mixed Units Spreads Its Numbers Over Many Powers of Ten


The colleague's MIXED model is the same model in other units. rescale.py prints its ranges and every warning line in HiGHS's log:



The plan is right: $23,161.5k, as lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters's tight model found, and one loan, $750k in 2030. The numbers are not:


  • The matrix spans ten powers of ten, from about 0.1 (a small project's spend in $M) to $10^9$ (the big-M). Gurobi's numerical guidelines say the ratio of the largest to the smallest matrix coefficient should ideally stay below $10^6$ and never exceed $10^9$; this one is above it.
  • The costs reach $4 \times 10^6$, an NPV in dollars. The same guidelines ask for objective values of good plans below about $10^6$; this objective is $2.3 \times 10^7$.
  • The RHS is small, 0.8 to 9: the rows are in $M, where a row's tolerance of $10^{-6}$ is worth $1 to HiGHS (lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters's table).


HiGHS warned about the costs, and suggested a fix. It said nothing about the $10^9$ in the matrix. Lesson Spotting numerical trouble in a log measures which numbers HiGHS warns about; here the point is that the model is badly scaled whether or not a warning appears.


Rescaling Multiplies a Row, a Column or the Objective, and Leaves the Plan Unchanged


Rescaling changes the units in which a model's numbers are written. It comes in three kinds, each of which leaves the set of allowed plans, and the best plan, exactly as they were:


  • A row times a positive number. $a^\top x \le b$ and $(s\,a)^\top x \le s\,b$, with $s > 0$, allow the same $x$. Writing a budget row in $k instead of $M multiplies it by 1,000.
  • A column times a positive number. Replacing a variable $\ell$ by $\ell = \ell'/r$ multiplies its coefficient in every row and in the objective by $1/r$, and its bounds by $r$. Measuring the loan in $k instead of $M is that substitution.
  • The objective times a positive number. $\max\, f(x)$ and $\max\, s f(x)$, with $s > 0$, have the same best plans; only the reported value is $s$ times larger. Writing NPV in $k instead of dollars multiplies the objective by $1/1000$.


In the model above, $o$ is an objective scaling and $r$ scales every money row and the loan's column together. rescale.py builds the model four times, changing one thing at a time, and solves each with HiGHS's defaults:






Step by step:


  • The objective in $k divides every cost by 1,000: the cost range falls from $[3 \times 10^4, 4 \times 10^6]$ to $[30, 4{,}000]$, the reported objective falls from 23,161,500 to 23,161.5, and the warnings stop. The interest coefficient is $o\rho/r$: with $o = 1$ and $r = 10^{-3}$ it is 50, the interest in $k on one $M of loan.
  • The rows in $k multiply every money row and the loan's column by 1,000: the RHS moves from 0.8–9 to 1–8,890, and the smallest matrix entry from 0.1 to 1. In $k a row's tolerance of $10^{-6}$ is a tenth of a cent instead of a dollar. The matrix still reaches $10^9$.
  • The tight M replaces $10^9$ by the credit line. The matrix span drops from $10^9$ to 5,680.


The plan never changed: the same 27 projects, the same $750k loan in 2030, and the same value once converted to $k with in_k. That is what rescaling must do. A test of a rescaled model should check both halves: the ranges shrank, and the plans allowed and refused are the same.


A Valid M Is Part of Scaling: Replace 1e9 With the Bound the Data Give


Units cannot fix the last step. $10^9$ is not a unit problem: in $k, $M or dollars, it is far larger than any loan the model allows. The data say how large a loan can be: the credit line, $L_y$. Lesson Deriving big-M from bounds derived such an $M$ from bounds, and showed it is the smallest valid one: the credit row already caps the loan at $L_y$, so the switch row needs no more.


The step table shows why it belongs to scaling. After the units were fixed, the $10^9$ alone kept the matrix span at $10^9$; with the credit line it fell to 5,680. And lesson Floating point, tolerances, and why 1e-6 matters showed what that span costs: a switch of $10^{-6}$ times $10^9$ is room for $1,000k of loan, more than any credit line, while $10^{-6}$ times 889 is less than a dollar.


The second line of the step table makes one more point. The model "objective in $k" still has $10^9$ in its matrix and 0.1 at the other end, and HiGHS printed no warning about it. A model without warnings is not a well-scaled model; the ranges are the measure.

HiGHS Can Scale the Objective and Bounds for You, Not the Matrix


HiGHS's warning offered a second fix: "setting the user_objective_scale option to -3". HiGHS's option list describes user_objective_scale and user_bound_scale as exponents of a power-of-two scaling of the objective and of the bounds. user_scale.py solves the MIXED model with it:






HiGHS multiplied every cost by $2^{-3} = 1/8$, which brought them under its warning threshold, solved, and reported the objective in the model's own units again: MathOpt returns 23,161,500, as before. A power of two changes only a float's exponent, so this scaling adds no rounding error of its own.


It is a convenience, not a substitute for choosing units. The matrix line still reads $[10^{-1}, 10^9]$: no option rescales the colleague's big-M away. HiGHS also scales the matrix internally while it solves (its option simplex_scale_strategy chooses how, with equilibration the default), but that is the solver's working copy. The model you wrote, the tolerance that applies to its rows, and the plan you round are still in your units, and only you know that the credit line is the right $M$.


Summary


  • Four ranges describe a model's numbers: matrix, cost, bound and RHS. Compute them from the model, compare them with the log, and know which data each extreme comes from.
  • A model written in mixed units spreads its numbers over many powers of ten. Dollars next to millions and an $M$ of $10^9$ spread the matrix over ten powers of ten and put the objective near $10^7$, outside the ranges the numerical guidelines recommend.
  • Rescaling multiplies a row, a column or the objective, and leaves the plan unchanged. The same 27 projects and the same loan came back at every step; only the numbers moved.
  • A valid M is part of scaling: replace 1e9 with the bound the data give. The credit line cut the matrix span from $10^9$ to 5,680, which no change of units could do.
  • HiGHS can scale the objective and bounds for you, not the matrix. user_objective_scale silenced the warning; the $10^9$ stayed.

Design Note: Scale in the Data Layer, Once


Each rescaling step above changed two numbers in one place, Units, and the model builder applied them everywhere. That is the shape to keep: the builder takes data in one unit and never mixes units itself, and conversions for reports happen after the solve (in_k). A model whose rows are written by different people from different systems, as the colleague's was, needs that rule more than any other.
