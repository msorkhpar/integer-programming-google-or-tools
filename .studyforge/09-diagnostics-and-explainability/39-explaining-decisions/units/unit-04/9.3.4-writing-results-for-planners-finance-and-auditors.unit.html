  15. Writing results for three readers

The 2029 budget cut of lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted has been run, diffed and priced. Now it has to be written up, and for three readers at once. The planning team wants to know which projects moved and why, so it can tell the fleet teams. Finance wants the money by year and what the cut costs in value. The internal auditor, who will one day stand behind this plan in a rate case, wants to know exactly what was run, on which inputs, and whether anyone can check it. Lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer set out what each group needs from an optimizer; this lesson writes one result three ways and states the rules that make each version trustworthy.

The rule that holds them together comes first: every number in every version comes from data the program produced, collected once in a record. A memo typed by hand from a screen of output is how a plan's NPV ends up as $23,068k in one email and $23,086k in another. Printing fewer numbers does not fix that: each reader still needs theirs, and any number copied by hand can be copied wrong.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course; no number in it describes a real one.

The Model

The result is the cut scenario of lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted: the model of lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out with $B_{2029} = 6{,}000$, solved to a relative gap of 0, compared with the base scenario ($B_{2029} = 8{,}760$), and with each changed decision priced by a forced re-solve of the cut scenario (lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out's $\Delta_{\text{in}}$, $\Delta_{\text{out}}$ and $\Delta_{\text{at}}$).

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years, 2027 to 2031; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; $F^{+}$ and $F^{-}$ the projects forced in and out (empty in both scenarios, except in a pricing re-solve).

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$, with $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ and $\text{by}(i, t) = \sum_{s \in T_i,\, s \le t} x_{i,s}$.

One Record per Run, and Every Text Is Written From It

record.make_record solves the scenario and the one it was copied from, diffs them, prices every changed decision with a forced re-solve, checks the plan, reruns it, and returns one dictionary. Its last lines show what goes in:

three_readers.py builds the record for the cut and prints some of its fields, as JSON:

Three properties make the record the right thing to write from.

  • It is data, not text. A reason is stored as facts (left: 310, needs: 1510), not as a sentence, so each reader's version can word it its own way and none can change the numbers.
  • It is complete. Each writer reads the record and nothing else: no writer solves, queries the model or looks at a screen. If a version needs a number, the number goes into the record first.
  • It can be kept. JSON goes into a file or a database beside the scenario's snapshot (lesson Result schemas planners can consume designs result schemas, Storing scenarios and results in SQLite stores them), so the texts can be written again, or checked, years later.

Note bound: MathOpt's best_objective_bound(), the solver's proven limit on how good any plan could be. Equal to the NPV, as here, it says the plan is proven optimal; finance and the auditor both read it.

Planners Read Projects, What Changed and the Rule Behind Each

A planner acts project by project, so that version is a list of projects: what happens to each, in words about the rules, and what it would cost to undo. It is lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted's diff with lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out's reasons attached. The rule facts are turned into words by one small function, so that every reason reads the same way:

What makes this version work for planners:

  • Every line is about a project, in the order a planner acts: what leaves, what joins, what moves. Row names such as budget_2030 are translated ("no room in the budget of 2030"); the facts behind them stay in the record for anyone who asks.
  • Every change has a reason and a price. P001 is out because its repair option P002 took its place and no year has $1,320k free; putting it back costs $45k. The price is computed in the cut scenario, the one the planner is looking at, so it differs from lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out's base-scenario prices.
  • It says where the plan is fragile. Five changes cost under $50k to undo. The threshold is a choice the planning team makes once and writes down (here about 0.2% of the plan's NPV); the program only applies it. Those five are where a planner's local knowledge should win, and where a small change of inputs could flip the plan (lesson Scenario diffs: selected, deferred, shifted's near ties).

Finance Reads Money by Year, the Change in Value, and Whether It Is Proven

Finance reads the same record through money: what is spent each year against what was authorized, what the change does to value, and whether the value is a proven optimum or a solver's best effort.

What makes this version work for finance:

  • Spend by year, against the authorized budget, and against the plan it replaces. A five-year total hides a year that breaks; here 2030 is spent to the dollar, which finance will want to know before anyone adds to it.
  • The spend is summed from the cost profiles, not read from the model's rows (real_spend, lesson Cash-flow spreading across years), so the table reconciles with the projects' own budgets.
  • Value with its proof. NPV, its change, the solver's status and the gap to the bound. An NPV from a run that stopped early is a lower limit on what the inputs allow (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap); finance must never read it as the best plan without the gap beside it.
  • A ratio labelled for what it is. Cutting $2,760k of 2029 budget removes $2,830k of spend over the plan and $1,174k of NPV, 0.415 per $k of spend removed. That is an average over one discrete change. It is not the value of the next dollar, which on a MILP is a step function; lessons Parametric analysis: the budget-value frontier and The marginal value of the next dollar build that curve. The text says so in its own words, so nobody multiplies 0.415 by a different cut.

    The finance version of a fleet plan

    • the finance version gives spend against budget by year and the NPV with its proof
    • every version of a result is written by code from one stored record of the run

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A transit agency's fleet-replacement optimizer stores each run as a record, and its finance office wants a short version of every run in its own terms. A record is a dictionary with "budget" and "spend" (each {year: $k}), "npv" (the plan's NPV, $k), "bound" (the solver's proven limit on the best NPV, $k) and "status" (the solver's termination, such as "OPTIMAL" or "FEASIBLE").

In fleet_finance.py, write finance_text(record), returning the lines of the finance version, every number taken from the record:

  • one line per year, oldest first: "2027: spend 10,850 of 11,000, 150 left", or, when spend is above the budget, "2027: spend 11,200 of 11,000, OVER by 200";
  • then one line for the value: "NPV $30,412k, proven optimal" when the run is proven, otherwise "NPV $29,950k, not proven: no plan is worth more than $30,600k".

Amounts use thousands separators; NPV and bound are rounded to whole $k. Budget and spend may be floats (a solver's spend is a sum of floats): round each to a whole $k first, then compare and subtract the rounded amounts, so 10,849.6 of 11,000.0 reads spend 10,850 of 11,000, 150 left. Write proven(record) first: a run is proven when its status is "OPTIMAL" and its bound is within 0.5 of its NPV. Before coding, say in one sentence why the status alone is not enough. The tests use their own runs.

Auditors Read Inputs, Settings and Checks They Can Repeat Without the Model


An auditor does not need the plan explained; the auditor needs to be able to repeat and check it. That version lists what was run, on what, with what, and the result of checks that do not trust the model. The checks are the heart of it:





What makes this version work for an auditor:


  • Full identifiers. The whole snapshot hash, not the 12 characters a screen shows, and the parent's, so the auditor can match the inputs to a stored scenario (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock) and see that only the 2029 budget changed.
  • The software and settings. OR-Tools' version, the solver and the gap tolerance. A rerun on different software is a different experiment (lessons Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver and Versioned input snapshots, pinned solvers and run manifests).
  • Checks that do not trust the model. verify reads the portfolio's data and the plan, and nothing the model built. If a row had been written wrongly, the model would have kept the wrong rule and its own slacks would agree with it; the check, written separately from the data, would not. That is lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer's worksheet habit applied to one run. The module's tests show that it catches a plan with both P001 and P002 funded.
  • The rerun, reported as evidence. "Same plan: True" is what one rerun on one machine showed. It is not a guarantee, and the version says what was done rather than "the result is reproducible".

Problem statement


A hospital network buys major equipment through a yearly capital plan that its optimizer proposes. Its internal auditor does not want to trust the optimizer's model to say the plan is valid: if a rule had been coded wrongly, the model would agree with its own mistake. The auditor wants a check written from the purchase data alone.


items maps each purchase to its data: "years", the years it may be bought in; "cost", $k, all spent in the year it is bought; and "mandatory". budget maps each year to its limit, and groups maps each exclusive group (at most one of its members may be bought, ever) to its members. A plan maps each purchase it makes to its year.


In hospital_audit.py, write audit_failures(plan, items, budget, groups), returning a sorted list with one entry per failure: "window <item>", "mandatory <item>", "exclusive <group>" or "budget <year>". An empty list means the plan keeps every rule.


Write the four rules first, as conditions on the plan, with $y_i$ the year purchase $i$ is bought in, $W_i$ its allowed years, $c_i$ its cost, $B_t$ the budget of year $t$ and $S$ the purchases the plan makes: one condition for windows, one for mandatory purchases, one per group and one per year. Then code each as a check. The tests use their own hospital; no solver is involved.

Summary


  • One record per run, and every text is written from it. The record holds inputs, snapshot, status, bound, plan, diff, priced reasons, spend and checks, as data; the writers read only the record.
  • Planners read projects, what changed and the rule behind each. One line per project, the rule in words, the price of undoing it, and the close calls.
  • Finance reads money by year, the change in value, and whether it is proven. Spend against budget and against the previous plan, NPV with its gap, and averages labelled as averages.
  • Auditors read inputs, settings and checks they can repeat without the model. Full snapshots, software and settings, rule checks from the data, and a rerun stated as evidence.

Further reading: the OR-Tools MathOpt overview (its detailed termination reasons, which the record keeps as status); MathOpt result.py (OR-Tools source) (the docstrings of objective_value, which points to the primal bound, and best_objective_bound, equal to the dual bound: the two numbers finance's line compares); the OR-Tools MathOpt sample integer_programming.py (it refuses to read values unless the termination is optimal or feasible, the first thing any of the three versions depends on); the Gurobi reference for linear constraint attributes (slack reported per row of the current solution, the kind of fact a record stores). The CIPT job (lesson A binding-constraints report) names planners, finance partners and regulators as the people who rely on the results, and asks for outputs that explain them.

Design Note: Templates in Code, Not Prose in Code


The three writers here are small Python functions that format strings. That is enough to show the rules, and it keeps every number tied to the record. A real service would keep the record as a typed schema (lesson Result schemas planners can consume) and the wording in templates that people outside the team can edit, because the people who know how finance wants a sentence phrased are not the people who write the solver. Whatever the tooling, the rule stands: wording may change freely, numbers may come only from the record.
