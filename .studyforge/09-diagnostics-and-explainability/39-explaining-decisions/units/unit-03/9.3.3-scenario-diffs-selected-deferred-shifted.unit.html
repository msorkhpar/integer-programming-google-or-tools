  15. Scenario diffs

Finance has asked what a smaller 2029 envelope would do: $6,000k instead of $8,760k. Lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock answered the right way, with a copy of the base scenario, its own snapshot, and its own run, and printed one line comparing the two plans. That line is what a planner reads first, and in a real planning cycle it is read dozens of times: base against cut, cut against cut-plus-override, this week's run against last week's. This lesson turns it into a report, the scenario diff, and states the rules that keep it honest.

A diff that is not honest is easy to produce. Compare start variables instead of projects, and a project that moved a year shows up as one project dropped and another added. Show the plans but not the inputs, and nobody can say what caused the change. Change two inputs at once, and the diff cannot say which one cost what. Leave out the change in NPV, and a reshuffle worth $8k looks as important as one worth $1,174k. Each rule below closes one of these.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course; no number in it describes a real one.

The Model

Both scenarios solve the model of lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out with their own data; the cut changes one parameter, $B_{2029}$. The last section adds one row, marked, to find the second-best plan.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; $F^{+}$ and $F^{-}$ the projects a scenario forces in and out. For the last section, $S^*$ the set of pairs $(i,t)$ with $x_{i,t} = 1$ in the best plan.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k): in the base scenario $B_{2029} = 8{,}760$, in the cut scenario $B_{2029} = 6{,}000$, every other year the same.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$, with $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ and $\text{by}(i, t) = \sum_{s \in T_i,\, s \le t} x_{i,s}$.

A diff by position in a list is worse: one project added near the top of a sorted list moves every line below it. Here is the base against the cut, from scenario_diff.py:

These are the same changes lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock printed in one line, now sorted into what a planner acts on. Three readings are worth making every time.

  • Look for swaps inside alternative groups. P001 is deferred and P002 selected, and both are options for asset A001 (group G001): this is one decision, "repair instead of replace", not two. A good report pairs them.
  • Shifts go both ways. Four projects move earlier, into years the cut did not touch, and two move later. A cut in one year rearranges the whole plan, because the five budget rows are solved together.
  • Count changes, not only money. Eleven projects change for one input; a planner who must tell eleven fleet teams wants that number as much as the NPV.

A Plan Diff Needs the Input Diff and the Money Beside It

A diff of plans says what changed; it does not say why. The other half is a diff of the inputs, which each scenario already holds in one canonical form for its snapshot (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock). input_changes walks two such dictionaries and lists every value that differs:

One input changed, so every line of the plan diff is its consequence. If the input diff listed anything else, a changed cost profile, a solver setting, the portfolio's own hash, the plan diff could no longer be read as the effect of the budget cut. The snapshots make the check cheap: equal snapshots, equal inputs.

The money goes beside the plans for the same reason. The NPV change, $-1,174$k, is the price of the cut. The spend change explains the shifts: 2029 spends $2,940k less, more than the $2,760k cut, because the cut plan leaves $310k of 2029 unused against $130k in the base. And three other years spend more, which is where the projects that moved went.

Problem statement

A gas utility plans its pipe replacements by quarter, and its planning tool keeps each scenario as a record: a name and a plan, the list of funded replacements, each with an id and a start such as "2028-Q3". Every time a scenario is re-run, the program managers ask the same thing: what changed since the last one? Write the function the tool will call for it, diff_scenarios(a, b) in gas_plan.py, comparing scenario a's plan with scenario b's. It returns a dictionary:

  • "adds": the ids in b's plan and not in a's, sorted;
  • "drops": the ids in a's plan and not in b's, sorted;
  • "shifts": one entry per id in both plans with a different start, sorted by id, each a dictionary {"id", "from", "to", "quarters"} where quarters is how many quarters later b starts it than a does (negative when earlier).

Plans list their rows in any order. Write quarters(start) first, a count of quarters that makes the difference of two starts the number of quarters between them, and say how $(y, q)$ maps to one integer. Then state the three lists as set expressions over $P_a$ and $P_b$, the ids of the two plans, and $a(i)$, $b(i)$, their starts. The tests use their own pipes.

Change One Input at a Time, or the Diff Cannot Be Charged to a Cause


Planners rarely stop at one change. Suppose they cut 2029 and force P024 in. diff_chain.py reaches the same final scenario by two paths, one change per step, and diffs each step:



The end is the same both ways: $-1,379$k, and the same plan. The steps are not. Forcing P024 in costs $273k on the base and $205k after the cut; the cut costs $1,174k on the base and $1,106k once P024 is in. Neither number is wrong. Each is the cost of one change given the other, and the two changes interact through the same budget rows. Even the project lists disagree: on the first path P001 is deferred by the cut and selected again by the override, so the whole-path diff never shows it.


Three habits follow.


  • Diff each step, and keep the path. A diff between two scenarios that differ in two inputs says what changed, not which input caused what. The chain of single-change diffs does.
  • State the order with every attribution. "The override costs $205k" is true after the cut and false before it. Write "forcing P024 in, after the 2029 cut, costs $205k".
  • Add step costs only along one path. $1,174k + $205k and $273k + $1,106k both give $1,379k; $1,174k + $273k does not, because those two steps start from the same scenario.

The row's left side reaches $|S^*|$ only when every start variable of the best plan is 1 and every other one is 0, so it removes that one plan and nothing else:






A second plan exists $8k below the best, with two projects trading years. Every run in this module solves to a relative gap of 0, so it must return a plan worth $24,260k, and the second plan is not one. A solve allowed to stop at a relative gap of 0.1% has no such duty: both plans are within 0.1% of the best bound, so it may stop at either, and a diff between two such runs could show P018 and P029 moving with nothing in the inputs changed. Lesson Alternative optima and deterministic tie-breaking is about this case, alternative and nearly equal optima, and how to break the tie on purpose.


So a diff must print its NPV change, and a reader must weigh changes by it. Two runs whose diff is worth 0.033% of the plan have made the same decision with a coin toss on the details; two runs whose diff is worth 5% have made different decisions. The same scale reads the forcing costs of lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out: the $8k there was this very plan.

Problem statement


A water utility's scenario diff lists the mains it adds and drops between two runs. Many mains have two options in an alternative group, reline or replace, and at most one option per group is funded. When a diff shows replace_M4 dropped and reline_M4 added, the asset managers read it as one decision, "reline main 4 instead of replacing it", and they want the report to say so.


In swaps.py, write pair_swaps(adds, drops, group_of). adds and drops are the diff's two lists; group_of maps a project to its alternative group, and a project with no group is absent. Return a dictionary:


  • "swaps": a list of (dropped, added) pairs, one per dropped project whose group gained an added project, sorted by the dropped id;
  • "adds" and "drops": what is left of each list once the swapped projects are taken out, sorted.


Because each group keeps $\sum_{i \in g} \text{started}(i) \le 1$ in both plans, a group can lose at most one option and gain at most one. Before coding, write down what that row implies about how many swaps one group can produce, and what a project with no group can be paired with. The tests use their own mains.

Summary


  • A diff compares plans project by project, in three lists. Selected, deferred, shifted (earlier or later), keyed by project id, never by variable or position. Pair swaps within an alternative group.
  • A plan diff needs the input diff and the money beside it. List every changed input (equal snapshots mean none), the NPV change and the spend change by year.
  • Change one input at a time, or the diff cannot be charged to a cause. On GridCap the P024 override costs $273k before the 2029 cut and $205k after it; report the path and the order.
  • A diff worth a few thousand is a near tie, not a decision. GridCap small's second-best plan is $8k (0.033%) below the best and differs by two shifts.

Design Note: Diff Results, Not Models


It is tempting to diff the two models: which rows and bounds differ. That diff is easy to compute and almost useless to a planner. One changed number on budget_2029 explains nothing about P001 or P035, and in a real system the two models may have been built by different code versions, so their rows differ for reasons no scenario chose. Diff the inputs, which people changed, and the plans, which people act on; leave the model out of it. When the inputs are equal and the plans are not, the cause is in the run itself (settings, versions, ties), and lessons Where nondeterminism comes from: threads, time limits, ties to Versioned input snapshots, pinned solvers and run manifests are about that case.
