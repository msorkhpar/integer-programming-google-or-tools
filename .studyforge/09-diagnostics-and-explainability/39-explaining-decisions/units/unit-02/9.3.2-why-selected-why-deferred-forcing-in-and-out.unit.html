  15. Why selected, why deferred

Two emails reach GridCap's optimization team on the morning the base plan is published. The Ridge region manager writes: "P024, the undergrounding job we promised Ridge customers, is not in the plan. Why?" And a finance analyst writes: "P032 costs $5,680k in one year. Why is it in?" Lesson A binding-constraints report gave part of an answer: as the plan stands, every budget row is too full to take P024. But the solver is not bound to the plan as it stands. It could drop one project, move two, and fit P024. The real question is what that would cost, and what would give way.

This lesson answers it the way an optimizer can prove: force the opposite decision and re-solve. Force P024 in, and the drop in NPV is the price of P024; force P032 out, and the drop is what P032 is worth to the plan. The comparison of the two plans then says which projects give way, and the rows of lesson A binding-constraints report say which rule makes them. Such a re-solve is often called a counterfactual: the best plan in a world where one decision went the other way.

GridCap is a fictional utility, generated for this course; no number in it describes a real one.

In this lesson, a project is selected when it is in the plan, and deferred when it is not: utilities say deferred because a candidate left out of this cycle's plan comes back in the next cycle's list. A selected project that starts after its earliest allowed year is late; lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer counted nine of them.

The Model

The model of lesson A binding-constraints report, whole, with this lesson's three forcing rows marked. A forcing row replaces the project's once row; a forced-in must-do keeps its must row.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years. For one re-solve: $F^{+} \subseteq P$ the projects forced in, $F^{-} \subseteq P$ those forced out, and $A \subseteq \{(i,t) : i \in F^{+},\ t \in T_i\}$ the forced-in projects pinned to a start year.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$, with $\text{started}(i) = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ and $\text{by}(i, t) = \sum_{s \in T_i,\, s \le t} x_{i,s}$.

The Cost of a Decision Is What the Best Plan Loses When You Force the Opposite

why.force reverses one decision of the base plan and re-solves. A project in the plan is forced out, a project out of it is forced in, and a year, if given, pins the start:

The forcing is a change to the scenario, not an edit of the solved model: build writes the model again from the scenario, with the in_ or out_ row in place of the project's once row. Each re-solve runs to a proven optimum (relative gap 0), because a cost is the difference of two optima; with a gap tolerance, the difference could be the solver stopping early rather than the decision. force_one.py answers the two emails:

P024 is deferred because funding it costs the portfolio $273k of NPV, the same $273k lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock found when a planner forced it in at Post-Opt (the base scenario there also had region floors, which did not change this plan). P032 is selected because the best plan without it is worth $112k less.

Neither number is the project's own NPV, and the second shows how far apart they can be. P032 brings $3,678k of NPV, yet losing it costs only $112k, because its $5,680k of 2027 money does not vanish: the re-solve spends it on P002, P036, P037 and P038 and rearranges eight start years. What a project is worth to a plan is its NPV less the NPV of the best use of its money and its place in the rules. Only a re-solve measures that. A ranking by NPV, or by NPV per dollar, cannot (lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer found the same for ratios).

A Forced Re-Solve Says What Gives Way, and the Rules Say Why

A cost tells a planner how much; the reason is in the rows. Two sources give it, and a good answer uses both:

  • The rows that refuse the project as the plan stands (lesson A binding-constraints report's refusals), turned into facts: for a budget row, the room left and the least the project needs there; for an alternative row, the option already funded. why.rules_against writes them.
  • What gives way in the forced plan: the projects dropped, added and moved. That is the cheapest way the solver found to make room, so it names the projects the deferred one is competing with.

Put together, the answers read as sentences a planner can check:

  • P024 spends $850k a year for two years, and no year of its window has more than $150k left. To fit it, the best plan drops P005, P009 and P016, adds P037 and moves three projects, and ends $273k lower.
  • P002 is the repair option for asset A001, whose replacement P001 is funded, and alt_G001 allows one of the two; the money is short as well. Forcing P002 in drops P001, as the alternative row says it must, and the plan ends $112k lower.

The order matters: the rule first, then what gives way, then the cost. A cost with no rule ("it would cost $273k") invites the reply "then cut something else"; the rule shows that every year is full, and the forced plan shows what "something else" would be.

    The price of reversing a renovation decision

    • the cost of a decision is the best value lost when the opposite decision is forced and the model re-solved
    • a forcing is a named row added to a fresh copy of the model, never an edit of the solved plan

Problem statement

A regional library board picks branch renovations each year with a small MILP: each renovation has a cost and a benefit, the year's budget is fixed, and renovations required by accessibility law are mandatory. Branch managers keep asking the same two questions: "why was my renovation left out?" and "why did theirs get in?". The board wants one answer to both: what the best plan would lose if that one decision went the other way, and which renovations would change.

renovations.py already has the board's model (build) and best, which solves a model and returns the best value and the chosen names, or None when no plan is feasible. best solves at a relative gap of 0, so both values in a cost are proven optima, as the lesson requires. Write forcing_cost(items, budget, name). It returns a dictionary:

  • "action": "out" if the best plan chose name, "in" if it did not;
  • "cost": the best plan's value less the best value with that decision reversed, rounded to an integer, or None when no plan allows the reversal;
  • "dropped" and "added": the names in the best plan but not in the reversed one, and the other way round, each sorted; both empty when there is no reversed plan.

The board's model, with $R$ the renovations, $M \subseteq R$ the mandatory ones, $c_r$ and $v_r$ each renovation's cost and value, and $B$ the budget:

Write the one row your reversal adds, with its label, for each of the two actions. Then code it: add it as a named row to a fresh copy of the model. The tests use their own branches.

A Forcing the Rules Cannot Satisfy Is Answered by a Rule, Not a Price


Some reversals have no price because no plan allows them. Force a must-do out:



The forced model holds both $\text{started}(i) = 1$ (must) and $\text{started}(i) = 0$ (out), and no plan keeps both. The answer to "why is P017 selected?" is then the rule, "it is a must-do", and the cost is not "very large" but undefined. A report must say "infeasible: must_P017", never print a number.


Here the conflict is plain from the project's own rows. When it is not (a project forced in whose parent cannot fit, or a forced-in pair that two budgets cannot both carry), the conflicting rows are found with the tools of lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo (an IIS) and worded with those of lesson Turning an IIS into a planner message. Some study guides say that an infeasible forced-in solve means the project "violates hard physical or timing constraints". Read it more narrowly: it means the forcing contradicts the model's rules as written, and which rules is a question for an IIS, not a guess.

The planners' requirement from lesson What planners, finance and regulators each need from the optimizer was a reason for every project. why_report.py runs one forced re-solve per project, 40 in all, plus one per late project pinned to its earliest year, and sorts each list by cost. The deferred projects:



The full list has all 13 (The Full Programs runs it); the most expensive is P007 at $1,145k. Then the selected projects, by what losing them costs, and the late ones:




Look at how small most costs are against a plan worth $24,260k. Forcing P004 in costs $64k, 0.26% of the NPV; moving P029 a year earlier costs $8k. And many different forcings cost exactly the same. The program groups the forcings whose re-solves return the very same plan:



Ten different reversals lead to just two plans: one worth $64k less than the best, one worth $112k less. These are the runner-up plans of the portfolio, and the forcings are different doors into them. That is what to tell a planner: P004 is not out because it is a bad project; it is out because a plan with it exists that is only $64k worse, and the solver must pick one. A cost of exactly 0 would mean the forced plan is another optimum, and then "why deferred" has no answer beyond "the solver had to choose" (lesson Alternative optima and deterministic tie-breaking covers alternative optima and how to break ties on purpose). On GridCap small no reversal costs 0; the cheapest, P029 a year earlier, costs $8k.


Treat a small cost as a finding in its own right. It says the decision is cheap to override, which is what a planner pushing for P004 wants to know, and it warns that a small change in the inputs could flip the decision, which is what finance wants to know before trusting it.

Problem statement


A water utility's capital plan chooses which mains to work on. Some mains have two options, reline or replace, in an alternative group: at most one option per main. For every project the plan defers, the utility's analyst has already forced it in and re-solved, so for each one there is the best plan and the forced plan. The asset managers want the reason written from those two plans, in the terms they use: which alternative the deferred option would push out, and which other projects would give way.


In reasons.py, write why_deferred(name, group_of, best, forced):


  • group_of maps a project to its alternative group; a project with no group is absent;
  • best is the set of projects in the best plan, and forced the set with name forced in, or None when no plan allows it.


Return a list of strings:


  • ["infeasible"] when there is no forced plan;
  • ["nothing displaced"] when the forced plan drops nothing from the best one;
  • otherwise one entry per project the forced plan drops: "alternative <id>" when it is in the same alternative group as name, "displaced <id>" when it is not. Alternatives come first, then the rest, each part sorted by id.


The forced plan was solved with $\sum_{t} x_{\text{name},t} = 1$ added, and each group $g$ keeps $\sum_{i \in g} \text{started}(i) \le 1$. Before coding, say which of those rows makes a dropped project an "alternative", and what a project with no group can never have. The tests use their own mains.

Summary


  • The cost of a decision is what the best plan loses when you force the opposite. Re-solve with $\text{started}(i) = 1$ or $= 0$ and compare optima. P024 costs $273k to force in; P032 costs $112k to lose, against its own NPV of $3,678k.
  • A forced re-solve says what gives way, and the rules say why. State the rows that refuse the project as the plan stands, then the projects that give way, then the cost.
  • A forcing the rules cannot satisfy is answered by a rule, not a price. Forcing a must-do out is infeasible; report the rows in conflict, never a number.
  • Report every project, and read small costs as near ties. Ten GridCap reversals lead to two runner-up plans, $64k and $112k below the best; a cost of 0 would mean another optimum.

Design Note: Forcings Are Named Rows, Not Bounds


Lesson Manual overrides as fixed bounds writes a planner's override as bounds on the start variables, which is the lightest way to fix a decision. This lesson writes a forcing as a named row (in_P024, out_P017) instead. A named row can be reported, diffed between scenarios and named by an IIS when the forcing makes the model infeasible. A bound has no name of its own. Lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo's run showed both ways a bound fails in Gurobi's IIS: a 0-1 variable typed binary never has its bounds in the IIS at all, and one typed integer (as MathOpt's 0-1 variables are when copied to gurobipy) can have them there, but only as a variable's lower or upper bound (25 of them in that IIS), marked exactly as the ordinary 0 and 1 bounds are. Either way, a forcing written as a bound could not be named as the forcing in the very explanation it caused. The cost is one row per forcing, which on a re-solve of one forcing is nothing.
