  16. Fixed-integer LP duals

Lesson Why a MILP has no duals told GridCap's finance partner that the selection MILP has no dual values. The partner has heard of a workaround: "Fix the projects you chose, solve what is left as an LP, and read its prices." Lesson Duals, reduced costs and sensitivity attributes did exactly that with gurobipy's Model.fixed() and got a budget price of 0 in every year. With every variable fixed, nothing was left for money to buy. This lesson gives the plan something continuous to buy, a routine line-clearance program, and works out what the fixed-integer prices then say, and what they do not. GridCap is fictional, and so are the clearance program's numbers: they are this module's assumptions.

The Model

The selection model of lesson Why a MILP has no duals, with one change, marked: the budget can also buy routine line clearance, in any amount up to what the crews can deliver.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, each with parent $p(c)$; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups.

Parameters. $v_i$, project $i$'s NPV ($k); $c_i$, its cost ($k); $B$, the capital budget ($k); $r = 0.25$, the NPV one $k of clearance returns; $U = 800$, the most clearance the crews can deliver ($k).

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$: project $i$ is funded; and $y \ge 0$, continuous: the $k spent on line clearance.

Objective.

Constraints.

In selection.py it is build(portfolio, budget, clearance=True), with y named clearance.

The fixed-integer LP of a solved MILP is the same model with every integer variable fixed at its value in the MILP's plan, $x_i = \hat x_i$, and treated as continuous. Only $y$ is left to decide. It is an LP, so it has dual values.

Fix Every Integer at Its Value, Make It Continuous, and Re-Solve

MathOpt has no fixed() method, so the program does it by hand. Each variable's lower_bound, upper_bound and integer can be set after the model is built:

round is a guard: a solver accepts an integer value within its integrality tolerance (see section 10), so a binary may come back a hair away from 0 or 1, and fixing it there would leave a sliver of a project in the LP.

The fixed LP has the MILP's value, 25,336.0, because it allows exactly the MILP's plan and the best clearance for it. At this budget the best plan spends the whole $38,390k on projects and buys no clearance. And the LP has dual values.

The Fixed LP Prices Only What Stayed Free

The budget's price is 0.25, the clearance rate $r$. That follows from the model: with the projects fixed, one more $k of budget can only go to clearance, and clearance returns $0.25k. The fixed-integer price is the price of the continuous part of the plan, and nothing else. In lesson Duals, reduced costs and sensitivity attributes's model nothing was continuous, so every price was 0.

The reduced costs say the same thing from the variables' side. A fixed $x_i$ is held by its bounds, so its reduced cost is its objective coefficient less the price of what it uses: $v_i - 0.25\,c_i$, the NPV of project $i$ against the clearance the same money would buy. The printed formula beside each one checks it. Read them with care:

  • P034's reduced cost, 1,590.5, says the project beats spending its $2,130k on clearance by $1,590.5k, at this one budget.
  • P001 is not funded, yet its reduced cost, 741, is positive. With the plan fixed, every other row is priced at 0, so the reduced cost ignores what keeps P001 out: it is in alternative group G001 with P002, which is funded, and its $2,640k would have to come from other projects, not from clearance (there is none to give up). A fixed-integer reduced cost is not a reason why a project was left out. Lesson Why selected, why deferred: forcing in and out answers that question by forcing the project in and re-solving.

Binding Means Zero Slack; a Binding Row Can Still Have a Zero Price

A row binds at a plan when its slack is zero: its two sides are equal, so it holds the plan where it is. The tempting shortcut is to call a row binding when its price is nonzero. The two are not the same, and the fixed LP shows why. The program's slack function measures how far the row's left side sits from its nearest bound:

In the output above, must_P003 has slack 0 and price 0. The row $x_{P003} = 1$ binds: it holds with equality. But the fixed bounds $1 \le x_{P003} \le 1$ hold the variable at exactly the same place, so the row is one of two things saying the same thing. The LP can put the whole price on either. GLOP put it on the bound, as the reduced cost of 72, and gave the row 0. Such an LP is degenerate (lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness): more things bind at the optimum than are needed to pin it down, and the split of prices among them is not unique.

The budget row binds with a price; must_P003 binds without one; clearance_cap has slack 800 and price 0. A binding-constraints report (lesson A binding-constraints report) must test the slack, report the price beside it, and never infer one from the other.

    A row report that keeps slack and price apart

    • slack, binding and price reported for each row of an LP

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A water treatment plant doses two chemicals each day. Its LP's rows are limits on stock, on the mixer's hours and on a minimum disinfection level. After each solve, the shift lead wants a report: for every row, how much room it has left and what it is worth.

Write row_report(model, result, tol=1e-6) for a solved LP. Return a dict keyed by each row's name, whose value is a dict with three entries:

  • "slack": how far the row's left side sits from its nearest finite bound, at the solution. A row may have a lower bound, an upper bound, or both (an equality has both, equal); a missing bound is infinite.
  • "binding": True when that slack is zero, within tol.
  • "price": the row's dual value.

Define binding first, in one sentence, from the row's two sides alone, then write the code. Each row's left side is a sum of coefficient × variable value: row.terms() yields the terms, each with .variable and .coefficient, and row.lower_bound and row.upper_bound are its bounds. model.linear_constraints() yields the rows.

The tests build small LPs, solve them with GLOP and read your report. About ten lines.

The Price Holds Only With the Plan Fixed and Inside Its Range


The finance partner reads "price 0.25" as "+$1,000k of budget gives +$250k". The program tests that sentence. For each change $d$ it re-solves twice: the fixed LP with budget $B + d$ (the plan held fixed), and the full MILP with budget $B + d$ (the plan free to change):






Three things happen, and each one follows from the model.


  1. With the plan fixed, the price holds over a range. Extra money buys clearance at 0.25 until the cap: $y$ can grow from 0 to 800, so the fixed plan gains exactly price × $d$ for $d \in [0, 800]$. At +1,000 it gains only 200, because the last $200k has nothing to buy. This is the range of lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver, for the fixed LP.
  2. Here the range is one-sided. The plan spends every dollar on projects and buys no clearance, so a smaller budget cannot be met by buying less clearance: with $10k less the fixed plan is infeasible. The price says nothing about cuts. The MILP has to drop or swap a project, and loses 4.0 for the first $10k, not 2.5.
  3. Inside the range the price is a floor on the MILP's gain, not its value. The fixed plan plus more clearance is still a feasible plan of the MILP, so the MILP gains at least price × $d$. It usually gains more, because it may choose a different plan: +$100k is worth $98.0k to the MILP, almost four times the price's $25k.


Fixed-integer prices also change with the plan that was fixed. Two more budgets show the other cases:



At $38,000k the best plan buys $60k of clearance, so the price 0.25 holds both ways, for $d \in [-60, 740]$. A cut of $100k is outside that range: the MILP loses $95.0k, nearly four times price × 100. At $66,000k the clearance program is full and $210k of budget is unspent: the budget price is 0, and the 0.25 has moved to the cap row. Yet $100k more budget is worth $17.5k to the MILP, and $1,000k is worth $205.0k, because a new plan can use it.


So a fixed-integer price is a sentence with two conditions in it: "with this selection of projects held fixed, and for budget changes between $-y$ and $U - y$, each $1k is worth $0.25k". Without the conditions it is wrong in both directions.


A report that tests a price this way is usually handed a row's name, such as "budget", not the row object. MathOpt's Model in OR-Tools 9.15 has no lookup by name: get_linear_constraint takes the row's integer id. Problem statement


A water utility decides which pumping stations to commission this year (yes or no each) and how many hours of contractor overtime to buy, any amount up to a cap. A limit row, such as the capital budget, caps what stations and overtime use together. After the MILP is solved, finance reads the fixed-integer price of that row and asks two questions: if the limit rises by step, does the plan (stations held as chosen) gain exactly price × step? And if it falls by step, does it lose exactly price × step?


Write price_directions(model, result, row_name, step) for a solved MILP whose row_name row is a $\le$ row. It returns a dict:


  • "price": the row's dual value in the fixed-integer LP;
  • "up": True when the fixed-integer LP with the row's limit raised by step is optimal and its value is the original fixed LP's value plus price × step (within 1e-6);
  • "down": the same for the limit lowered by step, with value minus price × step.

It changes the caller's model in place, and your function may leave the plan fixed there: afterwards the tests read only the row's limit. Pyomo's Dual Suffix Does Not Run the Re-Solve for You


Lesson Solving with HiGHS and reading results showed Pyomo's way of asking for duals: a Suffix named dual, which the solver interface fills, if the solver supplies duals (the Pyomo suffix documentation states that condition). It is easy to assume that Pyomo, asked for duals of a MILP, fixes the integers and re-solves. It does not. pyomo_fixed.py writes the same model in Pyomo and solves it with HiGHS through Pyomo's appsi_highs interface:




The re-solve is yours to write, and it has two steps. Fixing the variables (v.fix(...)) is not enough, because they are still declared Binary, so the model is still a MILP. Pyomo's core.relax_integer_vars transformation turns integrality off:



The program runs this twice, once with HiGHS's presolve on (its default) and once with it off:



With presolve off, Pyomo finds the plan MathOpt found (25,336.0, no clearance) and the same prices. With presolve on, the HiGHS that Pyomo calls (the standalone highspy package, a different build from the HiGHS inside OR-Tools) reported a plan worth 25,173.0 as optimal, although the presolve-off run found a feasible plan worth 25,336.0. A plan $163k better exists, so the first answer is not optimal, whatever its status says. Its fixed-integer prices differ too: clearance is full there, so the budget price is 0 and the cap carries 0.25.


That run teaches the lesson's point twice. A fixed-integer price belongs to the plan that was fixed; fix a different plan and the price changes. And before you explain a plan's prices, check the plan: here, a second solver configuration was enough to show that the first answer was wrong.


Summary


  • Fix every integer at its value, make it continuous, and re-solve. In MathOpt set each integer variable's bounds to its rounded value and integer = False; GLOP then reports duals.
  • The fixed LP prices only what stayed free. Its budget price is the rate of the continuous part of the plan (here, clearance at 0.25). A fixed binary's reduced cost is its value against that rate, not a reason it was funded or deferred.
  • Binding means zero slack; a binding row can still have a zero price. must_P003 binds with price 0, because its fixed bounds carry the price instead. Test slack and price separately.
  • The price holds only with the plan fixed and inside its range. Inside the range it is a floor on the MILP's gain; outside, or for a cut when nothing continuous can shrink, it says nothing.
  • Pyomo's dual Suffix does not run the re-solve for you. Fix, relax integrality, then solve; and check the plan before pricing it.

Design Note: Name the Plan Beside the Price


A fixed-integer price is meaningless without the plan it was computed for, so store them together: the funded set (or its hash) and the solver run beside every price. When a re-run finds a different optimal plan (an alternative optimum, or, as above, a better plan), the old prices are not "updated"; they belonged to another plan.
