Lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo found three conflicts in GridCap's Budget Lock scenario. A conflict says what clashes. Finance asks a different question: what is the smallest change that gives us a plan? Should the 2028 budget rise by $40k, or by $500k? Is one more crew-month enough? This lesson answers with elastic constraints: every rule planners could negotiate may bend, each unit of bending has a price, and the solver finds the cheapest set of bends that leaves a plan. Gurobi builds the same model for you as feasRelax, and the end of the lesson checks that both give the same answer. GridCap is a fictional utility; none of its numbers are real.

The Model

The model of lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell with every soft row made elastic. The marked items are new. Everything else (sets, parameters, $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$, the hard rows) is as in that lesson.

Sets. As in lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell, plus $S$, the soft rows: every $\text{budget}_y$, $\text{crews}_{q,y}$, $\text{floor}_r$, $\text{force}$ and $\text{must}_i$ row (30 rows on the Budget Lock scenario). The hard rows, which never bend, are $\text{once}_i$, $\text{alt}_g$ and $\text{after}_{c,t}$: bending them gives a plan that is not a plan (a project started twice, two alternatives, a child before its parent). $\leftarrow$ new

Parameters. As in lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell, plus each soft row $s$'s price per unit of bending, $w_s$, and an upper limit on its bending, $U_s$ (used only by the counting objective). Write soft row $s$ as $\ell_s \le a_s^\top x \le u_s$, where $\ell_s$ or $u_s$ may be infinite. $\leftarrow$ new

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ as before; for each soft row $s$, $o_s \ge 0$ (how far the row may go over its upper bound) when $u_s$ is finite, and $v_s \ge 0$ (how far it may fall under its lower bound) when $\ell_s$ is finite, both continuous; and, for the counting objective only, $z_s \in \{0, 1\}$, "row $s$ bends". $\leftarrow$ new

where $V^*$ is the optimum of the first objective.

Constraints. The hard rows exactly as in lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell. Each soft row is replaced by its elastic form ($o_s$ or $v_s$ is simply absent on a side with no bound), and the counting objective adds a link per elastic variable:

With every $o_s = v_s = 0$ the elastic model is the original, so the original is feasible exactly when the least violation is 0. This model is always feasible as long as the hard rows can hold together: any plan can be made to fit by bending enough.

An Elastic Variable Lets a Row Bend, at a Price

elastic.elastic(model, weight) copies the model and, for each row named in weight, adds one variable per side that has a bound. row.set_coefficient(var, c) puts a new term into an existing row:

A budget row has only an upper bound and gets over_budget_2028, the extra $k the year would need. A floor or force row has only a lower bound and gets under_.... A must-do row is an equation (lower and upper bound both 1) and gets both. The least-violation objective is one line:

bends(result) reads the answer back as "row: amount", positive for over and negative for under, listing only rows that bent.

Every Unit Costing 1 Mixes Dollars, Crews and Projects

The first try gives every soft row the price 1:

Total violation 2: one more transmission crew-month in 2031, and the board's P032 override broken (force_P032_2028 falls 1 short of its lower bound, so P032 does not start in 2028). Correct for the model, and useless for the planners. A unit of a budget row is $1k, a unit of a crew row is a crew-month, and a unit of an override is a whole project the board asked for. Pricing them all at 1 says that ignoring the board costs the same as finding $1k. The solver took the cheapest units, as told.

The price of each row is the policy, and the model only applies it. Lesson computeIIS, feasRelax and setObjectiveN's design note makes the same point about feasRelax's penalties.

Penalties in One Currency Give a Repair Planners Can Price

The second try puts every price in $k. A $k of budget or floor costs 1. A crew-month is priced at $50k, a figure the planners would supply (here it is an assumption of this lesson). Breaking a must-do or an override costs $10,000k a unit, more than any change of budget or crews in this scenario, so the solver bends one only if nothing else works:

The cheapest repair costs 390 in these units: $40k more in the 2028 budget, one more transmission crew-month in each of 2028 and 2031, and five more distribution crew-months in 2030 ($40 + 50 \times (1 + 1 + 5) = 390$). Each bend can be traced to the conflicts of lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo:

  • $40k in 2028 and a crew-month in 2028 end the two conflicts of the board's overrides: P032 and P022 together need $6,040k at the least, against $6,000k, and 20 transmission crew-months against 19 (lesson Turning an IIS into a planner message prints the arithmetic).
  • One crew-month in 2031 ends P017's conflict: it needs 13 and the scenario leaves 12.
  • The distribution crews of 2030 were in no conflict the filters reported. The repair's plan funds P023 (forced), P039 (must-do) and P037, which together use 13 distribution crew-months in 2030 against 8:

That last bend is the difference between "a conflict" and "a repair". An IIS names rows that clash among themselves. A repair has to leave a whole plan that keeps every other rule, and bending one row moves the plan into places where other rows bind. The solver found five distribution crew-months, at $250k, cheaper than every other way to keep all the remaining rules.

A least-violation repair can have many plans. The third objective picks the best one: maximize NPV with the violation capped at 390:

$17,060k, against $22,741k for the base scenario: the overrides and the locked budget cost the portfolio $5,681k, even after the repair. That number belongs in the message to the board, beside the repair. The 1e-6 tolerance on the cap keeps the second solve from failing on rounding in the first solve's value.

    How much over budget, year by year

    • elastic budget rows whose elastic variables are each year's overspend

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A school district's facilities plan funds building projects over a few years. Each project, if funded, spends a fixed amount ($k) in each year listed in its cost dictionary. Some projects are required by the state inspector; the rest are optional. Each year has its own budget, and money does not carry over from one year to the next. This year the required projects do not fit, and the superintendent wants to know the smallest overspend, year by year, that lets every required project be funded.

Write the formulation first: one 0/1 variable per project, an elastic budget row per year whose elastic variable is that year's overspend (continuous, at least 0), a row that funds each required project, and an objective that makes the total overspend as small as possible. Then write least_overspend(projects, budget, required) to build it with MathOpt, solve it with HiGHS at a relative gap of 0 (mathopt.SolveParameters(relative_gap_tolerance=0.0), so the overspend is a proven least, as in the lesson's own solves) and return {year: overspend} for every year of budget, each rounded to a whole number of $k.

projects maps a project name to {year: cost}; budget maps each year to its budget; required is a set of project names. The tests compare the overspends, so any correct formulation passes. About fifteen lines.

Counting Rows Asks "How Few Rules Must Give?"


Sometimes the question is not how much but how many: "what is the fewest rules we must break?". The counting objective gives each soft row a 0/1 variable $z_s$, ties its elastic variables to it with $o_s, v_s \le U_s z_s$, and minimizes $\sum z_s$. $U_s$ has to be large enough for any bend the row might need: $50,000k for a budget or floor row, 100 for a crew row, and 1 for an override or must-do row (a 0/1 sum can fall at most 1 short):




Two rows: drop P017's must-do, and stop forcing P023 in. That is the fewest possible, and it makes sense against lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo's three conflicts. force_P023 is in both override conflicts, and any row of P017's conflict ends the third. So no single row ends all three, and two do. The answer is not unique. Any row of P017's conflict could replace must_P017, and the solver picked one of the ties (lesson CP-SAT assumptions as a conflict finder finds a different pair with the same count). Counting also treats every row alike, so it happily breaks a must-do. Giving each row a weight inside the count fixes that, which is what feasRelax offers next as relaxobjtype=2. Two other edits do not: removing the must-do row drops the board's requirement itself, and removing its link lets the row bend without being counted at all. Raising the must-do row's U does not help either: U only lets the row bend further, and the plain count still treats it like every other row.

Problem statement


A port's harbour master builds next week's berth schedule. Each vessel must berth on exactly one day inside its window (first to last, days numbered from 1), and each day has a number of free berths. Each shipping line has also asked for one day (wanted). Those requests are the soft rules: the harbour master may refuse one, and the vessel then berths on another day of its window, but a vessel is never sent away. Refusals annoy customers, so the plan must refuse as few requests as possible. Every refusal counts the same.


Write the formulation first: a 0/1 variable per vessel and day of its window (days in berths only), a 0/1 "refused" variable per vessel, the rows that make every vessel berth once and keep each day within its berths, an elastic request row per vessel that the refusal variable relaxes, and an objective that counts refusals. Then write fewest_refusals(vessels, berths) to build it with MathOpt and solve it with HiGHS at a relative gap of 0 (mathopt.SolveParameters(relative_gap_tolerance=0.0)), so the number of refusals is a proven least, as in the lesson's own solves.


vessels maps a name to (first, last, wanted); berths maps a day to its free berths. Return (refused, schedule): the sorted names of the refused vessels, and a dict giving the day each vessel berths. When several answers refuse equally few, any of them is right: the tests check the number of refusals and that the schedule keeps every rule. About fifteen lines.

feasRelax Builds the Same Model in gurobipy


gurobipy builds this model for you. feasRelax(relaxobjtype, minrelax, vars, lbpen, ubpen, constrs, rhspen) relaxes the listed variable bounds and rows with the given penalties. relaxobjtype 0 minimizes the weighted sum of violations, 1 their weighted squares, and 2 the weighted count. With minrelax=True it then finds the best plan by the original objective among the least-violation repairs, our third objective. The call changes the model it is called on (Gurobi's reference calls it destructive), so it runs on a fresh copy each time. Its short form, feasRelaxS(relaxobjtype, minrelax, vrelax, crelax), takes no lists and no prices: vrelax=True lets every variable bound bend, crelax=True lets every row bend, and each bent unit costs 1 (gurobipy's docstring for feasRelaxS). So feasRelaxS(0, False, False, True) asks for the least total bending, one phase only, with every row elastic, the hard rows too, and no bound. That is too blunt for this scenario, where the hard rows must hold. feasRelax gets the same 30 rows and the same $k prices as the elastic model:






Gurobi's relaxation found the same least violation, 390, the same four bends and, with minrelax=True, the same best NPV, $17,060k. Its elastic variables are named after the rows, and reading three rows back shows they are this lesson's $o_s$ and $v_s$ under other names:



ArtN_ enters a row with $-1$, like $o_s$, and ArtP_ with $+1$, like $v_s$. A $\le$ row gets only the first, a $\ge$ row only the second, and an equation both. Two tools, one model, one answer. That is the check to make whenever a library builds a model for you.


Summary


  • An elastic variable lets a row bend, at a price: $a^\top x - o \le u$, $a^\top x + v \ge \ell$, with $o, v \ge 0$. The elastic model always has a plan, provided the hard rows can hold together, and the original is feasible exactly when the least violation is 0.
  • Every unit costing 1 mixes dollars, crews and projects. The flat repair broke the board's override because one project cost the same as $1k.
  • Penalties in one currency give a repair planners can price: $40k in 2028, a transmission crew-month in 2028 and in 2031, and five distribution crew-months in 2030. The best plan with that repair is worth $17,060k.
  • Counting rows asks "how few rules must give?": two here. Ties are common, and a plain count treats a must-do like any other row.
  • feasRelax builds the same model in gurobipy, and gave the same 390, the same bends and the same NPV.

Design Note: Hard Rows Are Part of the Question


Choosing $S$ is a modeling decision, as much as choosing prices. Leaving "once" and precedence rows hard is not caution. A bent "once" row would allow a project started twice, and a plan like that tells a planner nothing. A soft row should be one that a person could change in a meeting: a budget, a crew count, a floor, an override. If no such person exists, the row is hard.
