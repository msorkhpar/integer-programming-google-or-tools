The Budget Lock run of lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell came back INFEASIBLE. Before the team asks a solver which rules clash, a GridCap analyst asks something cheaper: is there a mistake in the input that would make any model fail? Most infeasible runs in planning work come from the data, not from the model: a window typed backwards, an extra zero on a cost, a crew count from the wrong spreadsheet, two alternatives both marked must-do. Each of these can be found by reading the input, with no solve at all, and named in the planners' own terms. This lesson writes five such checks, plants five errors to show what each one catches, and runs the checks on the Budget Lock scenario to show what they cannot catch. GridCap is a fictional utility; none of its numbers are real.

The Model

Two more rows come from the scenario: $\text{force}_i$ and $\text{force}_{i,f_i}$ ask for a project in any year or in year $f_i$, and $\text{drop}_i$ keeps it out. A project is required when it is must-do or forced in. Its possible start years are its window $T_i$, or only $f_i$ when the scenario names a year.

A Solver Says "Infeasible" Even When the Cause Is One Typo

presolve_checks.py plants five errors in copies of GridCap small, the way they arrive in real input, and asks the solver about each:

Planted errorRows it breaks
P017's window typed as 2030 to 2029$\text{must}_{\text{P017}}$ has no variable left
P039's first-year cost typed 11,700 instead of 1,170$\text{must}_{\text{P039}}$ against every $\text{budget}_y$
P003's crew need typed 3 instead of 1$\text{must}_{\text{P003}}$ against $\text{crews}_{\text{vegetation},2031}$
P001 and P002, alternatives, both marked must-do$\text{must}_{\text{P001}}$, $\text{must}_{\text{P002}}$, $\text{alt}_{\text{G001}}$
Plains floor typed 24,100 instead of 2,410$\text{floor}_{\text{Plains}}$

The solver's answer is the same five times: INFEASIBLE, and nothing else. It cannot tell a typo from a real conflict, and the next three lessons (IIS, elastic rows, assumptions) cost one or many more solves to find the cause. A check that reads the input finds each of these before the solve, and names it.

The first error deserves a closer look, because the model accepts it without complaint. With an empty window, P017 has no start variables, so its must-do row sums nothing and asks for that nothing to equal 1. MathOpt's fast_sum of nothing is still a linear expression, so the row is added and the model is simply infeasible. Python's built-in sum of nothing is the number 0, and 0 == 1 is the Python value False, which add_linear_constraint refuses with a TypeError. So a row whose sum can be empty is built from fast_sum, or from mathopt.LinearExpression() as the start of the sum, or skipped:

Either way, the real cause is the window, and only a check that reads the window says so.

Each Check Reads One Project, Group or Region Against One Limit

checks.py has five functions, one per cause. Each returns a list of Problem(check, subject, detail) records, so a report can say which check fired, what it is about, and the numbers. The first helper lists the required projects with the start years each may use, overrides included:

The five checks, each in one sentence and with the rows it protects:

CheckFires whenRow it protects

The budget check is typical. For each start year it asks whether some year of the project's work costs more than that year's budget. Only when every start year fails is there a problem:

Here is each planted error with the solver's answer and the checks' answer:

Each check fired exactly once, on its own error, and named the project, group or region with the numbers that clash. That message is what a planner needs: "P039's first-year cost is 11,700 and no year's budget is more than 8,890" points straight at the typo. The checks read only the input, and there are as many of them as there are projects, groups and regions, so they cost far less than a solve.

A Check Must Never Refuse a Scenario That Has a Plan

A check that fires on a scenario with a plan is worse than no check: the planners would chase a problem that does not exist, and soon stop trusting the checks. So every check here is sound: it fires only when the rows it protects cannot all hold, whatever the rest of the plan does. Each gets this by comparing a least demand with a most supply:

  • budget and crews look at one required project alone. If that project cannot fit in any start year with the whole budget or crew to itself, no plan can fit it.
  • floor adds the cost of every project in the region except those forced out, and ignores the alternative groups that would stop some of them being funded together. That total is more than any plan can spend in the region, so a floor above it can never be met.
  • group counts only required members, all of which every plan must fund.

The base data passes, as it must, because the base model has a plan worth $22,741k (lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell):

The direction of each comparison is what makes a check sound. Turn one around and it becomes unsound. Suppose the floor check added only the projects a good plan would fund. It would then fire whenever that plan fell short, even though another plan meets the floor. A sum over every project that is not forced out, as the lesson's check takes, is the most the region can get, so it cannot miss a floor that no plan meets.

    Five checks for a hospital's equipment program

    • five input checks, each comparing a least demand with a most supply

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A hospital plans its equipment replacements over a few years. Each item is installed in one year, pays its whole cost ($k) in that year and needs engineer_days of the biomedical engineers' time in that year. It may be installed in any plan year from its release year to its deadline year. Items marked required must be installed. An item may name another it excludes (the two need the same room, so at most one is installed). The finance office gives a budget per year and the engineering office engineers (days available) per year, both keyed by the plan's years. Each department has a dept_minimum: the least the plan must spend on that department in total.

Before the hospital's model is ever solved, the planners want the input checked for five known causes of "no plan":

CheckSubjectThe cause
"window"the itema required item has no plan year in its window
"budget"the itema required item costs more than the budget in every year it may use
"engineers"the itema required item needs more engineer-days than are available in every year it may use
"exclusion"both items' names, sorted, joined by "|"two required items exclude each other
"minimum"the departmenta department's minimum is more than all its items together cost

The plan years are the keys of budget; engineers has the same keys. For the "minimum" check, "all its items together" means every item of the department, required or optional, whatever its window. (Leaving out an item whose window misses every plan year is sound too, and the tests accept either.)

Write precheck(items, budget, engineers, dept_minimum) returning the sorted list of (check, subject) pairs, each pair once. Every check must be sound: it may fire only when no plan can exist, so a program with a plan always gets []. Work out, for each check, which quantity has to be the least possible demand and which the most possible supply. About twenty-five lines.

Passing Every Check Does Not Mean a Plan Exists


Now the Budget Lock scenario, first as finance locked it, and then with the transmission crews of 2030 and 2031 put back to GridCap's figures:






The checks found one cause: P017, a must-do, needs 13 transmission crew-months in its start year, and the scenario leaves 12 in each of the two years it may start (lesson Turning an IIS into a planner message prints the check's message in full). Restoring those crews clears the check, and the model is still infeasible. Something else clashes, and no single-project check can see it, because it needs several rows at once. The board forces P032 into 2028 and P023 into the plan, and P023 needs its parent P022 started at least a year earlier. P032 alone fits the locked 2028 budget, and so does P022. Together they do not (lesson Turning an IIS into a planner message works out the sums). That is a conflict between projects, and finding it takes the tools of the next lessons.


This is the shape of every pre-solve check: sound, but not complete. A check can prove "no plan", but passing every check proves nothing. Checks catch the common, cheap causes early and in plain words. The IIS (lesson IIS with MathOpt, gurobipy and Pyomo) and its relatives catch the rest.

Problem statement


A water utility's pipe program passes every single-job check: no required job, alone, costs more than the money it could use. Yet the model has no plan. The program's budget rolls over: money not spent in a year is added to the next year's. Each job is paid in full in the year it is done, and a required job must be done by the end of its deadline year.


So, by the end of any year $y$, the program must have paid for every required job whose deadline is $y$ or earlier, and it cannot have paid more than all the budgets up to $y$. A year where the first sum is larger than the second proves there is no plan, whatever else the model says. That is a check on several jobs at once, which the single-job checks cannot see.


Write cumulative_check(items, budget). items maps a job name to {"cost", "deadline", "required"}, and budget maps each plan year to its budget ($k). Return a list of (year, due, available) triples, in year order, one for every year where the required work due by the end of that year costs more than the money available by then. Return [] when no year falls short. About ten lines.

Summary


  • A solver says "infeasible" even when the cause is one typo. All five planted errors got the same INFEASIBLE, with no cause named.
  • Each check reads one project, group or region against one limit. Window, budget, crews, alternative group and regional floor: each fired once, on its own error, with the numbers.
  • A check must never refuse a scenario that has a plan. Compare a least demand with a most supply, and the check is sound. The base data passes.
  • Passing every check does not mean a plan exists. The Budget Lock scenario with its crews restored passes all five and is still infeasible: its conflict joins several projects.
