  15. Predictive vs. prescriptive

GridCap's asset analytics team has shipped a model. For every asset it predicts a probability of failure (PoF) and a remaining useful life (RUL). At the planning meeting someone says: "We have the risk scores now. Fund the riskiest assets first and we're done." That sentence mixes up two jobs. A prediction says what is likely to happen if nothing changes. A prescription says what to do about it, given the money and the rules. This lesson draws the line between them, in four rules.

A Prediction Describes the Future Without You; a Prescription Chooses What You Do

The analytics model hands over, per asset, the shape and scale of a Weibull failure curve (section 7 explains them), an age, an RUL and a consequence of failure (CoF): what one failure costs in outages, repairs and penalties. The first thing a planner can use is the chance the asset fails next year, given that it is working today:

Multiply by the consequence and you get the risk, the expected failure cost per year, in $k:

Everything in this table is a description. It says nothing about which project to fund, because it knows nothing about costs, budgets or rules. Look at A004: an RUL of 1 year, yet a PoF of only 3.4% next year. RUL and PoF come from different questions (when is the asset "used up", and how likely is a failure in the next twelve months) and they do not have to agree. A planner who reads "RUL 1" as "fails next year" overstates the urgency by a factor the table does not show.

Ranking by Risk Is Not a Plan

"Fund the riskiest first" is a rule for building a plan, and it is the greedy rule of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model with a different sort key. To compare it fairly, give each project the risk it removes: a replacement makes the asset new, and a repair or a trim takes it back to half its age.

Now fund the riskiest assets first, and separately search every plan for the one that removes the most risk within the rules:

The risk ranking funds both the replacement and the repair of the same transformer (P006 and P007), which counts the same risk twice, and it drops the must-do P002. Its 674.0 looks better than the optimized 436.3 only because it is not a plan the utility can carry out. The ranking is still useful: it tells you where the risk is. Deciding what to do about it is a different step.

Stepping back: the prediction gives you the coefficients (risk per asset, risk avoided per project). The optimization gives you the decision (which projects, within which rules). Merging the two steps into "sort by risk" throws away the rules.

Predictions Enter the Model as Coefficients, and Their Errors Travel With Them


Every PoF is an estimate. If the analytics model is biased for one asset class, how wrong does it have to be before the plan changes? Scale every distribution asset's PoF and solve again:






The plan holds from half to double the predicted PoF. It has changed by the time the true PoFs are 0.3 of the prediction, a model that overstates distribution risk about three times over. That is the answer the director needs about the analytics model: not "is the model accurate?" but "is it accurate enough for this decision?" Section 9 turns this into a habit (sensitivity analysis), and section 10 makes sure that when the plan changes, it changes because the data did and not because the solver ran differently.


The Model Owns the Trade-off, and the Planner Owns the Decision


Three parties touch a capital plan, and confusing their jobs is how bad plans get approved:


  • The predictive model estimates PoF, RUL and consequences. It is judged on accuracy.
  • The optimization model finds the best plan for stated rules and an objective. It is judged on whether its plan is feasible, optimal for the question asked, and reproducible.
  • The planner chooses the objective, signs off the rules, and decides whether to adopt the plan.


When a plan looks wrong, the first question is which of the three is wrong: a bad coefficient, a missing rule, or the wrong objective. The optimizer cannot tell you which, and section 9 is about the tools that help.

    • 

  • the plan is the allowed plan with the largest total risk avoided
    • 

  • funding the biggest risk first is not the best plan
    • 

  • the same station's risk is never counted twice
    • 













Summary


  • A prediction describes the future without you; a prescription chooses what you do. PoF, RUL and CoF are inputs, and PoF next year is conditional on the asset working today.
  • Ranking by risk is not a plan. It ignores costs and rules, can fund two alternatives on one asset and can drop a must-do.
  • Predictions enter the model as coefficients, and their errors travel with them. Ask how wrong a coefficient can be before the plan changes.
  • The model owns the trade-off, and the planner owns the decision. When a plan looks wrong, find out whether the coefficient, the rule or the objective is at fault.

Design Note: Why the Risk Coefficient Is Conditional


The unconditional probability $F(a+1) - F(a)$ is the chance that a new asset fails in exactly that year of its life. For an old asset it is small, because most assets of its kind would have failed earlier, and the one in front of you did not. On A006's curve:



The unconditional number peaks near 50 and falls, so a 90-year-old transformer would look safer than a 30-year-old one. Planners care about the asset that is still standing, so the course always divides by $1 - F(a)$. The cost is one more term to explain; the gain is a risk that grows with age, as the asset's real condition does.
