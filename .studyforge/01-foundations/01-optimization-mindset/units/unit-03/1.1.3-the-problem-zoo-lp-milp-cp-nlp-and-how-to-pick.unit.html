  16. The problem zoo

A colleague has written the tiny GridCap selection model and asks which solver to use. The OR-Tools documentation offers GLOP, PDLP, HiGHS, SCIP, CP-SAT and a routing library. Picking from that list by name is guesswork. The choice follows from two questions about the model: are the variables continuous or whole numbers, and are the objective and the constraints straight lines? This lesson solves one GridCap question four ways, one per problem class, in four rules.

A Linear Program Allows Fractions, and Solves Fast Because It Does

A linear program (LP) has continuous variables, a linear objective and linear constraints: every term is a number times one variable. Here is the tiny selection model with each $x_i$ allowed anywhere between 0 and 1, solved with GLOP, OR-Tools' LP solver:

The answer funds 9.9% of the tower set and 9.9% of the breaker bay. The utility cannot build a tenth of a tower set. For allocating money across programs, or flows on a network, fractions are fine and the LP is the right model. For yes/no decisions the LP answer is not a plan. It is still useful: no plan can be worth more than $3,770.1k, because the LP had every plan available and more besides. A number that no plan can beat is called a bound (an upper bound when you maximize). The same model with its whole-number requirement dropped is the model's LP relaxation, and its value is always such a bound. A bound says nothing about which plan is best: until a solver proves otherwise, the best plan may be worth anything between the best plan found so far and the bound. Section 3 builds on that bound.

Allowing fractions is also what makes an LP fast. The best point can be reached by moving steadily toward better values along the rules' boundary, and section 2 shows how. A model with yes/no variables has no such path between plans, so its solver has to search, which is the next rule.

A Mixed-Integer Program Adds Whole-Number Variables, and the Search That Comes With Them

Mark the variables as integer (is_integer=True), and the same model is a mixed-integer linear program (MILP). Solved with HiGHS:

That is the plan brute force found in lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model, and $3,627k is below the LP's $3,770.1k, as it must be. The gap between the two numbers is what section 3 is about: a MILP solver solves many LPs, branching on fractional variables until the fractions are gone.

Why not solve the LP and round its fractions? Because the best whole-number plan need not be near the LP's answer. The OR-Tools documentation shows this with a two-variable model (its MathOpt sample integer_programming.py), adapted here to solve it both ways:

The LP puts everything into $y$ and stops at $y = 2.5$. Rounding $y$ up breaks (c1); rounding it down gives an allowed point worth 20. The best whole-number point is worth 23, and it sits at $x = 3$, where the LP had $x = 0$. Rounding gave a worse plan or a broken rule; finding the best whole-number plan takes the MILP solver's search. (The sample writes $x \le 3.5$ as a second row and solves with SCIP; here it is a bound on the variable, and HiGHS solves the MILP. Both give 23 at $(3, 2)$.)

"Mixed" means some variables may stay continuous. A capital plan with binary fund decisions and continuous spend per year amounts is a MILP, and it is the main model class of this course and of the job it prepares you for.

Every MathOpt Program Has the Same Five Parts

The LP and the MILP above are one Python function, and every MathOpt model in this course has its shape. Read it once, part by part:

  1. A model. mathopt.Model(name=...) is an empty container. Nothing is solved yet.
  2. Variables, with their domains. add_variable(lb=0, ub=1, is_integer=...) is $0 \le x_i \le 1$, and is_integer=True makes it $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$. add_binary_variable(name=...) is a shorter way to write that binary. Keep each variable in a dict by its project id, because the rows and the answer are read through it.
  3. Constraints. add_linear_constraint(...) takes one comparison of linear expressions: <=, >= or ==. Sums of number × variable are written with Python's sum and *. The name is the row's label in the formulation (budget, must_P002), and it is what you will search for when a model misbehaves.
  4. An objective. model.maximize(...) or model.minimize(...), once.
  5. A solve, and a status before anything else. The rest of the program:

mathopt.solve(model, solver) returns a result. result.termination.reason says how the solve ended: OPTIMAL means the plan it returns is proven best. result.objective_value() is the plan's value, and result.variable_values(v) is one variable's value in it. Read the status first, because when no plan satisfies every rule there is no value to read. Give the tiny portfolio a budget of $2,000k, less than the must-do P002 costs, and the same kind of model answers:

INFEASIBLE is a proof that the rules contradict each other, not a failure of the solver. Section 9 shows how to find which rows are responsible. The last line is every way a solve can end. Only OPTIMAL comes with a proven best plan; FEASIBLE means a plan was found but not proven best (a time limit stopped the search, section 3), and every other status means there is no plan to read. So a program checks for OPTIMAL (or accepts FEASIBLE knowingly) before it asks for a value.

Problem statement

A harbor authority keeps its quay repairs in a list of Project records: a cost, a value (both in $k) and a must_do flag for repairs an inspector has ordered. Its analysts want one function that builds the selection model, solves it and hands back an answer they can trust, including the day the budget cannot even cover the ordered repairs.

Write solve_plan(projects, budget) in solve.py. It builds and solves this model with HiGHS, with $P$ the projects, $M \subseteq P$ the must-do ones and $B$ the budget:

It returns a tuple (status, value, plan). status is the name of the solve's termination reason ("OPTIMAL", "INFEASIBLE", ...). When the status is OPTIMAL, value is the objective value and plan the frozenset of funded project ids; otherwise both are None, because a solve that found no plan has no value to report.

Constraint Programming Searches Over Logic and Integers Directly


CP-SAT, OR-Tools' constraint programming solver, works on integer and Boolean variables and lets you state some rules as logic rather than arithmetic:






Same answer. The difference is in what each solver is good at. CP-SAT has no continuous variables, so a cost of $2,150.5k has to be scaled to integers, and it shines on scheduling, sequencing and dense logic (lesson When CP-SAT beats MIP, and when it doesn't measures when). A MILP solver handles continuous quantities directly and proves optimality through LP bounds. For GridCap's selection both work; for a crew schedule with no-overlap rules, CP-SAT is usually the better tool.


A Nonlinear Program Has Curves, and Curves Cost You Guarantees


Some relationships are not straight lines. Suppose each program's value shows diminishing returns, $v_k(s) = a_k \sqrt{s}$: the thousandth dollar of vegetation work buys less than the first. Splitting the budget to maximize total value is a nonlinear program (NLP):







A curve is concave when it bends downward, like $\sqrt{s}$: each extra dollar buys less, and a straight line between two points of the curve lies below it. It is convex when it bends upward, like $s^2$. When you maximize a sum of concave curves (or minimize a sum of convex ones) under linear rules, any point that no small step improves is the best point overall. This one is easy because the curve is concave. Without that shape, a local NLP method can stop at a point that is only the best in its neighborhood, and it will not tell you. It still respects the budget and every other constraint; the risk is which optimum it stops at, not whether the point is allowed. The usual move in capital planning is to approximate the curve with straight pieces and stay in MILP, where the solver proves optimality (section 6.6).

The Model


The LP and the MILP of this lesson are one model with one difference, the domain of $x_i$. With the sets and parameters of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model:



The CP-SAT model states the same rows over Boolean variables, with (parent$_c$) written as the implication $x_c \Rightarrow x_{p(c)}$ and (alt$_g$) as "at most one". The NLP of the last section is a different model: one continuous variable $s_k$ per program, the objective $\max \sum_k a_k \sqrt{s_k}$, the row $\sum_k s_k = 7{,}390$ and the bounds $s_k \ge 0$.


Summary


  • An LP allows fractions and solves fast because it does. Use it for amounts; for yes/no decisions, use its value as an upper bound, not as a plan.
  • A MILP adds whole-number variables and a search. It is the default model for capital planning, and its answer is never better than its LP relaxation.
  • CP searches over logic and integers directly. It suits scheduling and dense logic, and needs integer data.
  • Every MathOpt program has the same five parts. A model, variables with their domains, named rows, one objective, and a solve whose status you read before its values.
  • An NLP has curves and loses guarantees unless they bend the right way: concave when you maximize, convex when you minimize. Approximating a curve with linear pieces keeps you in MILP.

Design Note: One Model, Several Solvers


MathOpt lets the same model run on GLOP, HiGHS, SCIP or Gurobi by changing one argument, which is how this lesson switched between LP and MILP. The cost is that you can use only what every chosen solver understands; a feature one solver lacks is refused when you solve (lesson Common vs. solver-specific parameters, and what each solver rejects). Section 4 treats that trade in detail.
