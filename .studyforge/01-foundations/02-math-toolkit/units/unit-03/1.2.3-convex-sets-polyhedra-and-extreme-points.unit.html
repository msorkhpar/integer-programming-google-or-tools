GridCap's maintenance manager has to split part of the 2028 money between two programs: distribution maintenance ($d$, in $k) and vegetation management ($v$, in $k). Unlike the projects of lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy, these are amounts, not yes/no choices: any value between the limits will do. There are infinitely many allowed splits, so the brute force of lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model cannot list them. Yet the best one can be found by checking five points. This lesson shows why: what shape the allowed splits form, where on that shape the best split must lie, and why the yes/no decisions of earlier lessons lose that shape. Five rules, one per heading.

The numbers below are round on purpose; GridCap is a fictional utility and this is a teaching example on it, not any real utility's data.

Each Linear Row Cuts Space in Half, and the Rows Together Make a Polyhedron

The manager's rules, written as rows of $Ax \le b$ (lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy), with the plan $(d, v)$:

  • budget: $d + v \le 3000$, the money available;
  • crews: $d + 2v \le 4000$, crew-days, since a $k of vegetation work takes two crew-days and a $k of distribution work one;
  • veg_cap: $v \le 1500$, only so many miles of line need clearing;
  • dist_floor: $d \ge 500$, a commitment to the regulator, written $-d \le -500$;
  • veg_nonneg: $v \ge 0$, written $-v \le 0$.

The objective is risk removed, 2 points per $k on distribution and 3 per $k on vegetation: maximize $2d + 3v$.

The plan has two variables, $d$ and $v$, so each plan is a point in a plane: two dimensions, one per variable. A model with $n$ variables has its points in $n$ dimensions, one coordinate per variable, and "$n$ dimensions" in this lesson always means "$n$ variables".

Picture one row on a chart with $d$ across and $v$ up. The points where $d + v = 3000$ form a straight line, and the line splits the plane in two: on one side $d + v < 3000$, on the other $d + v > 3000$. The row keeps one side and the line itself. That kept side is a half-space (in two dimensions, a half-plane).

Every row keeps a half-space, and a feasible point must be in all of them at once. The set of points that satisfy every row of $Ax \le b$ is therefore an intersection of half-spaces, which is what a polyhedron is:

In two dimensions a bounded polyhedron is a polygon. Here it has five straight edges, one per row, because each of these five rows bounds it somewhere.

A Polyhedron Is Convex: The Segment Between Two Feasible Points Stays Feasible

Take two feasible splits, $p = (500, 0)$ and $q = (1000, 1500)$, and walk the straight segment between them. Each point on it is $(1 - \lambda)\,p + \lambda\, q$ for a $\lambda$ between 0 and 1: $\lambda = 0$ is $p$, $\lambda = 1$ is $q$, and $\lambda = 0.5$ is the midpoint. Such a point is a convex combination of $p$ and $q$.

It is a special case of a wider idea. A linear combination of vectors $p$ and $q$ is $\alpha\, p + \beta\, q$ for any two numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$, negative or larger than 1 included; with more vectors, each gets its own number. A convex combination is the linear combination whose numbers are not negative and add up to 1, here $\alpha = 1 - \lambda$ and $\beta = \lambda$. Linear combinations reach the whole plane; convex combinations of two points reach only the segment between them.

A set is convex when this always happens: for any two of its points, the whole segment between them is in the set. Five sample points are not a proof, but the proof is two lines. If $Ap \le b$ and $Aq \le b$, and $0 \le \lambda \le 1$, then

The first step is how matrix products distribute; the inequality holds because $1 - \lambda$ and $\lambda$ are not negative, so multiplying by them keeps each $\le$ the right way round. So every polyhedron is convex, whatever $A$ and $b$ are.

Why care? Because in a convex set there are no hidden pockets: if a point is better than where you stand, the straight path towards it stays feasible. Section 2's algorithms rely on this.

Extreme Points Are Where Two Rows Are Tight at Once

An extreme point (or vertex) of a polyhedron is a feasible point that is not the midpoint of any segment between two other feasible points: a corner. In two dimensions a corner is where two edges meet, so two rows are tight there (met with equality, lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy) and their lines cross.

That gives a recipe. For each pair of rows, solve the two equations $a_i^\top x = b_i$ and $a_k^\top x = b_k$ together. If the rows are parallel there is no single crossing point. If the crossing breaks some other row, it lies outside the polygon. Otherwise it is a vertex.

np.linalg.solve solves the 2-by-2 system. np.linalg.det is the determinant of the pair. For a 2-by-2 matrix with rows $(p, q)$ and $(r, s)$ it is the number $ps - qr$. It is 0 exactly when one row is a multiple of the other, so the two lines are parallel and do not cross at a single point, and then there is nothing to solve:

The budget and crews rows, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$, cross at one point. The rows of veg_cap, $(0, 1)$, and veg_nonneg, $(0, -1)$, are multiples of each other ($-1$ times), so their determinant is 0. The loop over all ten pairs prints:

Ten pairs, five vertices. budget & veg_cap crosses at (1500, 1500), which needs 4,500 crew-days, so it lies outside. veg_cap and veg_nonneg are both "v equals a number": parallel lines.

In $n$ dimensions the rule is the same with $n$ in place of two: a feasible point is a vertex when, among the rows tight there, $n$ are linearly independent, meaning none of those $n$ rows is a linear combination of the others, so together they pin down a single point. The rank of a set of rows is the largest number of them that are linearly independent, and NumPy's np.linalg.matrix_rank computes it:

The three rows have rank 2, not 3: $(1, 1)$ is $(1, 0) + (0, 1)$, so it adds no new direction. Any two of them are independent. $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$ have rank 1, because the second is twice the first: the same line, the zero-determinant case above.

To test a point, keep only its tight rows. A boolean mask (lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy) picks rows out of a matrix: A[mask] is the rows where the mask is True.

At (2000, 1000) two rows are tight and their rank is 2, the number of variables: a vertex. At (1000, 500), inside the polygon, no row is tight. A[tight] is then an empty matrix, 0 rows by 2 columns, and matrix_rank returns 0 for it, fewer than 2: not a vertex.

Problem statement

A materials yard plans how many tonnes of each material to stock, under rules written as rows of $Ax \le b$. Its optimizer reports a plan, and the yard's analyst wants to check that the plan is a corner of the allowed region, as the optimum of a linear objective can always be chosen to be.

Write is_extreme_point(A, b, x, tol=1e-9) in extreme.py. A has one column per variable, so there are $n$ = A.shape[1] variables. Return True when x is an extreme point of $\{x : Ax \le b\}$:

  1. x is feasible: every row has $a_r^\top x \le b_r + \text{tol}$;
  2. among the tight rows, those with $|a_r^\top x - b_r| \le \text{tol}$, there are $n$ linearly independent ones. np.linalg.matrix_rank(M) returns how many independent rows M has.

Otherwise return False. Return a Python bool. The function must work for any number of variables, not only two.

About six lines.

The best vertex removes 7,000 risk points. Could a split that is not a corner do better? Search a grid of 496 splits, every $100k in each direction, and then ask GLOP, OR-Tools' linear programming solver (lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick):



Both land on the vertex budget & crews. That is not luck. The points where $2d + 3v$ takes one value form a straight line, and raising the value slides that line, parallel to itself, across the polygon. The last point it touches before leaving is a corner, or a whole edge if the objective is parallel to one, and an edge ends in corners. Written as a rule: if a linear program over a polyhedron with at least one vertex has an optimum, some vertex is optimal. So with the vertices in hand, the best one is a single max:




The OR-Tools documentation makes the same point with its first LP example ("Solving an LP Problem"), which finds the optimum by sliding the objective line across the feasible region until it last touches it. Adapted here to MathOpt, with every vertex listed beside GLOP's answer:







GLOP's answer is the best of the four vertices, (6, 4), where c1 and c3 are tight. Note the >= row: MathOpt takes it as written, and only the vertex search flips it into $-3x + y \le 0$. The corner (0, 0) has three tight rows in two dimensions; any two of them are independent, so it is a vertex all the same, and the search reaches it from three different pairs (the program lists it once).


This is the idea the simplex method of lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) is built on: it walks from vertex to vertex, each time to a better neighbour, instead of trying every pair of rows, which grows fast with the number of rows and variables.

Problem statement


A bakery plans how many oven hours go to each of two products, under rules written as rows of $Ax \le b$, and wants the plan with the highest objective $c^\top x$. The region is a polygon, and the best plan can be found at one of its corners.


Write best_corner(A, b, c, tol=1e-9) in corner.py, for two variables. Try every pair of rows $i < k$:


  1. If the two rows are parallel ($|\det| < 10^{-12}$ for the 2-by-2 matrix of the pair), skip the pair.
  2. Otherwise solve $a_i^\top x = b_i$, $a_k^\top x = b_k$ with np.linalg.solve.
  3. Keep the crossing only if it is feasible: every row has $a_r^\top x \le b_r + \text{tol}$.


Return (value, (x0, x1)) for the kept crossing with the largest $c^\top x$, as Python floats. Return None if no crossing is kept. You may assume the region has at least one corner and that the objective does not grow without limit on it.

Yes/No Decisions Break Convexity, and That Is Why Integer Programming Is Hard


Return to the tiny GridCap projects of lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy, where each $x_j$ is 0 or 1. Take two of its 8 feasible plans and their midpoint:






The midpoint satisfies every row, as convexity promises, and it funds half of P007 and half of P008, which is not a plan. The set of allowed plans is a scattering of separate points inside the polyhedron, and a set of separate points is not convex.


That breaks the shortcut of the previous section. Drop the 0/1 requirement, let each $x_j$ be anything from 0 to 1 (the LP relaxation of lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick), and GLOP finds the best vertex of the polyhedron:



It funds 0.655 of P006 and 0.345 of P007, and claims $2,039.0k against the $1,396k of the best real plan (lesson Vectors, matrices and Ax ≤ b in NumPy). Every allowed plan is itself a corner of this polyhedron, since each of its $x_j$ sits on a bound, 0 or 1. But the polyhedron has other corners too, fractional ones, and here the best corner is one of those. So "the best is at a vertex" finds the best fractional point, not the best plan. Rounding the fractions is no fix either; section 3 shows why, and what solvers do instead.


The Model


The two-variable split this lesson solved, complete.


Sets. $K = \{d, v\}$, the two programs (distribution maintenance, vegetation management).


Parameters (round teaching numbers on fictional GridCap). Budget 3,000 ($k); crew-days 4,000, with 1 crew-day per $k of distribution and 2 per $k of vegetation; vegetation cap 1,500 ($k); distribution floor 500 ($k); risk points removed per $k, 2 for distribution and 3 for vegetation.


Variables. $d \ge 0$ and $v \ge 0$, continuous, in $k.


Objective.



Constraints.



Its feasible set is a polygon with five vertices, and its optimum is the vertex $(d, v) = (2000, 1000)$, where budget and crews are both tight, with objective 7,000.


Summary


  • Each linear row keeps a half-space, and the rows together make a polyhedron, the set $\{x : Ax \le b\}$.
  • A polyhedron is convex. The segment between two feasible points stays feasible, for any $A$ and $b$.
  • Extreme points are where enough rows are tight at once: two in two dimensions, $n$ linearly independent ones in $n$.
  • A linear objective is best at an extreme point. GLOP and a grid search both agree with the best of the five vertices.
  • Yes/no decisions break convexity. The midpoint of two plans is not a plan, and the LP relaxation's best vertex can be fractional.

Design Note: Enumerating Vertices Is a Lesson, Not a Method


Trying every pair of rows works for five rows in two dimensions. With $m$ rows and $n$ variables the number of candidate corners is "$m$ choose $n$", which for a model of a few hundred rows and variables is far beyond counting. The point of this lesson is the geometry, not the enumeration: it tells you where an LP's optimum lives, and why the 0/1 models of section 3 need more than an LP solver.
