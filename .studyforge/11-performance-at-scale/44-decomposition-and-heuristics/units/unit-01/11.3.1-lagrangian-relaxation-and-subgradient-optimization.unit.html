GridCap's planners solve the multi-year plan of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years every week, and on the small portfolio the solver answers at once. The board now asks a different kind of question: "whatever plan you bring us, how far could it be from the best one?" A plan answers half of that. The other half is a bound: a number that no plan can beat, proved without searching through plans. Lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap showed where a MIP solver gets its bound (the LP relaxation and the search tree). This lesson builds a bound of a different kind, by hand, from one observation: in the plan model only the five yearly budget rows tie the 40 projects together. Put a price on each year's money instead of a hard limit, and the model falls apart into dozens of tiny problems that are solved by inspection. That is Lagrangian relaxation, and the method that finds good prices is subgradient optimization.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio; none of its numbers are real.

The Model

The plan model is lesson Cash-flow spreading across years's, with lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms's cumulative precedence rows. Every program in this module builds it with capital.build.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k), so that started in $t$ it spends $c_{i,y-t}$ in year $y$ (zero outside its years of work); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. In code, x[i, t].

Objective.

Constraints.

$z^*$ names the optimal NPV. On GridCap small, $z^* = 24{,}260$ ($k), the optimum of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years.

A Complicating Row Ties Everything Together; Without It the Model Splits

Look at which projects each row mentions. A once row mentions one project. An alt row mentions the two or three projects of one group, an after row a parent and its child. The budget rows are different: each one mentions every project that could be at work in that year, which is nearly all of them. These rows are the complicating constraints: delete them, and what is left is a set of small problems that share no row, each solvable on its own.

lagrange.components finds those small problems by joining projects that share a row other than a budget row, alternatives and parent-child pairs:

A group of two projects with two start years each has at most $3 \times 3 = 9$ choices (each project: not started, or one of its start years), so each group is solved by trying every choice. On the medium portfolio the same function finds 364 groups of at most two projects: the work grows in step with the number of projects, not exponentially. Dropping the rows, however, throws away the very rule that makes planning hard; a plan built without them overspends. Lagrangian relaxation keeps a trace of the rows in the objective.

Any Nonnegative Prices on the Budget Rows Give an Upper Bound

Give each year a price $\lambda_y \ge 0$ per $k of spend, and reward a plan for every $k it leaves unspent (or charge it for every $k it overspends):

This is the Lagrangian relaxation of the budget rows, and $\lambda$ are its Lagrange multipliers. $L(\lambda)$ is an upper bound on $z^*$ for every $\lambda \ge 0$, by a two-line argument. Take the optimal plan $x^*$. It meets every budget row, so $B_y - \text{spend}_y(x^*) \ge 0$, and with $\lambda_y \ge 0$ the added term is $\ge 0$; so the Lagrangian objective at $x^*$ is at least $z^*$. And $x^*$ meets the rows left in the relaxation, so it is one of the plans the max looks at; the max is at least its value. Together: $L(\lambda) \ge z^*$. The sign of $\lambda$ matters: a negative price would reward overspending and break the first step. This is the same argument as weak duality in lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness, with integer variables kept integer.

Rearranged, the relaxation scores every start with its own priced value, NPV minus the price of the money it spends, and adds the constant $\sum_y \lambda_y B_y$:

So each group picks, independently, the choice with the largest total priced value (starting nothing is worth 0; a must-do has to start even when every start is worth less than 0):

Every bound sits above 24,260, as the argument says it must, and enumeration and solver agree. The prices change the bound a lot. At $\lambda = 0$ money is free: 38 of 40 projects start and the bound is the sum of every project's best NPV. At $\lambda = 1$ money costs a whole dollar of NPV per dollar, only 4 projects are worth starting, and the bound is dominated by the constant $\sum_y B_y$ of unspent money. Problem statement

A telecom's fiber program plans which ring builds to start in which year. Each build has a value for every year it may start in, and needs splicing-crew hours in each of its years of work: started in year $t$, build $i$ uses $h_{i,0}$ hours in $t$, $h_{i,1}$ in $t+1$, and so on. Each year has $H_y$ crew hours. Some builds are must-dos. The full model, with $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$:

The crew rows are the only rows that tie builds together. The planners want a quick upper bound on the best plan's value, for any set of nonnegative prices $\mu_y$ per crew hour.

Write the Lagrangian relaxation of the crew rows first: the bound $L(\mu)$ as a formula, and what each build chooses on its own once those rows are priced. Then write crew_bound(builds, hours, prices) to return (bound, plan): the value $L(\mu)$ and the relaxed plan that reaches it, as a dict from build name to start year (builds not started are left out). builds[name] holds "value" (start year to value), "hours" (hours by year of work, a list) and "must" (a bool); hours and prices are dicts by year, with a price for every year a build can be worked in. When two choices of a build are worth exactly the same, either may be returned.

No solver is needed: about ten lines of plain Python.

Subgradient Steps Raise the Price of an Overspent Year and Lower the Price of an Underspent One


$L(\lambda)$ is the largest of many linear functions of $\lambda$ (one per plan), so it is convex but has corners, and there is no derivative to follow at a corner. What is always available is a subgradient. Let $x^\lambda$ be the plan that reached $L(\lambda)$. Then the vector



satisfies $L(\mu) \ge L(\lambda) + \sum_y g_y (\mu_y - \lambda_y)$ for every $\mu \ge 0$, because the plan $x^\lambda$ is still available at prices $\mu$ and is worth exactly the right side there. So moving against $g$ is the only direction in which the bound can fall. In words: if the relaxed plan overspends year $y$ ($g_y < 0$), raise $\lambda_y$; if it leaves money unspent ($g_y > 0$), lower it, but never below zero:

A Relaxed Plan Repaired Into a Feasible One Gives the Lower Bound


$x^\lambda$ ignores the budgets, so it usually overspends. A Lagrangian heuristic repairs it: repair drops, while some year overspends, the optional project that earns least NPV per dollar spent in the overspent years (never a must-do, never a parent whose child stays), then adds back, best ratio first, any project that still fits every rule. Every repaired plan is feasible, so its NPV is a lower bound $\underline{z} \le z^*$, and the best one so far is the $\underline{z}$ in Polyak's step. With an upper bound from the prices and a lower bound from a plan, each iteration reports a gap, as a MIP solver does (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap):



Read it as a race between two numbers. The bound falls from 36,852 to 26,642.5 in 60 iterations, fast at first and slowly at the end, which is typical of subgradient methods. The prices zigzag early (iteration 2 overshoots 2030 to 1.453, iteration 3 drops it back), then settle between 0.34 and 0.64. The repaired plans climb from 22,474 to 23,369. Both bounds are on the right side of the true optimum, 24,260: the Lagrangian bound $2,382.5k above it, the plan $891k below it. The 14.01% gap is the honest statement the board asked for: "our plan is worth 23,369, and no plan is worth more than 26,642.5".


When the Pieces' Own LP Has Whole-Number Answers, the Best Bound Is the LP Bound


Look at the second-last output line. The LP relaxation of the full model (lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap) gives 26,587.4, and the Lagrangian bound is creeping down toward it, not toward 24,260. That is a theorem, not an accident of the step rule. If the relaxed problem (once, must, alt and after rows) has an LP relaxation whose optimum is always a 0/1 plan, its integrality property, then adding the integer requirement gives the relaxed problem nothing, and by LP duality the best Lagrangian bound equals the LP bound: $z_{LD} = z_{LP}$. Each group here is a choice of at most one start among a handful, which is such a problem. So on this model the subgradient loop can, at best, reproduce the bound the LP relaxation gives in one solve.


The argument takes three steps. Write $X$ for the 0/1 plans that keep the once, must, alt and after rows, and $\bar X$ for the plans that keep the same rows with each $x_{i,t}$ allowed anywhere in $[0, 1]$ (their LP relaxation).


  1. For fixed prices, the max over $X$ equals the max over $\bar X$. At fixed $\lambda$, the objective of $L(\lambda)$ is linear in $x$. The integrality property says that an LP over $\bar X$ has a 0/1 optimum, and that optimum is in $X$. So allowing fractions gains nothing, and $L(\lambda)$ is also the optimum of an LP over $\bar X$.
  2. Then $z_{LD}$ is the dual of an LP. By step 1, $z_{LD} = \min_{\lambda \ge 0} L(\lambda)$ prices the budget rows of an LP: maximize NPV over $\bar X$ subject to the budget rows. That LP is the LP relaxation of the full model, with optimum $z_{LP}$.
  3. LP duality closes the gap. For an LP, the best prices on some of its rows give a bound equal to its optimum (strong duality, lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness). So $z_{LD} = z_{LP}$.


Without the property, step 1 fails in one direction only. Fractions can then raise the max, so the max over $X$ can be lower than the max over $\bar X$, and $z_{LD} \le z_{LP}$, sometimes strictly. That is the case the third reason below relies on.


Why use it, then? Three reasons hold in practice:


  • Scale. The bound is computed by enumerating tiny groups, never by solving an LP of the whole model. When the model is too large to load into one LP, or must be bounded in parallel, this still works.
  • Prices that mean something. The final $\lambda$ are prices on each year's money. The last output line prints the LP relaxation's budget-row duals (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver): 0.640, 0.547, 0.465, 0.422, 0.316, against the loop's 0.640, 0.557, 0.457, 0.467, 0.340. The loop found nearly the same prices without ever solving an LP.
  • Stronger bounds from other relaxations. If the relaxed problem lacks the integrality property, $z_{LD}$ can be strictly below $z_{LP}$. Relaxing the once and alt rows instead would keep the five budget rows in the relaxed problem: a knapsack with five rows, which has no integrality property. Its best bound can then beat the LP bound, at the price of solving that knapsack as a MIP in every iteration, and without the split into tiny groups.


Lesson Fixed-integer LP duals, and when they mean something priced the budget rows a different way: it fixed the integer plan and read the LP's duals. Those prices explain one plan. Lagrangian prices are the other way round: they are chosen first, and the plan follows from them.


The Model


The Lagrangian dual of the budget rows, whole. New are the multipliers $\lambda_y$, one per budget row; the budget rows move from the constraints into the objective.


Sets, parameters. As in the plan model above.


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$ (inner problem), and $\lambda_y \ge 0$ for every $y \in Y$ (outer problem), in $k of NPV per $k of spend.


Objective.



Constraints of the inner problem.



For every $\lambda \ge 0$: $z^* \le z_{LD} \le L(\lambda)$, and here $z_{LD} = z_{LP}$.


Summary


  • A complicating row ties everything together; without it the model splits. The yearly budget rows are the only rows that mention most projects; without them GridCap small falls into 36 groups of at most 2 projects.
  • Any nonnegative prices on the budget rows give an upper bound. $L(\lambda) \ge z^*$ for every $\lambda \ge 0$, by weak duality; each group picks its best choice at the priced values.
  • Subgradient steps raise the price of an overspent year and lower the price of an underspent one. $g_y = B_y - \text{spend}_y(x^\lambda)$, projected onto $\lambda \ge 0$, with Polyak's step; keep the best bound, since a step can make it worse.
  • A relaxed plan repaired into a feasible one gives the lower bound. The two bounds bracket the optimum and give a gap.
  • When the pieces' own LP has whole-number answers, the best bound is the LP bound. Here $z_{LD} = z_{LP} = 26{,}587.4$; a stronger bound needs a relaxed problem without that property.

Design Note: Keep Both Bounds and the Iteration in Every Report


A subgradient loop that prints only its last $L(\lambda)$ hides two things: that the bound zigzags (the last value is not the best), and how far the bound is from any plan. Reporting the best bound, the best plan and the iteration together turns the loop into the same kind of evidence a MIP log gives (lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line), and lets the reader check the one fact that must always hold: the bound above the plan.
