GridCap's planners have one more rule to respect. A transmission or distribution project takes its equipment out of service while it is worked on, and the network can spare equipment only in certain seasons. The utility has one specialist outage crew for the whole network, so in any year the outage jobs of the projects starting that year must fit, one after another, each inside its season. That is a scheduling question (which week each job begins), and the natural tool for it is CP-SAT with interval variables (lessons Interval variables and NoOverlap and Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows), not an LP. Benders as lesson Benders decomposition built it needs the subproblem's duals, and a scheduling model has none. Logic-based Benders decomposition (LBBD), due to Hooker, keeps the loop and replaces the duals with the subproblem's reasoning: if a year's jobs cannot be scheduled, find which jobs cause it, and tell the master never to put those jobs in that year together again. This lesson builds that loop with a MILP master and a CP-SAT subproblem, measures how much a smaller conflict and a relaxation in the master save, and checks the answer against one CP-SAT model of the whole problem.

The outage layer (job lengths, seasons, one crew) is invented for this lesson; GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio.

The Model

Sets. The plan model's sets (lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization), and $J \subseteq P$, the projects with an outage job: every transmission and distribution project.

Parameters. The plan model's parameters, and for each $j \in J$: $p_j$, the job's length in weeks (3 plus the project's crew-months); $[r_j, e_j)$, the weeks it may occupy: spring $[10, 20)$, autumn $[34, 44)$ or the whole year $[0, 52)$, depending on the project (a job longer than its season may run at any time).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ as before. For every $j \in J$ and $t \in T_j$, an integer start week $S_{j,t} \in [r_j,\, e_j - p_j]$, used only if $x_{j,t} = 1$.

Objective. As in lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization: $\max \sum_{i,t} \text{npv}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$.

Constraints. The once, must, alt, after and budget rows of lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization, and:

The outage rule is not a linear row: it is CP-SAT's add_no_overlap over optional intervals. A MILP can state it too (with a variable per job and week, or with big-M ordering rows), but every such form is large or weak. That is the situation LBBD is for: a master that a MILP solver handles well, and a subproblem that another kind of solver handles well.

A Subproblem Solved by Any Method Can Still Return a Cut

Fix the plan $\hat x$. What is left is one independent question per year: can this year's chosen jobs be scheduled? outages.schedulable asks CP-SAT, with each job an interval that must lie in its season and all of them under one add_no_overlap (the pattern of the OR-Tools job shop example):

The answer is yes or no, with no prices. Hooker's observation is that a Benders cut never needed prices as such: it needs a proof that the subproblem fails (or costs at least so much), stated so that it also says something about other plans. LP duals are one kind of proof, the kind classical Benders uses (lesson Benders decomposition). For a scheduling check the simplest proof is the set of jobs itself: "these jobs cannot all be done in one year". It holds for every plan that puts them in the same year, whatever else the plan does. So the cut for a failed year $y$ with chosen jobs $S$ is a no-good cut:

"at least one of these projects starts in another year, or not at all". The master is the plan model plus every no-good found so far. The loop needs no lower bound of its own here: the subproblem only says yes or no, so the first master plan whose every year schedules is optimal (the master is a relaxation of the whole problem, and this plan is feasible for it).

A Smaller Conflict Makes a Stronger Cut

A no-good on all of a year's six jobs forbids only plans that contain all six. Usually two or three of them are the real clash. Find those and the cut forbids every plan containing them, however the other jobs are arranged. outages.minimal_conflict is a deletion filter, the same idea as lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility's: drop each job in turn, and keep it dropped if the rest still cannot be scheduled. What remains still fails, and dropping any one more job makes it schedulable. Such a set is a minimal (irreducible) conflict: no job can be taken out of it. That is not the same as the smallest conflict, the one with the fewest jobs: the filter keeps whichever minimal set its order of deletions leads to, and a year can hold another, smaller one. Finding the smallest would mean searching over subsets; a minimal one costs one check per job.

outage_jobs.py shows one such conflict. Three long autumn jobs cannot share an autumn, and the filter shrinks them to two:

Autumn has 10 weeks, and any two of these jobs need 15 or more. The loop with the filter, and with a 52-week limit per year in the master (next section), prints the core of every conflict it finds:

Every conflict on this data is a pair; the master's value falls from 24,260 to 22,817 in 11 master solves, and the 11th plan schedules in every year, so it is optimal. The cost of the outage rule is $1,443k of NPV. Each core costs one extra CP-SAT solve per job of the year, on a model of a few jobs. The table in the next section shows what the filter buys: 84 master solves without it, 11 with it.

    Shrink a refused crane booking to its core

    • a deletion filter shrinks a failed set to a core that still fails and from which nothing can be dropped
    • a failed check yields a cut naming the jobs that cannot share a year

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A transmission utility books one mobile crane for its substation lifts. A scheduling check, fits(lifts), answers whether a set of lifts can all be booked in one month (it is given, and its inside does not matter here). When a month's set is refused, the planning master needs a no-good cut: "not all of these lifts in this month". The fewer lifts the cut names, the more plans it rules out, so the planners want the refused set shrunk to a core: a subset that fits still refuses, from which no single lift can be dropped without fits accepting the rest.

Write first what the cut on a core $S$ says in the master's variables $x_{l,m}$ (lift $l$ in month $m$), and why it is valid for every plan. Then write shrink(lifts, fits) to return a core of the refused list lifts, as a list in the input's order. Try each lift once, in order.

A Relaxation of the Subproblem in the Master Saves Iterations


The master starts out knowing nothing about outages, so its first plans crowd them. Hooker's second tool is to put a relaxation of the subproblem into the master: linear rows in $x$ that every schedulable year satisfies. They never cut off a good plan, and they refuse many bad ones before CP-SAT is ever asked. Two such relaxations, from weakest to strongest:


  • Weeks per year: a year's jobs cannot need more than 52 weeks, $\sum_{j \in J} p_j\, x_{j,y} \le 52$ (weeks_y).
  • Weeks per season: the jobs that must happen inside a season cannot need more weeks than the season has, $\sum_{j:\ [r_j, e_j) \subseteq [a, b)} p_j\, x_{j,y} \le b - a$ for spring and autumn (weeks_y_spring, weeks_y_autumn). A job allowed the whole year is not counted in a season's row, since it may run outside the season.




Hooker's paper writes the general form as an energy bound for every interval between a release and a deadline; the season rows are that bound for the two intervals this data has. All five variants reach the same optimum:



Read it row by row. Plain no-goods need 84 master solves and 105 cuts: each one forbids only the exact set of jobs it names, so the master keeps finding new sets with the same clash. The minimal conflict brings that to 11 and 15. The 52-week row changes nothing: no year's chosen jobs come near 52 weeks; the clashes are inside seasons. The season rows remove the loop altogether: the first master plan already schedules. On this data that is because every conflict is a pair of jobs that overflow their season, which is exactly what the season row states. With jobs allowed at any time filling the gaps between seasons, conflicts would exist that no season row sees, and the CP-SAT check would still be needed; it always runs, and here it confirms the plan in one pass.


The lesson for practice: the cut decides how much one failure teaches the master, and the relaxation decides how many failures happen at all. On this data the relaxation did more.

Problem statement


The crane of the previous practice is booked by a planning master that chooses which lifts to do this month, $x_l \in \{0,1\}$; a scheduling check then tests each chosen month. Every refused month costs one more master solve. The planners want the master to refuse, by itself, the months that are obviously too full, with linear rows in $x$ that never refuse a month the crane could really do.


Lift $l$ needs $p_l$ crane days, may begin on day $r_l$ and must end by day $d_l$ (one crane, one lift at a time). Days are counted as in the lesson's half-open windows $[r, e)$: the lift may use the days $r_l, r_l + 1, \dots, d_l - 1$, so its window holds $d_l - r_l$ crane days, and a stretch $[a, b)$ holds $b - a$. Write the rows first: which stretches $[a, b)$ get a row, which lifts each row counts, and the row itself with a label and quantifier. A stretch runs from some lift's release $a$ to some lift's deadline $b > a$, and it gets a row only when at least one lift's whole window lies inside it; a stretch with no such lift gets none. Then write add_energy_rows(model, x, lifts) to add them and return them in a dict keyed by (a, b). lifts[name] is (days, release, deadline); x[name] is the master's variable.


The tests fix a month's lifts and ask the solver whether the master allows it, so any correct formulation passes. About six lines.

One CP-SAT Model of the Whole Problem Checks the Answer


On GridCap small the whole problem fits in one CP-SAT model: the start variables as Booleans, the plan rows, and for every year an optional interval per job, present only if the project starts that year (new_optional_fixed_size_interval_var, with x[j, y] as the presence literal), under one add_no_overlap:






The two agree, and they have to: an instance has one optimal value, however many plans reach it. If the one model ever found a schedulable plan worth more than the loop's answer, the loop would be the one in the wrong, because its master is a relaxation that only a wrong cut or row can make refuse a schedulable plan. On an instance this small the one model is the simpler tool. LBBD earns its place when the whole model is too big for one solver: many years, many crews, a long horizon in weeks, where the master stays a MILP of the size of lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization's plan and each year's schedule is a separate small CP-SAT model, as in Hooker's planning and scheduling problems, and in the time-slot problem of the arXiv paper in Further reading, whose master chooses delivery slots and whose subproblems are vehicle routing problems.


The Model


The LBBD master after $k$ iterations, whole. New are the relaxation rows and the no-good cuts; the scheduling lives only in the yearly CP-SAT subproblems.


Sets. The plan model's sets; $J$ the projects with outage jobs; $\Sigma$ the seasons spring $[10, 20)$ and autumn $[34, 44)$, and $J_\sigma \subseteq J$ the jobs whose window lies inside season $\sigma$; $\mathcal{N}_y$ the conflicts found so far in year $y$, each a set of jobs.


Parameters. The plan model's parameters; $p_j$ (weeks), and each season's length $|\sigma|$ (10 weeks).


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for every $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$.


Objective. $\max \sum_{i \in P} \sum_{t \in T_i} \text{npv}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$.


Constraints. The once, must, alt, after and budget rows of lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization, and:



The subproblem of year $y$ at plan $\hat x$: is there a start week $S_j \in [r_j, e_j - p_j]$ for every job with $\hat x_{j,y} = 1$ such that no two of the intervals $[S_j, S_j + p_j)$ overlap? If not, its minimal conflict $S$ joins $\mathcal{N}_y$.


Summary


  • A subproblem solved by any method can still return a cut. The cut is a proof of failure stated for every plan; for a scheduling check, the no-good $\sum_{j \in S} x_{j,y} \le |S| - 1$. The first master plan that passes every check is optimal.
  • A smaller conflict makes a stronger cut. A deletion filter shrank every conflict to a pair: 11 master solves instead of 84.
  • A relaxation of the subproblem in the master saves iterations. The 52-week row did nothing here; the season rows, which state the real clash, left nothing for the loop to do.
  • One CP-SAT model of the whole problem checks the answer. Both give 22,817 on GridCap small.

Further reading: The Job Shop Problem (OR-Tools) (interval variables built from a start and a fixed duration, and add_no_overlap for one machine, the pattern of each yearly check); MathOpt (OR-Tools) (the master's MILP); Hooker, Logic-based Benders decomposition for large-scale optimization (arXiv 1910.11944) (section 2, cuts from the subproblem's inference dual; section 5, the job assignment and scheduling example with a no-good per facility and its strengthening by removing jobs one at a time; section 6, energy relaxations of a scheduling subproblem added to the master); Abdolhamidi, Juvin and Lurkin, Logic-Based Benders Decomposition for Time Slot Management with Mixed Logit Demand (arXiv 2407.02339) (an LBBD whose master picks delivery slots and prices and whose subproblems are vehicle routing MILPs, with problem-specific cuts stronger than no-goods, and a finding that the value of each strengthening depends on how strong the master already is); The CP-SAT Primer (Krupke) (its advanced modeling chapter on intervals and no-overlap).


Challenges


  1. Give the outage crew a second team, so two jobs may run at once. Which CP-SAT constraint replaces add_no_overlap, and how do the season rows change?
  2. A no-good on a pair $\{a, b\}$ in year $y$ says nothing about $a$ in $y$ and $b$ in $y+1$. Is that right for this outage rule? When would it be wrong?
  3. Allow every job to run at any time of year. Predict the table's last two rows.
  4. The deletion filter's result depends on the order it tries the jobs. Could two orders give cores of different sizes? What does that mean for the cut?


Design Note: Keep the Subproblem a Pure Check


schedulable takes a list of jobs and answers yes or no. It knows nothing of the master, the plan, the years or the cuts. That is what lets the deletion filter call it on any subset, the tests call it on hand-made cases, and a different scheduler replace CP-SAT without touching the loop. A subproblem that reaches into the master's state is much harder to check on its own, and a wrong cut in a Benders loop removes good plans silently.
