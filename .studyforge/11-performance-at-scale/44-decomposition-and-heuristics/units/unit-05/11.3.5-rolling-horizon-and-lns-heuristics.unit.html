GridCap's medium portfolio, 400 projects over ten years with lesson Workers, search strategies and LNS's crew limits, is the model the planners rerun in every scenario meeting. HiGHS proves its optimum, but only after a long search: 21,794 branch-and-bound nodes in this lesson's run. In a meeting the planners need a good plan from a fraction of that work, and an honest statement of how good it is. This lesson builds the two heuristics most used for multi-period plans: a rolling horizon, which plans a few years at a time, and large neighborhood search (LNS) around the MIP solver, which re-plans one part of a plan at a time. Both are measured the same way, against the best bound known, and then against the proven optimum, and one of them loses to the plain solver. That result is part of the lesson.

Lesson Workers, search strategies and LNS showed CP-SAT's own LNS and a region-based LNS on the same portfolio with CP-SAT. This lesson runs LNS around a MIP solver (HiGHS through MathOpt), adds neighborhoods by years, and compares them. GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio.

Every Heuristic Plan Is Reported Against the Best Bound Known

A heuristic returns a plan, never a proof. Its quality is the gap between its value and the best upper bound known for the optimum (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap's gap, with the heuristic's plan as the incumbent). Bounds come from relaxations: the LP relaxation, a Lagrangian bound (lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization), or a MIP search stopped early, whose best bound is the tightest of the three here. rolling_lns.py first collects them, stopping the monolithic HiGHS search after a fixed number of nodes. A node limit, not a time limit, so every run of the program prints the same numbers:

The best bound known is 219,722: no plan is worth more. The solver itself already has a plan within 0.15% of it after 200 nodes. Any heuristic below has to be judged against both numbers: the bound, which says how far a plan could be from the best, and the solver's own plan for comparable work, which says whether the heuristic was worth writing.

A Rolling Horizon Plans a Few Years at a Time and Keeps Only the First Ones

A rolling horizon solves the plan in windows of $W$ years. In each window it decides the starts in the window's years, keeps the first $S$ years of those decisions, and moves the window forward $S$ years. Chapter 9 of LINDO's text describes multi-period models used this way, in a rolling format: solve, implement the first period, slide forward.

For GridCap, window $[a, b]$ means, for each start variable:

  • decided in an earlier window: fixed to that decision;
  • a start after $b$: held at 0 for now (a later window decides it);
  • a start in $[a, b]$: free.

A must-do project whose latest start lies beyond $b$ may wait for a later window, so its must_ row is relaxed to $\le 1$ until the window that contains its last chance. Budget and crew rows stay in for every year, so money and crews a project will use after the window are counted when it starts:

Every window is solved to optimality, so the program's nodes count all the search it did:

Every plan breaks no rule (capital.violations checks each one in plain Python). Longer windows give better plans: 3.92% from the bound with 3-year windows, 0.82% with 5. The loss has a name, the end effect (LINDO's section 9.8): a window that ends in year $b$ sees nothing beyond it, so it spends money and crews on projects that start inside it, even when the same money would earn more on a project whose best start comes later. Keeping only the first years of each window, and re-deciding the rest, softens it; a longer window softens it more, at the price of a larger MIP.

And here is the honest part. The window-4 plan, after 5,909 nodes of search spread over four windows, is worth 216,672; the monolithic solver had 219,411 after 200 nodes. On this model a rolling horizon is the wrong tool for speed. Each window is not much smaller than the whole model (every project that may start in the window is in it, with budget and crew rows for all ten years), and GridCap's LP bound is tight (220,238.3, 0.28% above the optimum found below), so the monolithic search finds good plans early. A rolling horizon earns its place when the whole horizon cannot be built or solved at all, for example a twenty-year horizon of monthly periods, and as a source of a first plan for something else.

Problem statement

A water utility rolls its pipe-renewal plan forward year by year. Its planners use a variant of the lesson's rolling horizon called relax-and-fix: in each window, the starts in the window's years are yes/no, and the starts in every later year are kept in the model as continuous variables between 0 and 1, instead of being held at 0. The later years are then a cheap look ahead: the window sees roughly what its choices leave for later. As before, the first keep years of each window are kept, starts decided in earlier windows are fixed, and the last window keeps everything.

The years are the calendar years of budgets, in order, and every start year a project offers is one of them. A window is window consecutive years of that list, and its decisions are the starts in those years: a start is yes/no when its year is in the window or earlier, and relaxed when it is later. The first window begins at the first year, and each next one begins keep years later. The last window is the first one that reaches the final year of budgets (or would run past it, in which case it is cut short at that year); it keeps every start it has not yet fixed, and the plan ends there. With 2027 to 2029, window=2 and keep=1, the first window 2027-2028 keeps 2027, and the second, 2028-2029, is the last and keeps 2028 and 2029.

build(projects, budgets) gives the whole plan model (at most one start per project, a budget row per year counting each project's spend in every year of work). Write first which variables a window fixes, which it makes integer and which continuous, and what it keeps. Then write relax_and_fix(projects, budgets, window, keep) and return the plan, a dict from project name to start year. projects[n] holds "value" (start year to value) and "cost" (spend by year of work). A MathOpt variable becomes continuous with var.integer = False.

  • by region: all projects of one region (about 100), regions in turn, as lesson Workers, search strategies and LNS did;
  • by years: every project that starts, or could start, in a run of three years (about 140), moving two years each step.




Regions found $37k in eight steps; years found $1,819k. The reason is in the model. GridCap's budget and crew rows are by year and program, not by region. Freeing one region while the other three keep their plans leaves that region only the money and crews the others left over, which the window plan had already used up: there is almost nothing to rearrange. Freeing three years frees every project competing for those years' budgets and crews at once, including the end-effect mistakes the rolling horizon made, so moving one project's start can make room for another's. This is the CP-SAT Primer's point about LNS, seen on GridCap: a neighborhood should hold the variables that interact.

LNS Polishes the Solver's Own Early Plan Better Than a Rolling Horizon's


The rolling-horizon start was the weak link. The third run starts LNS from the monolithic solver's plan after 200 nodes:



200 nodes of monolithic search plus 128 nodes of LNS give 219,560, more than the monolithic search reached in 8,000 nodes (219,482), and 0.07% from the best bound. Node counts are only a rough measure of work here. An LNS node belongs to a smaller MIP, one with most variables fixed, and is cheaper than a node of the whole model. If anything, then, the count overstates the work LNS did.


Then the proof. medium_proof.py runs the monolithic MILP to the end:



With the optimum known, every number above can be checked. The LNS plan is $59k (0.027%) below the optimum; its reported gap of 0.07% was honest and, as gaps against a bound are, pessimistic, because the bound 219,722 was itself $103k above the optimum. The window-4 rolling-horizon plan is 1.34% below. A planner who had stopped at "the heuristic's plan" without a bound would have had no way to tell these two apart.


The Model


One LNS step, whole: the plan model with the neighborhood rows added.


Sets, parameters, variables, objective. As in the model above, plus $F \subseteq P$, the projects the step frees, and $\bar x$, the current plan ($\bar x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$, a parameter).


Constraints. The once, must, alt, after, budget and crew rows, and



written in code as bounds: both bounds of $x_{i,t}$ set to $\bar x_{i,t}$.


One rolling-horizon window $[a, b]$, whole: the same model with, for every $i, t$, $x_{i,t} = \hat x_{i,t}$ if $t < a$ (decided by earlier windows), $x_{i,t} = 0$ if $t > b$, and $\text{must}_i$ relaxed to $\sum_t x_{i,t} \le 1$ for must-do projects with latest start after $b$. It keeps the decisions for years $a, \dots, a + S - 1$ (all remaining years in the last window).


Summary


  • Every heuristic plan is reported against the best bound known. On GridCap medium with crews: bound 219,722 from 8,000 nodes, LP 220,238.3.
  • A rolling horizon plans a few years at a time and keeps only the first ones. Longer windows gave better plans (3.92% to 0.82% from the bound), but here the monolithic solver beat all of them with 200 nodes: the windows were nearly as big as the model and its LP bound is tight.
  • LNS frees one neighborhood of a plan, keeps the rest, and re-solves. It cannot make a plan worse.
  • A neighborhood must free projects that compete for the same money and crews. Region neighborhoods gained $37k, three-year neighborhoods $1,819k, because GridCap's limits are by year.
  • LNS polishes the solver's own early plan better than a rolling horizon's. 200 + 128 nodes gave 219,560, 0.027% from the proven optimum 219,619, which took 21,794 nodes.

Design Note: A Heuristic Ships With Its Bound


Every function in this lesson returns a plan, and every line of output pairs it with a gap. A heuristic plan alone invites the question "how good is it?", and the only honest answers are a bound or "we do not know". Put the bound in the same report, from the same run, and say where it came from; when the optimum becomes known later, the report can be checked against it, as this lesson's last output did.
