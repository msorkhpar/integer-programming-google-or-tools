GridCap's crews cannot do every project the capital plan of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years funds. Each program has its own crews, paid for already; beyond them, the work is done on overtime (up to a quarter of the program's crews, at $15k a crew-month) or by contractors from one pool of 3 crew-months a year, at $60k, $40k or $25k a crew-month for transmission, distribution and vegetation work. Which projects to start is a yes/no question. Who does each year's work, once the projects are chosen, is a small continuous one: a linear program per year. Benders decomposition keeps the two apart. A master problem chooses the projects and carries one variable per year standing in for that year's crew cost; for each plan the master proposes, the yearly LPs report the true cost, and their dual values become rows, Benders cuts, that teach the master what crew work costs, for every plan at once. This lesson builds the loop, checks every cut by hand on the first plan, and checks the loop's answer against the whole model solved in one MILP.

The crew-cost layer (overtime share, prices, contractor pool) is invented for this lesson, like the budget pots of lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps; GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio.

The Model

The whole model, the one Benders will split. It is lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization's plan model plus the crew layer.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i$ the start years of project $i$; $M$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$; $G$ the programs (transmission, distribution, vegetation), and $P_g$ the projects of program $g$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, $c_{i,k}$ and $B_y$ as in lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization ($k); $m_i$, the crew-months project $i$ needs in each year it is worked on; $d_i$, its years of work; $K_{g,y}$, program $g$'s own crew-months in year $y$; $\rho = 0.25$, the overtime share; $q^o = 15$, the price of an overtime crew-month ($k); $q^c_g$, the price of a contractor crew-month for program $g$ (60, 40, 25 $k); $U = 3$, the contractor crew-months available per year.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. For every $g \in G$, $y \in Y$, continuous: $w_{g,y} \ge 0$ own crew-months used, $o_{g,y} \ge 0$ overtime crew-months, $h_{g,y} \ge 0$ contractor crew-months (in code own, over, hire).

Fixing the Integer Plan Leaves One Small LP per Year

Fix a plan $\hat x$. Every $\text{need}_{g,y}(\hat x)$ becomes a number, and what is left falls apart by year: nothing links 2027's crew variables to 2028's. Each year is a three-program LP, crew_costs.year_lp:

Call its optimal value $Q_y(\hat x)$, the subproblem value: the cheapest way to staff year $y$'s work under plan $\hat x$. The whole model is then

with $Q_y(x) = +\infty$ when no staffing covers the work. This is the move Benders makes: the continuous variables disappear into functions of the integer ones. The functions are not known as formulas, but each one can be evaluated at any plan by one LP. The five yearly LPs are independent, so they could run in parallel; on a larger model with one LP per region and year, that matters.

The Year's Duals Price Its Right-Hand Sides, and the Same Prices Bound Every Other Plan

Solve the year's LP for $\hat x$, and read the dual value $\pi_r$ of every row $r$ (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver: the change in the optimal cost per unit increase of the row's bound). Check 2027 by hand. Vegetation needs 3 crew-months and has 2 of its own. Overtime may add a quarter of 2, that is 0.5, at $15k: $7.5k. The last 0.5 comes from contractors at $25k: $12.5k. Total $20k, as printed. The prices say the same thing in marginal terms: one more crew-month of vegetation work would be bought from a contractor ($\pi_{\text{cover}} = 25$); one more own crew-month would save a contractor's $25k ($\pi_{\text{own}} = -25$); one more overtime month would save the $25k contractor month but cost $15k, a net $10k ($\pi_{\text{over}} = -10$). The cut evaluates to $25 \cdot 3 - 25 \cdot 2 - 10 \cdot 0.5 = 20$ at this plan, and for any plan it is $\theta_{2027} \ge 25\,\text{need}_{\text{veg},2027}(x) - 55$: the master now knows that 2027's crew cost is at least $25k for every vegetation crew-month of need beyond 2.2. At need 2 the cut asks only $\theta \ge -5$; the bound $\theta \ge 0$ does the rest. A cut is exact where it was made and a bound everywhere else.

    A cut from a pumping LP

    • with the integer plan fixed, the continuous decisions form an LP whose right-hand sides depend on the plan
    • the subproblem's duals times its right-hand sides, written in the master's variables, give a cut exact at the plan and valid for all others

Problem statement

A water district chooses which booster stations to build; that is the master's decision, $x_j \in \{0,1\}$. Once the stations are fixed, it pumps the cheapest way it can: station $j$ delivers up to its capacity $u_j$ at an energy cost $e_j$ per megalitre, and water bought from the neighboring district costs $p$ per megalitre, at most $L$ megalitres. The district must deliver its demand $D$, and every station built also needs $r_j$ megalitres to fill its own tank. Pumping and buying are continuous: this is the subproblem.

Write the subproblem for a fixed plan $\hat x$ first: its variables with their bounds, its objective, and each row with a label, with every row's right-hand side written as a function of $\hat x$. Then write the optimality cut it gives: the expression in the master's $x$ that equals the pumping cost at $\hat x$ and is at most the pumping cost at every other plan.

Then write pumping_cut(stations, demand, limit, price, plan, x) to build and solve the subproblem with GLOP for plan (a dict from station name to 0 or 1) and return (cost, cut): the optimal pumping cost and the cut, as a MathOpt linear expression in the master variables x (a dict from station name to variable). stations[j] holds "capacity", "energy" and "refill". The tests only call plans for which the subproblem is feasible.

An Infeasible Year Returns a Feasibility Cut From a Shortfall LP


The same first plan in 2031:



Vegetation needs 11 crew-months against 2 of its own, 0.5 of overtime and a pool of 3 shared with distribution, which is itself 1 month short. No staffing covers it: $Q_{2031}(\hat x) = +\infty$, and no optimality cut can say so. The master needs a row that forbids this plan, and every plan that fails for the same reason: a feasibility cut.


The textbook route reads a dual ray of the infeasible LP (a Farkas certificate: prices that prove no solution exists). MathOpt's GLOP and HiGHS do not return one here, on a deliberately infeasible one-year LP:



PDLP, a first-order method, returns one, but its numbers are those of an approximate method, and lesson Infeasible, unbounded, or the solver can't tell met the same gap for primal rays. The course uses a route that needs no ray and works with any LP solver: the shortfall LP. Add a variable $s_g \ge 0$ to each cover row, $w_g + o_g + h_g + s_g \ge \text{need}_g$, and minimize $\sum_g s_g$ instead of cost. It is always feasible (put all the need in $s$), and its optimum is zero exactly when the year can be covered. When it is positive, its duals $\sigma$ make the same argument as before: for every plan, the least shortfall is at least $\sum_r \sigma_r\, \text{bound}_r(x)$, so a plan with that sum above zero cannot be covered. The feasibility cut is



Here it reads $\text{need}_{\text{dist},2031}(x) + \text{need}_{\text{veg},2031}(x) \le 10.5$: distribution and vegetation together cannot need more than their own crews (4 + 2), their overtime (1 + 0.5) and the pool (3). A planner would have written that rule by hand, had they known it was the one that binds. Benders found it from one infeasible plan.




The Master Is an Upper Bound, Each Plan's True Value a Lower Bound; Stop When They Meet


The master is the plan model with a variable $\theta_y \ge 0$ per year and the objective $\max \sum \text{npv}\,x - \sum_y \theta_y$, plus every cut found so far. It starts with no cuts, so it believes crews are free.


  • The master's optimum is an upper bound on the whole model: every cut is valid for every plan, so the master is a relaxation, and adding cuts can only lower it.
  • Each plan the master proposes has a true value, $\sum \text{npv}\,\hat x - \sum_y Q_y(\hat x)$, when every year can be covered. That is a feasible plan's value, a lower bound.
  • When the master's $\theta_y$ already equal the $Q_y(\hat x)$, no cut is added: the master's value is the true value of a feasible plan, the two bounds meet, and the plan is optimal.


The loop re-solves the master from scratch each time (lesson Incremental solving: edit the model, re-solve's incremental edit, then a new solve); the next section says why not inside one search.








The upper bound falls in every iteration, 24,260 to 23,812.25, as cuts accumulate. The first two plans cannot be staffed in some year, so there is no lower bound yet; the third plan can be, and its true value, 23,812.25, turns out to be the optimum, which the master proves three iterations later. Six master solves and 12 cuts in all reach the same value and the same plan as the monolithic MILP. The optimal plan gives up $194k of NPV (24,260 to 24,066) to cut the crew bill to $253.75k.


Two habits from this output carry over to any decomposition. Compare against the monolithic solve on an instance small enough to solve whole, and compare with a tolerance: HiGHS returns objective values with noise near $10^{-5}$, so a test of equality must allow for it (this module's tests use $10^{-3}$). And report the bounds per iteration, not only the final plan: a loop stopped early still gives an honest gap between its two bounds.

HiGHS Cannot Take the Cuts Inside Its Search; SCIP Can, as Lazy Constraints


Each master solve above starts the branch-and-bound search from scratch, and throws away a tree that already proved most of the answer. The alternative, often called branch-and-Benders-cut, solves the master once and adds each cut the moment an integer plan breaks it, as a lazy constraint (lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem). Lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem showed that HiGHS through MathOpt accepts a lazy-constraint callback, never calls it, and returns plans that break the lazy rows. So the one-search version runs on SCIP, with the same cut function:








One search reaches the same optimum. It sent 37 cuts, against the loop's 12, because SCIP calls the callback at every integer plan it meets, including plans the loop's master never proposed. Which version is faster depends on how costly a master solve is against a callback; the one search never throws a tree away, the loop never sends a cut for a plan that could not win. When this program runs, SCIP also writes two [scip_event.c:305] ERROR lines to the error stream; lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem met them too, and the solve is unaffected.


So when the same callback is registered with HiGHS and the solve reports OPTIMAL above the loop's value, neither the cuts nor the loop is at fault. The cuts are not weaker in one search: sent by the same function to SCIP, they reach 23,812.25 above. The loop did not stop early: its two bounds met at 23,812.25, the monolithic MILP's value. HiGHS never called the callback, so it solved the master with no cuts, the master that believes crews are free, as in iteration 1 of the loop.


The Model


The Benders master after $k$ iterations, whole. New are the $\theta_y$ and the two kinds of cut; the crew variables and rows live only in the yearly subproblems.


Sets, parameters. As in the whole model above, plus $\mathcal{O}_y$, the optimality cuts found so far for year $y$, each with duals $\pi^{(j)}$, and $\mathcal{F}_y$, the feasibility cuts, each with shortfall duals $\sigma^{(j)}$.


Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ as before; $\theta_y \ge 0$ for every $y \in Y$, the master's estimate of year $y$'s crew cost ($k), continuous.


Objective.



Constraints. The once, must, alt, after and budget rows of lesson Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient optimization, and, writing $\beta_y(\pi, x) = \sum_{g} (\pi_{\text{cover}_g} \text{need}_{g,y}(x) + \pi_{\text{own}_g} K_{g,y} + \pi_{\text{over}_g} \rho K_{g,y}) + \pi_{\text{pool}} U$:



The subproblem of year $y$ at plan $\hat x$ is the LP $Q_y(\hat x) = \min \sum_g (q^o o_g + q^c_g h_g)$ over $w, o, h \ge 0$ subject to cover, own, over and pool with $\text{need}_{g,y}(\hat x)$ on the right; its shortfall version adds $s_g \ge 0$ to each cover row and minimizes $\sum_g s_g$.


Summary


  • Fixing the integer plan leaves one small LP per year. The continuous variables become a function $Q_y(x)$ of the plan, evaluated by an LP.
  • The year's duals price its right-hand sides, and the same prices bound every other plan. $\theta_y \ge \sum_r \pi_r\, \text{bound}_r(x)$ is exact at the plan that produced it and valid for every plan.
  • An infeasible year returns a feasibility cut from a shortfall LP. GLOP and HiGHS return no dual ray here; the shortfall LP's duals give the cut with any LP solver.
  • The master is an upper bound, each plan's true value a lower bound; stop when they meet. On GridCap small: 6 master solves, 12 cuts, the monolithic optimum 23,812.25 and the same plan.
  • HiGHS cannot take the cuts inside its search; SCIP can, as lazy constraints. One SCIP search sent 37 cuts and reached the same optimum.

Design Note: Name Every Cut


Every cut the loop adds has a name, opt_2029_1 or feas_2031_1: the year it bounds and the iteration that found it. When a master turns infeasible, or a plan is refused for a reason a planner cannot see, the names lead back to the plan and the year that produced the rule. Unnamed cuts make a decomposition a black box at exactly the moment someone needs to look inside.
