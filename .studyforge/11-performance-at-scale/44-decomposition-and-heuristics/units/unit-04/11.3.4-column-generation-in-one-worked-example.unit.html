GridCap's vegetation program has its season's tasks counted: 48 spans to trim, 30 hazard trees to remove, 22 corridors to clear, 14 rights-of-way to widen and 17 sites of storm debris. Each kind of task takes a known number of minutes, and one crew-day has 600 minutes of work in it. The vegetation manager asks: how few crew-days cover the season? A crew-day is a pattern: so many tasks of each kind that fit in 600 minutes. There are hundreds of possible patterns, and on a real season with dozens of task kinds there are millions. Column generation solves the problem without listing them: it starts with a handful, and asks a small optimization problem, the pricing problem, for the one pattern that would improve the plan most, until no pattern would. This lesson walks one example end to end: the model, the first iteration by hand, the loop, the integer answer, and a check against a compact model solved directly.

The program is adapted from the OR-Tools MathOpt example cutting_stock.py, which cuts boards into pieces; here a crew-day is "cut" into tasks. The task data is invented for the lesson; GridCap is a fictional utility.

The Model

Sets. $K$ the kinds of task (trim span, hazard tree, clear corridor, widen right-of-way, storm debris); $\Pi$ every feasible pattern, a pattern $p$ being a vector of whole numbers $a_{k,p}$, the tasks of kind $k$ done in one crew-day with pattern $p$.

Parameters. $w_k$, the minutes one task of kind $k$ takes (55, 110, 250, 340, 190); $d_k$, the tasks of kind $k$ to do this season (48, 30, 22, 14, 17); $W = 600$, the minutes in a crew-day. A pattern is feasible when $\sum_k w_k\, a_{k,p} \le W$ and $0 \le a_{k,p} \le d_k$.

Variables. $\lambda_p \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ for every $p \in \Pi$: the number of crew-days worked with pattern $p$. In code, days_0, days_1, …

Objective.

Constraints.

Five rows and one column per pattern: a tall, thin matrix of the kind lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)'s simplex never sees all of at once. The rows are $\ge$, not $=$: a crew-day may finish a task kind early and do fewer of something than its pattern allows (the OR-Tools sample writes equalities; with $\ge$ the duals below are never negative, which keeps the pricing problem simple).

Read the dual value $\pi_k$ of each cover row (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver): the crew-days one more task of kind $k$ would cost the current plan. A new pattern $p$ costs one crew-day and does the work of $\sum_k \pi_k a_{k,p}$ crew-days at those prices, so its reduced cost (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver) is

    Price a cable reel

    • the pricing problem finds the pattern worth most at the master's duals

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

GridCap's undergrounding crews pull cable from standard reels of reel metres, cut into pieces of a few standard lengths; the season needs $d_k$ pieces of length $\ell_k$. A column generation master chooses how many reels to cut with each pattern, and after each master solve its cover rows' duals price one piece of each length. The pricing problem asks for the pattern, the pieces cut from one reel, worth the most at those prices.

Write the pricing problem first: its variables with domains and bounds, its objective, and its row with a label. Then write best_reel(prices, pieces, reel) to solve it without a solver, and return (value, pattern): the pattern's worth at the prices and the pattern itself, a dict from piece name to count (counts of zero left out). pieces[name] is (length, demand); lengths and reel are whole metres. On small reels the tests compare your value with every pattern tried by brute force. One test prices a 1,000-metre reel with eight lengths and up to 30 pieces of each: that is $31^8$ count combinations, too many to try one by one, so it stops your function after 10 seconds. Trying every pattern is not the method this practice asks for.

About twelve lines; dynamic programming over metres used is one way.

Stop When No Day Has a Negative Reduced Cost; the Pricing Value Bounds the LP on the Way


Each iteration re-solves the master LP with the new column. mathopt.IncrementalSolver, as in the OR-Tools sample, keeps GLOP's model between solves and receives only the added column (lesson Incremental solving: edit the model, re-solve covers incremental solving):






The lower line is a bound that holds before the loop ends. Every pattern does at most $v^*$ at prices $\pi$, so the scaled prices $\pi / v^*$ are dual feasible for the full master LP; by weak duality its optimum is at least $\sum_k (\pi_k / v^*) d_k$, and $\sum_k \pi_k d_k$ is the restricted master's optimum $z_{RMP}$ (strong duality, lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness). So



The same argument, one step at a time:


  1. What dual feasible means here. The full master LP's dual has one price $\rho_k \ge 0$ per task kind, and one row per pattern: $\sum_k a_{k,p}\, \rho_k \le 1$ for every $p \in \Pi$ (a pattern's work, valued at the prices, is worth at most the one crew-day it costs). Any such $\rho$ gives $z_{LP} \ge \sum_k \rho_k d_k$ (weak duality, lesson Weak and strong duality, complementary slackness).
  2. The restricted master's prices may break some of those rows. $\pi$ satisfies the rows of the patterns already in the master, but a missing pattern can be worth more than 1 at $\pi$. The pricing problem finds the worst one: no pattern is worth more than $v^*$.
  3. Dividing by $v^*$ repairs every row at once. With $\rho = \pi / v^*$, each pattern's worth is divided by $v^*$ too: $\sum_k a_{k,p}\, \pi_k / v^* \le v^* / v^* = 1$. The prices stay nonnegative, so $\rho$ is dual feasible.
  4. Its value is the restricted master's optimum, scaled. That is $\sum_k \rho_k d_k = \bigl(\sum_k \pi_k d_k\bigr) / v^* = z_{RMP} / v^*$, and step 1 makes that a lower bound on $z_{LP}$.


The code divides by $\max(v^*, 1)$: when no pattern is worth more than 1, $\pi$ is already dual feasible and needs no scaling, and the bound is $z_{RMP}$ itself.


The loop prints both sides, keeping the best lower value seen:



The master LP falls from 41.467 to 33.1 and the bound rises from 27.644 to 33.1; at iteration 6 the best day is worth exactly 1, no day has a negative reduced cost, and the two meet. Five columns were added to the five starting ones: 10 columns out of the hundreds that exist. Because a crew-day count is a whole number, the LP optimum 33.1 proves that no plan uses fewer than 34 crew-days. A loop stopped early would still give an honest interval, for example $[31.938, 33.5]$ after iteration 4.


Integer Answers Come From the Generated Columns, and the LP Bound Says How Good They Are


The LP answer uses fractional days. The simplest way to an integer plan is price-and-branch: make the generated columns integer and solve the restricted master once as a MIP (the sample does the same):






34 crew-days, and the LP said no plan uses fewer: this plan is optimal, proved by the bound, not by listing patterns. That proof is luck of the data, not a property of the method. The best integer plan may need a pattern the LP never priced, and then the integer master over the generated columns ends above $\lceil z_{LP} \rceil$ with no proof either way. Closing that gap exactly needs branch-and-price, which generates columns at every node of a branch-and-bound tree; MathOpt has no built-in support for it, and it is beyond this lesson. The honest report in that case is the interval $[\lceil z_{LP} \rceil,\ z_{IP}]$.


Some days in the plan do more than the season needs (the integer master may over-cover a $\ge$ row); a planner drops those tasks from the last days.

A Compact Model of the Same Question Checks the Answer and Shows Why Patterns Help


OR-Tools' bin packing page writes the same kind of question with a variable per item and bin and a yes/no per bin. compact.py does that here: a whole-number variable $n_{k,j}$ for the tasks of kind $k$ on day $j$, a yes/no $u_j$ per day, cover rows, a 600-minute row per day, and $u_j \le u_{j-1}$ so days are used in order. Any upper bound on the days will do for the number of days; it uses 36:






Both agree on 34. The bounds do not: the compact model's LP relaxation is 32.383, the total minutes divided by 600 (19,430 / 600), and proves only 33. Its LP may split a 340-minute widening across two days; the pattern LP cannot, because every column is a whole day that really fits. That is the general reason column generation is used: the pattern formulation has a stronger LP relaxation, the same strength gap lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms measured between aggregated and cumulative rows, at the price of exponentially many columns that the pricing problem lets you never write down. On this small season the compact MILP still solves. On a season with dozens of task kinds and hundreds of days, its weaker bound and its interchangeable days (any reordering of the days is the same plan, the symmetry lesson Symmetry and how to break it breaks with ordering rows) work against branch-and-bound, while the pattern model keeps one row per task kind; this lesson does not measure that larger case.


The Model


The restricted master and its pricing problem, whole, as column generation solves them.


Sets. $K$ the task kinds; $\Pi' \subseteq \Pi$ the patterns generated so far.


Parameters. $w_k$, $d_k$, $W$ as above; $a_{k,p}$ for $p \in \Pi'$; $\pi_k$, the cover rows' duals after the last master solve.


Variables. Master LP: $\lambda_p \ge 0$, continuous, for $p \in \Pi'$ (integer, $\lambda_p \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, in the final price-and-branch solve). Pricing: $a_k \in \{0, 1, \dots, d_k\}$ for every $k \in K$.


Restricted master.



Pricing problem.



Loop. Add the pricing answer as a new column while $v^* > 1$ (its reduced cost $1 - v^*$ is negative); then $z_{RMP} = z_{LP}$. At every iteration $z_{RMP} / v^* \le z_{LP} \le z_{RMP}$.


Summary


  • Each column is a pattern, and the master LP needs only a few of them. Start from simple patterns; the restricted master's optimum is an upper bound on the full LP.
  • The master's duals price each task, and the pricing problem finds the best new day. The reduced cost of a pattern is $1 - \sum_k \pi_k a_{k,p}$; the pricing knapsack finds the most negative one.
  • Stop when no day has a negative reduced cost; the pricing value bounds the LP on the way. $z_{RMP}/v^*$ is a lower bound at every iteration; here 6 master solves and 10 columns.
  • Integer answers come from the generated columns, and the LP bound says how good they are. 34 crew-days, proved optimal here by $\lceil 33.1 \rceil = 34$.
  • A compact model of the same question checks the answer and shows why patterns help. The compact LP proves only 33.

Design Note: The Pricing Problem Is the Only Place the Day's Rules Live


The master knows nothing about minutes: it only counts tasks covered and days used. Every rule about what one day can hold (the 600 minutes here, travel time between sites, a crew that cannot do widening work) belongs in the pricing problem. That separation is what makes column generation extendable: a new rule about a day changes one small model, and the master, its duals and its bound stay as they are.
