A GridCap engineer finds a forum thread about slow MIPs and pastes its advice into the planning service: "turn heuristics up, set the MIP focus to feasibility, presolve aggressive". Every MIP solver has many parameters. Which of them matter, what does each of the big four actually trade, and how would anyone know whether the pasted advice helped? Lessons Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand to Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS opened up presolve, cuts and heuristics one at a time and showed that switching one off changes the work, not the answer. This lesson treats them as knobs, adds the fourth one, the solver's focus, and measures every setting the way lesson Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean taught, on two measures at once. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

The Model

The regional timing model of lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan, unchanged, on the eight regional plans of its first cycle (plan_model.instance_set(): four regions of the medium portfolios drawn with seeds 2027 and 1). No parameter in this lesson changes which plans are allowed or which is optimal: every run below proves the same optimum for its plan.

Sets. $P$ the region's projects; $Y$ the ten plan years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years of project $i$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, the spend of project $i$ in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the region's budget in year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$.

Four Knobs, and Each Solver Names Them Differently

The four parameters this lesson covers are the ones every MIP solver has in some form, and the ones the solvers' own guidance points to first:

  • Presolve: how hard to simplify the model before the search (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first).
  • Cuts: how many cutting planes to add to tighten the LP bound (lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand).
  • Heuristics: how much effort goes to finding plans outside the tree search (lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS).
  • Focus: which side of the gap to favour. A MIP solver works on two numbers (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap): the incumbent, which only plans can raise, and the bound, which only proof can lower. Focus tilts the whole search toward one of them.

MathOpt gives the first three common names: SolveParameters(presolve=..., cuts=..., heuristics=...), each an Emphasis from OFF through LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH to VERY_HIGH. Its docstring says a solver's default usually maps to MEDIUM, a solver without the feature accepts only an unset value, and when a setting is given both by its common name and in the solver's own parameters, the solver's own wins. knobs.py tries each knob at OFF and HIGH on each MathOpt MIP solver:

"Taken" means only that the solve did not fail. For HiGHS the program then checks whether the common heuristics knob does anything, against HiGHS's own option for the same thing, mip_heuristic_effort (default 0.05, the share of effort spent on heuristics, per HiGHS's option list):

The common knob is accepted at every level and changes nothing; HiGHS's own option changes the run. The same was seen for OFF on the model of lessons Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand to Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS; here it holds at VERY_HIGH too. So the rule for every knob: set it in the solver's own parameters, and check that it changed something. The table names the four knobs in each solver's own terms:

Judge a Setting on Two Measures: a Good Plan Soon, and the Proof

A planner looking at a running solve sees two things: the best plan so far, and whether the solver has finished. A setting can move them in opposite directions, so each setting below is judged on both, with the measures of lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan:

  • to 1%: the work until a plan within 1% of the plan's optimum was found;
  • to proof: the work to prove optimality, with the gap tolerance at 0.

Each is compared with the default over the eight plans and seeds 1, 2 and 3, 24 paired observations, as a ratio of shifted geometric means (below 1 means less work) with the wins and losses:

The work to 1% needs the moment each plan was found, in a unit that repeats. SCIP and Gurobi report it to a callback, and HiGHS in its log (lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan). CP-SAT needs care, and cpsat_clock.py shows why: it reads two of CP-SAT's counters in a solution callback at every new plan.

Read inside the callback, CP-SAT's deterministic time stood still from the first plan to the last, at 0.0008, while the solve as a whole used 8.6984. The branch count did move, from 894 to 192,566. (With several workers the plans found, and when, change between runs, lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs; one worker keeps this output repeatable.) Lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs measured CP-SAT's deterministic time at the end of a solve, where it is right; to date each plan, use a counter you have seen advance. So the rule for any solver: before you trust a work counter to date a plan, print it at two plans and check that it moved.

Problem statement

A freight company's depot-loading model is a cost-minimizing MIP solved by HiGHS, and HiGHS does not call MathOpt callbacks (lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem shows it). To date each new plan in a unit that repeats, the team reads HiGHS's log instead. Its progress table starts after a header line containing LpIters; each row ends with these columns, in order: BestBound, BestSol, Gap, Cuts, InLp, Confl., LpIters, Time (a time such as 0.4s). A row may start with a one-letter source (J, L, T, ...) or with spaces. BestSol is inf until there is a plan. Blank lines and restart messages can appear inside the table, and the table ends at Solving report.

On a long run, HiGHS stops printing LpIters in full once the count passes about a million and prints it in thousands with a k suffix. Three rows from one such run (a 90-second, time-limited solve of a small market-split model, through MathOpt) show the change:

Read 1107k as 1,107,000 iterations: the count to the thousand, which is as exact as the log is. (The same run's final report gives the exact total, LP iterations 1873361, where the last row printed 1873k.)

Before you code, write down which lines are rows, which columns give a plan's work $w$ and objective $z$, and when a row is a new plan: in terms of $z$ and the best objective $z_{\text{best}}$ so far, for a minimization and for a maximization.

Write incumbents(log, minimize=True) in highs_log.py: it returns the list of (LP iterations, objective) pairs, as floats, one per new better plan, in log order. The tests pass small logs as lists of lines; no solver runs. About fifteen lines.

Heuristics Find the Plans: Switched Off, Good Plans Came 7 to 11 Times Later





The clearest result in the three tables: with heuristics off, SCIP needed 10.74 times the work to reach a plan within 1%, and lost on all 24 observations; Gurobi with Heuristics=0 needed 6.89 times, also 0 wins in 24. Without heuristics a plan comes only from a node whose LP happens to be integral (lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS), and the search reaches such nodes late. The proof got slower too (1.34 and 1.43), because the tree cannot be pruned against an incumbent it does not have yet.


The reverse does not hold. More heuristic effort bought nothing here: Gurobi's Heuristics=0.5 found plans within 1% exactly as soon as the default (0 wins, 0 losses: the same work in every observation) and made the proof 16% slower; HiGHS's effort of 0.3 changed the work in only 12 of 24 observations and the ratio not at all. The defaults already find good plans within a few hundred LP iterations on these models (lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan), so there is nothing left for extra heuristics to win.


Presolve and Cuts Trade Early Plans Against the Proof, Differently on Each Solver


Presolve shrinks the model before the search (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first). On SCIP, switching it off made the proof cheaper, 0.64 with 16 wins in 24, and the first good plan later, 1.38. On Gurobi, off made the proof 17% dearer; on HiGHS it changed little either way (0.97 and 1.06). One knob, three solvers, three answers: presolve that is slow relative to a small model can cost more than it saves, and each solver's presolve is different code.


Cuts tighten the LP bound (lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand). Off, SCIP's proof needed 1.65 times the work and lost 18 of 24. Gurobi's Cuts=0 left the proof unchanged on average (1.00, 12 and 12) and found good plans sooner (0.72), while aggressive cuts (Cuts=2) cost 1.73 times the work and lost 23 of 24: on models this small, extra cuts are LP rows to carry, not bound to gain. HiGHS takes no cut setting through MathOpt (the table above); its own options for cut rounds are a matter for its option list, not for this lesson.


HiGHS's mip_allow_restart is not one of the four knobs, but it is a reminder that the big four are not the only ones: with restarts off, HiGHS proved optimality with 15% less work (17 wins, 7 losses), and found good plans as soon as before. (Lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line read a restart in a HiGHS log.)


Focus Says Which Side of the Gap to Work On


Gurobi's MIPFocus documents its four values: 0 balances the two sides (the default); 1 favours finding good feasible plans; 2 favours proving the incumbent optimal, for models where good plans come easily; 3 favours moving the bound, for models where the bound stalls. SCIP has the same idea as emphasis settings: its documentation describes FEASIBILITY as detecting feasibility fast and OPTIMALITY as proving optimality fast, among others such as HARDLP and NUMERICS.



SCIP's FEASIBILITY emphasis did what it says: a plan within 1% with 0.39 of the work, 20 wins and 1 loss. It paid for that on the proof, 1.51, losing 17 of 24. That is the trade a focus makes, and here it shows cleanly: a focus moves work from one side of the gap to the other. It is the right setting for a service that stops at a work limit and reports the best plan (lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan's "insurance" case), and the wrong one for a service that must prove its answer.


The settings that favour proof did not prove faster. SCIP's OPTIMALITY was slower on both measures (1.41 and 1.14), and so were Gurobi's MIPFocus=2 (1.41, 1.66) and MIPFocus=3 (3.65, 4.29, losing all 24 on proof). Gurobi's own guidance tells you when each value is meant to help: MIPFocus=2 when good plans come easily and the proof is the hard part, 3 when the bound barely moves. On these models neither is the case: the default reaches a plan within 1% in a few thousandths of a work unit and proves it soon after. A focus setting is a cure for a symptom; read the log (lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line) and find the symptom first.

Problem statement


An airline's crew-pairing team tries one solver setting at a time on a fixed set of models and seeds, and records two measures per run, paired with the default's run on the same model and seed: the work until a good plan was found, and the work to prove optimality. They want one word per setting, decided by a written rule, not by whoever reads the table.


For one measure, with paired work $a_k$ (new setting) and $b_k$ (default), $k = 1..n$, let $r = \text{SGM}_s(a) / \text{SGM}_s(b)$ (the shifted geometric mean of lesson Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean, given in the starter as sgm), $W$ the number of $k$ with $a_k < b_k$ and $L$ the number with $a_k > b_k$. Before you code, write the rule as inequalities on $r$, $W$, $L$ and a margin $m$: a measure is better when the ratio is at least $m$ below 1 and the wins outnumber the losses, worse in the mirror case, and same otherwise. "At least" includes the edge: a ratio of exactly $1 - m$ can be better (the test is $r \le 1 - m$, not $<$), and a ratio of exactly $1 + m$ can be worse ($r \ge 1 + m$). Then write how two judgements combine: better and worse is a trade-off; better with no worse is adopt; worse with no better is reject; anything else is no effect.


Write judge(new, old, shift, margin) (returns "better", "worse" or "same") and verdict(plan_new, plan_old, proof_new, proof_old, shift, margin=0.05) (returns "adopt", "reject", "trade-off" or "no effect") in verdict.py. The tests pass lists of numbers; no solver runs. About fifteen lines.

Defaults Win Here, So Change One Knob at a Time and Keep the Default Unless Both Measures Agree


Seventeen settings were tried in the four tables. Two helped on one measure without hurting the other by more than noise: Gurobi's Cuts=0 (0.72 to a good plan, 1.00 on proof) and HiGHS without restarts (0.85 on proof, 1.04 to a good plan). Two traded one measure for the other: SCIP's FEASIBILITY emphasis and SCIP with presolve off. Two changed next to nothing (HiGHS's heuristic effort 0.3, and HiGHS with presolve off at 1.06 and 0.97). The other eleven were worse on at least one measure and better on neither, some by a factor of four. The forum thread's advice (heuristics up, focus on feasibility, presolve aggressive), tried here one knob at a time on Gurobi, helps nowhere: none of Heuristics=0.5, MIPFocus=1 and Presolve=2 beats the default on either measure.


Gurobi's parameter guidelines say the defaults generally work well, and also that MIP models often benefit from tuning (lesson Automated tuning, and how not to overfit it takes that up); the CP-SAT Primer advises leaving most parameters at their defaults and improving the model instead. The way to change one anyway:


  1. Name the measure first. "Faster" means a good plan sooner, or a proof sooner; a setting can do one and undo the other.
  2. Change one knob at a time, against the default, on a fixed instance set with several seeds. Two knobs changed together cannot tell you which one did the work.
  3. Adopt it only if both measures agree, or if the one it hurts is a measure you do not need. A ratio near 1 with wins and losses close to even is no effect.
  4. Write the setting down with the reason and the evidence (the table), so that the next model change can check whether it still holds. Lesson Automated tuning, and how not to overfit it shows why that check is not optional.


Summary


  • Four knobs, and each solver names them differently. Set each in the solver's own parameters: MathOpt's common heuristics was accepted by HiGHS at every level and changed nothing, and its common cuts was refused.
  • Judge a setting on two measures. Work to a plan within 1%, and work to proof, both as paired ratios over seeds and instances; date each plan with a counter that you have seen advance (CP-SAT's deterministic time stood still inside its callback).
  • Heuristics find the plans. Off, good plans came 10.74 (SCIP) and 6.89 (Gurobi) times later; more effort than the default bought nothing.
  • Presolve and cuts trade early plans against the proof, differently on each solver. Presolve off sped up SCIP's proof (0.64) and slowed Gurobi's (1.17); aggressive cuts cost Gurobi 1.73 times the work.
  • Focus says which side of the gap to work on. SCIP's feasibility emphasis reached good plans with 0.39 of the work and needed 1.51 times as much to prove them.
  • Defaults win here. Change one knob at a time, and adopt it only when both measures agree.

Design Note: One Report for Every Setting


settings_bench.report takes runs and prints the same columns for every solver and every setting: solved count, the two ratios, the wins and losses. Nothing about a setting is special-cased. That uniformity is what makes the four tables comparable at a glance, and it is what lesson Automated tuning, and how not to overfit it's tuner builds on: a tuner is this report run over many settings, with a rule for picking one.
