  17. PDHG and PDLP

GridCap's analysts solve the LP relaxation of the capital plan many times a night: once per scenario, per region, per budget case. The relaxation is the plan with "fund it or not" loosened to "fund any share from 0 to 1" (lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails), and its optimum is an upper bound on what any real plan can be worth. A colleague asks whether a GPU (graphics processing unit: a card with thousands of small arithmetic cores that run the same instruction on many numbers at once) would make those solves faster. The answer depends on the algorithm. Simplex and interior point, the methods of lessons Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) and Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP, spend their time solving systems of linear equations, and that work does not spread well over thousands of cores. A first-order method spends its time on matrix-vector products, and those do. This lesson shows the first-order method HiGHS, cuOpt and OR-Tools share, PDHG, on GridCap's numbers; what PDLP adds to it; and what it gives up: it stops at a tolerance, not at a vertex. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

Lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP met PDHG on a two-variable LP with rows of the form $Ax \le b$. Here the rows are GridCap's, some of them equalities, and the program runs on the matrix as a whole.

The Model

The LP relaxation of GridCap small's start-year model (lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, without its parent-child rows), built by lps.gridcap_timing("small"):

The code names each row by its label: once_P001, must_P003, alt_G001, budget_2029. The 47 rows and 80 columns hold 204 nonzero coefficients.

The module's programs keep a model as a plain dict (objective, rows in compressed sparse row form, row bounds, variable bounds), so HiGHS, OR-Tools' PDLP and cuOpt all read the same numbers. In that form every row is two-sided: $\ell^c_r \le a_r x \le u^c_r$, with $\ell^c_r = -\infty$ for a $\le$ row and $\ell^c_r = u^c_r = 1$ for a must row.

Every LP Is a Saddle Point: the Plan Maximises, the Prices Minimise

Write the model as $\max c^\top x$ subject to $\ell^c \le Ax \le u^c$ and $\ell \le x \le u$. Give each row $r$ one price $y_r$ of any sign: $y_r > 0$ prices the row's upper side ($a_r x \le u^c_r$), $y_r < 0$ its lower side. With $[y]_+ = \max(y, 0)$ and $[y]_- = \max(-y, 0)$, the Lagrangian is

For a price $y_r \ge 0$ the row's term is $y_r (u^c_r - a_r x)$, its slack valued at the price, exactly lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP's $y^\top (b - Ax)$. For $y_r \le 0$ it is $|y_r| (a_r x - \ell^c_r)$, the slack above the lower side. A row with no lower side ($\ell^c_r = -\infty$) can only take a price $y_r \ge 0$, and a row with no upper side only $y_r \le 0$.

The optimal plan and the optimal prices form a saddle point of $L$:

The plan $x$ makes $L$ as large as it can, the prices make it as small as they can, and at the saddle point neither can gain by moving alone. The inner minimum is $c^\top x$ when $x$ keeps every row (the best price on a kept row is 0) and $-\infty$ when it breaks one, so the outer maximum is the LP. The OR-Tools PDLP documentation writes the same problem with a minimisation outside, the objective's sign flipped, and $K = -A$; the content is the same.

A PDHG Iteration Is Two Matrix-Vector Products and Two Clips

PDHG, the primal-dual hybrid gradient method, moves the plan up its gradient and the prices down theirs, in turn. With step sizes $\tau, \sigma > 0$, one iteration is

The first line is lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP's primal step: $c - A^\top y$ is each variable's reduced cost at prices $y$, and the clip puts each variable back between its bounds. The second and third lines are the dual step at the look-ahead point $2x^{k+1} - x^k$. The third line is the clip for a two-sided row: if the plan overshoots the upper side, the first term raises the upper price; if it falls below the lower side, the second term makes the price negative; if it sits between, both terms shrink toward 0. When $\ell^c_r = -\infty$ the second term is always 0, and the line becomes Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP's $\max(0, \cdot)$. pdhg_gridcap.py writes it with NumPy and SciPy, on the whole matrix:

Constant steps converge when $\tau \sigma \lVert A \rVert_2^2 \le 1$, where $\lVert A \rVert_2$ is the matrix's largest singular value (the PDLP paper cites this condition). The program uses $\tau = \sigma = 0.9 / \lVert A \rVert_F$; the Frobenius norm (the square root of the sum of all squared entries) is never smaller than $\lVert A \rVert_2$, so the condition holds.

Problem statement

A feed mill blends ingredients into a ration. Ingredient $j$ costs $c_j$ per tonne, at most $u_j$ tonnes of it are in stock, and each nutrient row $i$ must be met exactly: a tonne of ingredient $j$ brings $A_{ij}$ units of nutrient $i$, and the ration needs $b_i$ units. The mill wants the cheapest ration, and its LPs will grow too large to factor, so the team wants a first-order method.

Before you code, write the model (variables with their bounds, the objective, the rows). This model differs from the lesson's in two ways: it minimises, and its rows are equalities.

The sign convention the tests use. The Lagrangian is $L(x, y) = c^\top x + y^\top (b - A x)$ over $0 \le x \le u$, with one price $y_i$ per nutrient row. The plan minimises it and the prices maximise it, so the plan moves down $c - A^\top y$ and each price moves up its row's shortfall $b_i - a_i x$. An equality row has both sides, so its price may take either sign and is never clipped. One iteration is then

This is the lesson's iteration rewritten for a minimisation with equality rows: in the lesson's form (maximise $-c^\top x$, price on the upper side), the price of an exact row is the negative of this $y$, and the lesson's dual clip $\max(0, v - \sigma b) + \min(0, v - \sigma b)$ is just $v - \sigma b$ when both sides equal $b$.

Write pdhg_step(A, b, c, u, x, y, tau, sigma) in blend.py: one PDHG iteration, a primal step clipped to the bounds and then a dual step at the look-ahead point $2x^{k+1} - x^k$, returning (x_new, y_new) as NumPy arrays. solve, which runs your step from zero, is given. About three lines.

Those Products Are What a GPU Does Best


An iteration reads each nonzero of $A$ twice, once for $A x$ and once for $A^\top y$, and then clips two vectors element by element. Every row of $A x$ is a separate sum that needs nothing from the other rows, and every clip is separate too. That is the shape of work a GPU is built for: thousands of cores each take a few rows, with no core waiting for another, and the speed is set by how fast the card's memory streams the matrix past them. PDHG never solves for the prices exactly: each iteration moves them by one dual step (the second and third lines of the iteration above), and the next iteration starts from there.


Simplex and interior point do different work. A simplex iteration solves linear systems with the current basis, and an interior point iteration solves one with a matrix built from $A$ and the current point (lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP). Both factorize a matrix (split it into triangular factors, the LU or Cholesky factorization), and a factorization is a chain of steps where each one needs the ones before it, and it can fill zero entries with nonzeros, so it needs more memory than $A$. That chain does not split cleanly into thousands of independent pieces. A first-order method never factorizes anything: it needs memory for $A$ and a few vectors, and nothing else. The PDLP paper gives this as the reason it scales, since its core operation is the matrix-vector product, and the cuPDLP papers built GPU versions on it (lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools measures two of them).


Plain PDHG Stalls on GridCap's Numbers; Rescaling Rescues It


The bare iteration above, run 100,000 times on GridCap small, and then again after rescaling the rows and columns:



As built, the method is stuck: after 100,000 iterations the plan is 13% above the optimum and still breaks a row by 3.0. The reason is in the first output line: the entries of $A$ run from 1 (the once and must rows) to 5,680 (a budget row). The step sizes must suit the largest entries, so they are tiny ($4.66 \times 10^{-5}$). A price moves by $\sigma$ times its row's breach per iteration, about $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$ for the breach of 3.0 in the table, so the prices crawl and the plan with them. After rescaling the steps are 1,500 times larger. Raising the steps that far by hand is no cure: with the entries as built, steps 1,500 times larger break the convergence condition above many times over. Rescaling shrinks the large entries, so that the condition itself allows the larger steps.


Rescaling (also called diagonal preconditioning) fixes the units. It multiplies row $j$ by a number $r_j$ and column $i$ by a number $s_i$, so that the rescaled matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(r)\, A \, \operatorname{diag}(s)$ and every row's and column's largest entry is near 1, solves the rescaled LP for $x' = x / s$, and maps the answer back. The Ruiz method does it by repeatedly dividing by the square root of each row's and column's largest entry:






The same iteration on the rescaled LP reaches a relative error of $4.2 \times 10^{-4}$ in 1,000 iterations and $4.8 \times 10^{-6}$ in 100,000. Rescaling changes the units, not the LP: the plan mapped back is a plan of the original model, and the row bounds and objective scale with it. It is still slow, and it wanders (at 10,000 iterations the violation is back up to 2.9), which is what PDLP's other additions are for.


PDLP Adds Restarts, Rescaling, Adaptive Steps and a Primal Weight


PDLP (primal-dual hybrid gradient for LP) is PDHG with enhancements that make it practical. The PDLP paper names diagonal preconditioning, presolving, adaptive step sizes and adaptive restarting, and its algorithm also updates a primal weight. OR-Tools' parameters (PrimalDualHybridGradientParams in solvers.proto) let you switch each one off:


  • Rescaling. Ruiz rescaling (l_inf_ruiz_iterations, 5 rounds by default) and then a rescaling by the rows' and columns' 2-norms (l2_norm_rescaling, on), as in the section above.
  • Adaptive restarts (restart_strategy, ADAPTIVE_HEURISTIC). PDHG's average of recent iterates is often closer to the optimum than its last iterate, but averaging over a long run slows it down. PDLP periodically restarts the method from the better of the two, judged by a measure of distance from optimality, which keeps the progress and drops the slow tail.
  • Adaptive step sizes (linesearch_rule, ADAPTIVE_LINESEARCH_RULE). Instead of the safe but pessimistic $0.9 / \lVert A \rVert$, PDLP tries a larger step, checks a condition from the convergence theory, and reduces it and tries again only if the step fails it.
  • Primal weight $\omega$ (primal_weight_update_smoothing). The steps are $\tau = \eta / \omega$ and $\sigma = \eta\,\omega$, so $\omega$ trades how far the plan moves against how far the prices move. PDLP starts it from the ratio of the objective's norm to the bounds' norm and updates it at each restart, so that neither side lags.
  • Presolve (presolve_options.use_glop, off by default in OR-Tools): simplifications before the method starts.


pdlp_gridcap.py solves GridCap small with OR-Tools' PDLP through its direct API (the one OR-Tools' simple_pdlp_program.py sample uses), once with the defaults and once with each feature switched off:






With everything on, PDLP reaches the tolerance in 576 iterations, against the 100,000 that plain PDHG did not finish. Each feature's share, on this model: without rescaling it does not finish either (the stall of the section above); without restarts it needs 13 times the iterations; a constant step needs 1.8 times; a frozen primal weight costs 11% more. These are one model's numbers; the PDLP paper measures each feature over hundreds of LPs.


None of these features adds a factorization. Each iteration is still matrix-vector products and clips, plus a few vector norms, so PDLP keeps the property that puts it on a GPU.


A First-Order Method Stops at a Tolerance, Not at a Vertex


Simplex ends on a vertex with a basis: an exact corner of the feasible region, with the list of variables that define it (lessons Solving a 2-variable LP by picture and Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation)). PDLP has neither. It stops when its point is close enough, by three measures from the PDLP paper, each relative to the size of the data:



OR-Tools sets $\varepsilon$ in simple_optimality_criteria (both the relative and the absolute part default to $10^{-6}$); ortools_pdlp in lps.py sets both to the tolerance it is given:




Read it row by row:


  • The tolerance sets the cost. Each factor of 100 in accuracy costs more iterations: 64, 192, 576, 896. The answer is as accurate as asked and no more.
  • The relative error tracks the tolerance. At every row the objective is within the tolerance of the simplex optimum, relatively.
  • "Optimal" allows broken rows. At $10^{-6}$ the plan breaks a row by 0.012, in $k. That is allowed: the primal measure compares the breach with the size of all the row bounds together, and GridCap's budgets run to thousands. A planner who reads "optimal" as "keeps every budget to the dollar" is wrong at every tolerance above. At $10^{-8}$ the breach is $6.3 \times 10^{-8}$.
  • It is not the vertex. Simplex ends with 72 of the 80 values exactly 0 or 1; PDLP, even at $10^{-8}$, has 70. Its point is near the optimal face, not on its corner, and it returns no basis. A model that needs a basis (for the warm starts of lesson Solution hints and ModelSolveParameters, or for sensitivity ranges read from it) needs a simplex solve, or a crossover step that some solvers run after PDLP to move to a vertex.


The trace shows how it gets there:



The primal and dual objectives close in on each other from both sides, but not steadily: the primal residual (how far the rows are broken) falls to $5.2 \times 10^{-4}$ at iteration 256 and rises to 0.85 at 320. The residuals are not monotone, which is why PDLP checks all three measures together, and why "how many iterations" has no answer before the run.

Problem statement


A staffing team buys shifts: shift $j$ costs $c_j$ and covers $A_{ij}$ hours of need $i$, and each need must be covered at least $b_i$ hours. Their LP, $\min c^\top x$ subject to $Ax \ge b$ and $x \ge 0$, will be solved by a first-order method, and they need the rule that says when to stop.


Before you code, write this LP's dual: one price $y_i \ge 0$ per row, the dual objective, and the sign each reduced cost $c_j - (A^\top y)_j$ must have when $x_j$ has a lower bound of 0 and no upper bound. Then write PDLP's three relative tests for this form, each with tolerance $\varepsilon$: the gap between the primal and dual objectives, against $1 + \lvert\text{primal}\rvert + \lvert\text{dual}\rvert$; the 2-norm of how far the plan leaves rows short, against $1 + \lVert b \rVert_2$; and the 2-norm of the reduced costs of the wrong sign, against $1 + \lVert c \rVert_2$.


Write converged(A, b, c, x, y, eps) in stopping.py: True when all three hold. The inputs are NumPy arrays and y is already non-negative. About eight lines.

Summary


  • Every LP is a saddle point: the plan maximises, the prices minimise. One price per row, of either sign for a two-sided row; at the saddle point neither side gains by moving.
  • A PDHG iteration is two matrix-vector products and two clips. The dual clip for a two-sided row is $\max(0, v - \sigma u^c) + \min(0, v - \sigma \ell^c)$.
  • Those products are what a GPU does best. Each row's sum is independent, and there is no factorization to chain the work or fill the memory.
  • Plain PDHG stalls on GridCap's numbers; rescaling rescues it. As built, 13% off after 100,000 iterations; rescaled, $4.8 \times 10^{-6}$.
  • PDLP adds restarts, rescaling, adaptive steps and a primal weight. 576 iterations to $10^{-6}$ with all of them; without rescaling, not done in 100,000.
  • A first-order method stops at a tolerance, not at a vertex. Relative criteria on the gap and the two residuals; rows broken by up to 0.012 at $10^{-6}$; no basis.

Design Note: One Model Format for Every Solver


Every program of this module builds its model once, as a dict of plain lists, and hands the same numbers to HiGHS, to OR-Tools' PDLP (through SciPy's sparse matrices) and to cuOpt (as JSON, in a separate process). A comparison between solvers is then a comparison between algorithms and devices, never between two model builders that might disagree on a row. violation checks any solver's answer against those same lists, so no solver grades its own work.
