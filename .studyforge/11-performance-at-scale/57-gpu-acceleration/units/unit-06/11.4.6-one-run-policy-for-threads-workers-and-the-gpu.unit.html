By now GridCap's planning service has settings in many places: one thread for HiGHS so plans repeat (lesson Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver), workers for CP-SAT, a GPU when there is one (lesson Detecting a GPU, with a CPU fallback that gives the same answer), PDLP's tolerance, a gap for the MILP, a method for cuOpt (lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records). Each was decided for a good reason, and each lives next to the call that uses it. The result is a service whose behaviour no one can state in one place, and whose manifest records what the code happened to pass, not what was intended. This lesson gathers every one of those decisions into a single run policy: one object, made by one function from the job and the machine, that decides the device, solver, method, threads and tolerance, falls back to the CPU, is written into the manifest, and says how a GPU result may be used for a capital plan. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

The Model

The policy decides how to run the models of this module; it builds none. They are, in brief:

The capital plan (lesson MIP on a GPU: what cuOpt accelerates and why branch-and-bound stays on the CPU), GridCap medium: projects $i \in P$, start years $t \in T_i$, must-do projects $M$, alternative groups $\mathcal{G}$, budgets $B_y$, spend $s_{i,t,y}$ and NPV $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ in $k, and $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$:

Its LP relaxation (lesson Why first-order LP methods suit a GPU: PDHG and PDLP): the same rows with $0 \le x_{i,t} \le 1$, here for GridCap small. The transportation LP (lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools): $x_{i,j} \ge 0$, minimise $\sum k_{i,j} x_{i,j}$ subject to $\text{supply}_i$ and $\text{demand}_j$, here with $n = 300$ (90,000 variables, 180,000 nonzeros), standing in for a large LP.

One Object Decides the Device, Solver, Method, Threads and Tolerance

A job says what is being solved and what the answer is for. A run policy says how to solve it. Both are frozen dataclasses, so a policy cannot be changed after it is made:

One function makes the policy, from the job and one fact about the machine:

Each branch carries a reason from this module:

  • The plan of record never goes to the GPU. It must repeat bit for bit for the auditor, and HiGHS on one thread does (lessons Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver and MIP on a GPU: what cuOpt accelerates and why branch-and-bound stays on the CPU); cuOpt's MILP does not (lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records). A continuous plan of record goes to simplex for the same reason, and because simplex ends on a vertex (lesson Why first-order LP methods suit a GPU: PDHG and PDLP).
  • A starting plan may come from cuOpt, with a looser gap, because it will be checked and only handed to the CPU solver as a start. threads 0 means cuOpt picks its own CPU workers; HiGHS gets exactly 1.
  • A large LP goes to the GPU's PDLP, a small one stays on the CPU. GPU_MIN_NONZEROS is 100,000: on the build machine the GPU first matched the CPU at the 300 × 300 transportation LP (180,000 nonzeros) and lost clearly at 100 × 100 (20,000), lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools.
  • Every tolerance is explicit, so the manifest can record what "optimal" promised.

Problem statement

A rail operator's planning service runs three kinds of job: answers it publishes (timetables the public sees), exploratory runs for analysts, and LP bounds. It runs on laptops, on servers and on one GPU machine, and the team wants every decision about how a job runs in one frozen object, Policy(device, solver, method, threads, tolerance, verify) (given in policy.py). A job is {"kind": "lp" | "milp", "nonzeros": int, "use": "publish" | "explore" | "bound"}; a machine is {"gpu": bool, "cuopt": bool, "cores": int}.

The rules, in order:

  1. A published answer must repeat: CPU, HiGHS, one thread, never verified elsewhere. A MILP uses "branch-and-bound" at gap 1e-4; an LP uses "simplex" at 1e-7.
  2. Any other MILP goes to cuOpt ("gpu", "cuopt", "milp", all the machine's cores, gap 1e-2) when the machine has a GPU and cuOpt; otherwise to HiGHS's "branch-and-bound" on the CPU, with 4 threads or the machine's cores if fewer, gap 1e-2.
  3. Any other LP with at least 500,000 nonzeros goes to HiGHS's "pdlp" on the GPU (one thread, 1e-6) when there is a GPU; otherwise to HiGHS's "ipm" on the CPU, with 4 threads or fewer as above, at 1e-8.
  4. Every other LP: CPU, HiGHS "simplex", one thread, 1e-7.

Every answer from a GPU is verified on the CPU (verify=True); no CPU answer is. Write decide(job, machine) in policy.py. About fifteen lines.

A GPU Result Serves a Capital Plan Only as a Bound or a Start, Verified on the CPU


Every GPU branch in the policy has verify_on_cpu set, and run acts on it before anything uses the answer:




A MILP plan is judged as lesson MIP on a GPU: what cuOpt accelerates and why branch-and-bound stays on the CPU judges one: against the tightest bound any solver proved, not against the bound of the solver that found it. So run also solves the job with HiGHS on one CPU thread to the same gap, and passes the smaller of the two bounds (the model is a maximisation) to check. A cuOpt plan that only met cuOpt's own bound, when HiGHS had proved a tighter one, is refused. cuOpt's plans change from run to run (lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records), so the objective above is one run's.


Why each use is acceptable, and the one that is not:


  • An LP bound or relaxation. The relaxation's optimum caps every plan's worth (lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails); analysts use it to say how far a plan is from the best possible. A GPU's PDLP gives it within its tolerance, $2 \times 10^{-9}$ here, which is far finer than any budget decision. The CPU interior point confirms it, and the manifest says it was confirmed.
  • A starting or heuristic plan. A cuOpt plan that passes lesson MIP on a GPU: what cuOpt accelerates and why branch-and-bound stays on the CPU's check (rows kept, integral, within its gap) can be handed to HiGHS as a start (lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan). HiGHS then proves, or improves, the plan on the CPU, and the plan of record is HiGHS's. A start that fails the check is dropped and the CPU solve runs without it.
  • The plan of record: never from the GPU. It must repeat bit for bit and carry a full proof from one solver, and no GPU path in this module repeats a MILP plan. The policy has no branch that sends it there, whatever the machine has.


The rule in one line: the GPU may make the night shorter, never the plan different. Everything it produces is either a number the CPU confirms or a start the CPU solver improves on, and every answer a planner signs comes from a run the auditor can repeat.

Problem statement


A port authority plans its capital works with a MILP that maximises value, and its nightly service now has GPU answers on hand. Its rule: a GPU answer may serve as an LP bound or as a start for the CPU solver, each verified on the CPU, and never as the plan of record.


Write usable(use, gpu, cpu) in answers.py, returning (accepted, value) where value is the thing to use when accepted and a reason when not:


  • use == "plan": never; the reason is "the plan of record runs on the CPU".
  • use == "bound": gpu is {"status", "objective"} from an LP relaxation and cpu is {"objective"} from a CPU solve of the same LP. Refuse with "not optimal" unless the GPU status is "Optimal"; refuse with "not confirmed" unless the two objectives differ by at most $10^{-6}$ relatively (divided by $\max(1, \lvert\text{CPU objective}\rvert)$). Otherwise use the larger of the two values: a bound on a maximisation must not understate the cap.
  • use == "start": gpu is {"status", "x"} from a MILP solver and cpu is {"feasible", "integral"}, the CPU check of x. Use x when both checks passed, whatever the status label says; otherwise refuse with "fails the check".

HiGHS Stays at the Centre of the Policy


This course runs HiGHS as its default solver (the course README says so, and lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP set it beside GLOP and PDLP). The policy keeps it at the centre on every device: HiGHS's simplex and interior point on the CPU, HiGHS's own GPU PDLP (cuPDLP-C) for a large LP, and HiGHS on one thread for the plan of record. A team that standardises on HiGHS therefore gets the GPU path from the same library, with the same model format, the same status codes and the same version pin, and the CPU fallback is HiGHS too. cuOpt enters only where it is the better tool: a GPU heuristic plan, checked.


Summary


  • One object decides the device, solver, method, threads and tolerance. decide(job, gpu) is pure and frozen; every gpu=False branch is a CPU branch.
  • The policy falls back to the CPU and is recorded in the manifest. A refused GPU answer makes run ask the policy again without a GPU, and the record holds the policy that ran.
  • A GPU result serves a capital plan only as a bound or a start, verified on the CPU. The LP bound is confirmed by an interior point solve; a cuOpt plan is checked and then improved by HiGHS; the plan of record never comes from the GPU.
  • HiGHS stays at the centre of the policy. CPU simplex and interior point, GPU PDLP, and the one-thread plan of record all come from HiGHS.

Design Note: A Policy Is Data, So It Can Be Diffed


Because a policy is a frozen dataclass printed into the manifest, a change of policy shows up as a change of data: two manifests that differ only in policy.device tell the auditor exactly why their bits may differ, and a review of decide is a review of one function with one table of outputs. The alternative, settings scattered beside each call, has no single place to review and nothing to record.
