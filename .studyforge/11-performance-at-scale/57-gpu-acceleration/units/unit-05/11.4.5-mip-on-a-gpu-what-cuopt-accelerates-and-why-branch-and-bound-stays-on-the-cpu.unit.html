  16. MIP on a GPU

The LPs of lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools went faster on a GPU when they were large. GridCap's real product is not an LP, though: it is a capital plan, a MILP with yes-or-no decisions, and NVIDIA's cuOpt solves MILPs too. This lesson reads cuOpt's own log to see which parts of a MILP solve it runs on the GPU and which it leaves on the CPU, solves GridCap medium's capital-planning MILP with HiGHS on one thread and with cuOpt, and checks cuOpt's plans instead of trusting them, since lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records found they do not repeat. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

Branch and Bound Is a Tree of Small, Dependent Steps

A MIP solver's work, from the lessons of section 3, has several parts: presolve shrinks the model (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first); the root LP solves the relaxation; cuts tighten it (lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand); primal heuristics look for plans (lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS); and branch and bound splits the problem into a tree of nodes, solving an LP at each, until the bound and the best plan meet (lessons Branching on a fractional variable to Node and variable selection).

A node's LP differs from its parent's by one variable's bound, and the solver re-solves it from the parent's basis with dual simplex, a few pivots each (lesson Re-solving after the data changes measured that kind of warm re-solve). Each node's LP is small; each pivot depends on the one before; which node comes next depends on what the last ones found. That is the opposite of the work lesson Why first-order LP methods suit a GPU: PDHG and PDLP put on a GPU: not one large matrix-vector product with thousands of independent rows, but many small sequential steps that each wait for the last. Thousands of GPU cores have nothing to do on one pivot.

So the parts of a MIP solve that suit a GPU are the ones made of large, regular, independent work: a first-order LP method on a big relaxation, and a heuristic that evaluates many candidate moves at once.

cuOpt's Log Says Which Parts Run on the GPU

milp_gpu.py keeps four kinds of lines from cuOpt's log (the worker drops every line that names the card, the CPU model or the memory). On the build machine, one run:

Read with the full log of the toolchain's cuOpt probe, which shows the rest:

  • Presolve (Papilo, then cuOpt's own) runs on CPU threads: 4 of them here.
  • The feasibility-jump heuristic, a primal heuristic (lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS) that searches for a feasible plan by changing variables' values to shrink how much the rows are broken, runs as 15 CPU workers (CPUFJ) and 1 GPU worker (GPUFJ). This is the GPU's main MILP job in the log: a search that tries many changes at once is the kind of large, regular work a GPU suits.
  • The root LP is solved concurrently: PDLP on the GPU races dual simplex and barrier (lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records). Here dual simplex won, in 153 iterations: on an LP this size, the CPU method finished first, as lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools would predict.
  • Cuts and branch and bound run on the CPU, as do the other heuristics. cuOpt's documentation says the same in its own terms: with the GPU and CPU both running, the CPU works on the dual bound (the tree), and its heuristics_only setting keeps only the GPU's primal search.

So "MIP on a GPU" means: the GPU helps find plans and can solve a large root LP; proving a plan is good still happens in a branch-and-bound tree on the CPU.

HiGHS on One Thread Is the Plan of Record

HiGHS has no GPU MIP solver; its branch and bound runs on the CPU, and with one thread it repeats bit for bit (lesson Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver). milp_gpu.py solves the model twice and checks the result:

HiGHS stopped at the root node (1 nodes): its presolve, cuts and heuristics found a plan worth 226,886 and proved a bound of 227,074, within 0.083% of each other, before branching. The second run gave the same bits. That is what a plan of record needs: an auditor who reruns it gets the same plan, and lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records's manifest can hold it to every bit.

A MILP in a HighsLp: Integer Columns and the Sense

The first practice below builds its model as one highspy.HighsLp, the object lesson PDLP on CPU vs. GPU: a measured comparison with HiGHS (cuPDLP-C) and OR-Tools introduced with highs_lp.py. The fields are the same as for an LP (num_col_, num_row_, col_cost_, col_lower_ and col_upper_, row_lower_ and row_upper_ with highspy.kHighsInf for a missing side, and a_matrix_ with format_, start_, index_ and value_). For a row-wise matrix, format_ is highspy.MatrixFormat.kRowwise and start_[r] is where row $r$'s entries begin in index_ (their column numbers) and value_. Two more lines make it a maximising MILP:

    The crew's job selection, as a plan of record

    • the plan of record is a MILP formulated by hand and solved on one CPU thread, so a rerun repeats

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A maintenance crew chooses which jobs to do this year. Job $j$ is worth $v_j$ ($k of avoided risk) and takes $h_{j,q}$ crew-weeks in quarter $q$; quarter $q$ has $C_q$ crew-weeks. Some jobs depend on others: for each pair $(a, b)$ in requires, job $b$ may be done only if job $a$ is. The selection is the plan of record, so it must come from a solve that repeats bit for bit when the auditors rerun it.

Before you code, write the formulation: the binary variables, the objective, one labelled row per quarter and one per dependency, with their quantifiers. Then write build(values, hours, capacity, requires) in crew.py, returning a highspy.HighsLp that maximises the value. solve(lp, fixed=None) is given: it solves on one thread and can pin some jobs to 0 or 1 first, which is how the tests check plans you did not choose. Any correct formulation passes. About twenty-five lines.

A cuOpt Plan Is Checked, Not Trusted


cuOpt's plans do not repeat (in lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records, two runs with the same settings returned different plans), and its status may not say what a reader expects, so a program never takes its plan on its word. check tests three things against the model's own lists, not against anything cuOpt reported about itself:




  • Feasible: every row and bound kept, to $10^{-6}$.
  • Integral: every value within $10^{-5}$ of a whole number.
  • Good enough: the objective within the gap of the best bound any solver proved. For a maximisation each solver's bound is an upper limit on every plan, so the smallest of them is the tightest, and a plan is measured against that, not against the bound of the solver that found it.

What the run shows:


  • Both plans pass the check. They keep every row, are integral, and are within 0.1% of the best bound, 227,045, which cuOpt's second run proved (tighter than HiGHS's 227,074).
  • The two runs found different plans, worth 226,856 and 226,819, after different trees (76,961 and 67,430 nodes) and times. Neither is the plan of record; each differs from HiGHS's in 29 to 34 start decisions, and all three are within the gap. This is what "optimal within 0.1%" allows: many plans, none promised. Earlier runs of the same program found plans worth 226,828, 226,830 and 226,923.
  • The status is FeasibleFound, not Optimal, although both runs stopped at the gap target well inside the time limit. Code that accepts only Optimal would discard both plans; code that accepts any FeasibleFound would also accept a plan stopped far from the gap. The check above decides from the numbers, not the label.
  • The GPU did not make it faster here. HiGHS reached the same gap at the root node in 4.2 seconds; cuOpt took 7.2 to 8.5 seconds and tens of thousands of nodes. On the toolchain's harder 300-binary MILP, cuOpt reached its gap in 14.6 and 30.0 seconds against HiGHS's 44. Measure on your own models (lesson Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean).


The determinism setting mip_determinism_mode did not make cuOpt's plans repeat either (lesson Determinism on a GPU: what repeats, what doesn't, and what the manifest records): two 20-second runs on this model explored the same 127 nodes and returned plans worth 204,983 and 204,849, with a gap of 11%, both stopped by the clock.


So a cuOpt MILP plan has a place: a starting plan handed to HiGHS (lesson MIP starts from last cycle's plan), or a quick plan for a what-if, always checked as above. The plan of record comes from the CPU solver that repeats.

Problem statement


The maintenance crew's planners (jobs worth $v_j$, crew-weeks $h_{j,q}$ per quarter, capacity $C_q$, and pairs $(a, b)$ meaning job $b$ only with job $a$) now get a quick selection from a GPU MILP solver each night, to hand to their CPU solver as a start. The GPU solver's plans change from run to run, and its status line says FeasibleFound whether or not the plan is any good, so nothing it reports is taken on trust.


Write accept(x, reported, bounds, values, hours, capacity, requires, gap) in check.py, returning (accepted, reason). x is the solver's vector of values, reported the objective it reported, and bounds the upper bounds on the best possible worth proved by every solver that ran (this is a maximisation). Check, in this order, and return the first failure:


  1. every value within $10^{-5}$ of a whole number, else "not integral";
  2. round x once it passes that check, and judge the rounded plan from here on: values a hair off a whole number count as that whole number;
  3. each quarter's crew-weeks, from the rounded plan, within $C_q$ (to $10^{-6}$), else "over capacity in quarter <q>", with quarters numbered from 1;
  4. each dependency kept by the rounded plan, else "job <b> without job <a>";
  5. the rounded plan's own worth, computed from the plan (not reported), within gap of the tightest bound, relatively (divided by the bound), else "outside the gap".


Otherwise return (True, "accepted"). About fifteen lines.

Summary


  • Branch and bound is a tree of small, dependent steps. Node LPs are warm re-solves of a few pivots each; they do not spread over thousands of cores.
  • cuOpt's log says which parts run on the GPU. One GPU feasibility-jump worker and PDLP in the concurrent root LP; presolve, cuts, branch and bound and the other heuristics on the CPU.
  • HiGHS on one thread is the plan of record. It reached the 0.1% gap at the root and repeated bit for bit.
  • A cuOpt plan is checked, not trusted. Feasible, integral, within the gap of the best bound any solver proved; two runs gave two different plans of the same worth, reported as FeasibleFound.

Design Note: The Check Uses Every Bound, From Every Solver


A bound proved by any correct solver limits every plan, whichever solver found it. Taking the smallest bound across solvers makes the check stricter than any one solver's own gap, and it costs nothing: the bounds are already in the results. It also means a plan from a solver that reports a loose bound, or none, is still judged fairly.
