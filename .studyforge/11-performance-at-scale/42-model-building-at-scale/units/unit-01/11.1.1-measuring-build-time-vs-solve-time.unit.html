GridCap's planners have run the timing model on the medium portfolio (400 projects) for a year. Next cycle they move to the large one: 10,000 projects over 10 years, about 22,000 binary variables. The first trial run feels slow, and two fixes are proposed in the same meeting: buy a faster solver, or rewrite the code that builds the model. Both cost weeks. Neither should start before someone knows where the run's time goes. This lesson measures it, in four rules, and the numbers it prints are a method more than a result: your machine will give different ones.

The Model

Every program in this module builds and solves the same model: the timing model of lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows and Cash-flow spreading across years, with cash flow spread across the years of work and precedence in the cumulative form of lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms. Nothing in it changes in this module; what changes is how fast and how safely it is built.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use (its window, cut so the work ends inside $Y$); $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work, $k = 0, 1, \dots$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$.

A Run Has Phases, and Each Phase Gets Its Own Clock

A planning run does four things in order: it gets the data (here, generate the portfolio), builds the model, hands the model to the solver, and solves it. Put a clock around each one, not one clock around the whole run. time.perf_counter() is the clock to use: Python documents it as the highest-resolution clock for short durations, and only the difference between two readings means anything.

The solve gets two clocks, because mathopt.solve does more than solve. Its source shows the steps: it exports the model to a ModelProto (MathOpt's protocol-buffer form of a model, the one a solver reads), passes the proto to the solver's C++ code, and parses the result back into Python. result.solve_stats.solve_time is MathOpt's own measure of the middle step only, "roughly the time inside" the solver by its documentation, and it leaves out building the model. The wall time around mathopt.solve minus that reported time is the hand-off: export, set-up and parsing.

PARAMS stops HiGHS at a 1% relative gap (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap), the tolerance a planning team would use for a 10,000-project run. The program does three runs of each size. First the lines that repeat on every run:

Read the shares, not the seconds:

  • On medium, the solve is the run. The build is 4% of it. A builder twice as fast would save 2% of the run; a faster solve is the only lever that matters here.
  • On large, the build is no longer noise. It is 15% of the run: roughly one second of building for every five or six seconds of solving to a 1% gap. Building grew about 27 times from medium to large, in step with the model's size (25 times as many variables, 25 times as many nonzeros).
  • When the solve is short, the build can be most of the run. The last line solves the large model's LP relaxation (every $x_{i,t}$ allowed anywhere in $[0, 1]$), as a first bound or a quick what-if would. The build took twice as long as the solve.
  • The hand-off is small (about 1%), but it is not free, and it grows with the model, since the whole model is exported on every call.

So the answer to "buy a solver or rewrite the builder?" depends on which run you do most. A team that solves once a night to a tight gap should look at the solver. A team that rebuilds the model for every scenario, every what-if and every LP bound pays for the build each time. Lessons MIP starts from last cycle's plan to Automated tuning, and how not to overfit it work on the solve; this module works on the build.

One Timing Is an Anecdote: Warm Up, Repeat, and Report the Median and Range

A wall-clock time is a sample. The same build takes a different time on each run, because the operating system schedules other work, the CPU changes its speed with load and heat, and the first run of anything pays for caches and memory that later runs reuse. So a timing is reported the way any noisy measurement is: several samples, a middle value, and their spread. stopwatch.py does it in one function:

Timing keeps every sample and reports their median (the middle sample, which one slow outlier cannot move) and their range. The clock argument is there for tests: a test passes a fake clock that returns numbers it chose, so it can check the arithmetic without timing anything.

The program times one cold build of the medium model, then seven more. Here are two runs of it, one after the other, on the same machine:

The same code on the same data took a median of 0.021 s in one run and 0.037 s in the next, more than the spread inside either run. Two things follow:

  • Warm up before you time. The cold build was the slowest sample both times. measure runs the work once untimed (warmup=1) so that one-time costs do not land in the samples.
  • Compare like with like: same process, same machine, one right after the other. Whatever made the second run slower made everything in it slower. A ratio of two builders measured side by side survives that; two absolute times measured at different moments do not. When the machine is busy, alternate the two builders round by round (old, new, old, new, ...) and take the ratio within each round, so a slow patch hits both. This module's speed-ups are always ratios of builders timed in one process.

A speed claim in a test would fail on a busy machine for reasons that have nothing to do with the code. So this module never asserts a time in a test. Its tests check what the speed is for: that a fast builder makes exactly the model the slow one makes (lesson Vectorized model building from DataFrames). The timings are run and read by a person, like the ones above.

Problem statement

GridCap's model builds run on a shared test server, where other jobs come and go. Last week a rewrite of the budget rows was declared "three times faster" from one timing of the old builder on Monday and one of the new builder on Tuesday. Nobody could repeat it. The team wants a function that compares two builders fairly, on a busy machine, in one process.

Write speedup(old, new, rounds, warmup, clock) in compare.py. old and new are functions that take no arguments (each builds a model); clock is a function that returns the current time in seconds, time.perf_counter by default. The function returns how many times faster new is than old, measured this way:

  • first, warmup untimed runs of each builder, old then new, so one-time costs do not count;
  • then rounds rounds; each round times one run of old and then one run of new, each between two readings of clock, and computes the round's ratio, old's time divided by new's time;
  • the answer is the median of the round ratios (with an even number of rounds, the mean of the two middle ones).

A slow patch on the machine then hits both builders of the same round, and their ratio survives it. The tests pass a fake clock that moves only when a builder runs, so your function must read the clock only around each timed run; the given helper timed(work, clock) does exactly that for one run. Run the file to measure two toy functions on your machine. About five lines.

Time at Several Sizes to Learn How Build Time Grows


One size tells you how long a build takes. Two or more tell you how the time grows, which is what decides whether next year's portfolio will still build. If the time is proportional to $n^a$ for $n$ projects, then $a$ is the growth exponent: doubling $n$ multiplies the time by $2^a$, so 2 for a linear builder ($a = 1$) and 4 for a quadratic one ($a = 2$). Taking logarithms, $\log t = a \log n + \text{const}$, a straight line whose slope is $a$. growth.py fits that slope by least squares over every size it measured:




It times three builders on the first 1,250, 2,500, 5,000 and 10,000 projects of GridCap large (a child's parent is always the project just before it, so a prefix cuts no parent off):


  • build_loops, lesson Cash-flow spreading across years's builder, looping over GridCap's project objects;
  • build_slow, the first builder a team typically writes when its data arrives as pandas DataFrames: it walks the tables with iterrows and finds each project's rows with a boolean mask, starts[starts.project_id == pid], which compares every row of the table each time;
  • build_matrix, the builder lesson Vectorized model building from DataFrames develops.


build_slow is the one to look at:



A mask over a table of $n$ rows, done once per project, is $n \times n$ comparisons: quadratic work hidden in one readable line. Before any clock, the program counts that work, because counts repeat exactly:



Every doubling of the portfolio quadruples the rows the masks compare. Then the clock, from one run on the author's machine:




The rows compared quadrupled at every doubling, yet the slow builder's time grew with an exponent of 1.4, not 2. Both are true, and the gap between them is the lesson:


  • A count of work is not a time. Comparing one row inside a mask is a tight loop in compiled code and costs almost nothing; what costs is everything around it, the hundreds of microseconds pandas spends setting up each operation and each iterrows row. That part grows linearly. The quadratic part is still small at 1,250 projects and grows four times per doubling, so the fitted exponent sits between 1 and 2 and climbs as $n$ grows. At the next portfolio size it would be the whole bill.
  • The loop builder is close to linear (1.1): twice the projects, about twice the time.
  • The matrix builder hardly grows at all (0.6) over this range, because most of its time is a fixed cost per pandas operation, paid once per build whatever the size.
  • The exponent is noisier than the counts. An earlier run of the same program measured the slow builder at 3.97, 6.63, 19.42 and 42.96 seconds. Fit over several sizes, never two, and read the exponent to one digit.


The measured times disagree with a simple model of the code, and that is normal: measure the sizes you will run, and use the counts to say why the times bend.


A Profiler Names the Hot Spot, and Its Call Counts Repeat When Its Times Do Not


Knowing that a builder is slow does not say which line to fix. A profiler does: Python's cProfile records every function call while it is switched on, and pstats reports, for each function, how many times it was called (ncalls), the time spent in its own code (tottime) and the time including everything it called (cumtime). profile_build.py profiles one slow build of the first 2,500 projects:




A full report lists hundreds of pandas functions; the program picks out the ones that tell the story. Their call counts come from the code and the data alone, so they are the same on every run:



The times, from one run on the author's machine (cumulative, so a function's line includes the functions it calls: starts_of contains some of the masks below it, and the shares do not add up to 100%):




What the two tables say together:


  • The time is in pandas, not in MathOpt. Adding 5,415 variables and 2,924 rows to the model costs a few percent. The rest is the builder reading its own data.
  • The counts name the cause. 8,606 masks for 2,500 projects is more than three per project, each comparing a whole table. 33,049 iterrows rows each became a pandas Series (a one-row object pandas builds for every row it yields), and 163,721 field reads went through pandas' attribute lookup. pandas' own documentation says iterrows returns each row as a Series and points to itertuples as the faster way to walk a table.
  • The profiler inflates the times. The same build of 2,500 projects took about 10 s without the profiler in the growth run above and about 14 s with it, because the profiler does extra work on every one of the millions of calls. Python's documentation of cProfile notes that this error accumulates in code that makes many calls. Read a profile for where time goes, as shares and counts, and measure speed without it.


The fix is not to make each mask faster. It is to stop asking the same question of a whole table thousands of times: compute every coefficient for every row in a few operations on whole columns. That is lesson Vectorized model building from DataFrames.

Problem statement


A regional utility (fictional, like GridCap) times its model builder on four portfolio sizes this year and wants to know two things before next year's merger quadruples the portfolio: how fast the build time grows, and roughly how long the merged portfolio will take to build.


If the build takes about $t = c\, n^{a}$ seconds for $n$ projects, then $\log t = a \log n + \log c$: a straight line in $(\log n, \log t)$ whose slope is the growth exponent $a$ and whose intercept is $\log c$. Real measurements are noisy, so no line passes through all of them; take the least-squares line, the one that makes the sum of squared vertical distances smallest. Summary


  • A run has phases, and each phase gets its own clock. Data, build, hand-off and solve; solve_stats.solve_time is the solver's part, and the rest of mathopt.solve is the hand-off. On GridCap medium the solve was 94% of the run; on large the build was 15%, and twice the solve of the LP relaxation.
  • One timing is an anecdote: warm up, repeat, and report the median and range. The same build measured 0.021 s and 0.037 s in two runs; ratios of builders timed side by side are what carry over. Tests check identical models, never times.
  • Time at several sizes to learn how build time grows. The slope of $\log t$ against $\log n$ is the growth exponent; the slow builder's masks compare four times as many rows each time the portfolio doubles.
  • A profiler names the hot spot, and its call counts repeat when its times do not. The slow builder spends its time in pandas lookups, not in MathOpt.

Design Note: Report Times With Their Method, and Count What You Can


A time printed without its method is close to useless to the next reader: which machine, how many repeats, warm or cold, median or best, what else was running. This module prints the method next to every timing ("3 runs each; medians") and keeps timings in their own block, apart from the lines that repeat. Where a quantity can be counted instead of timed (rows compared, functions called, nonzeros built), the count goes in the test and the time goes in the report. Lesson Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean applies the same rule to comparing solvers.
