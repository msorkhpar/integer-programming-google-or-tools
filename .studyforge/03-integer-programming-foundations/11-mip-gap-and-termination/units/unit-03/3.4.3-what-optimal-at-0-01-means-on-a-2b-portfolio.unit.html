  18. What a reported gap is worth

A slide in GridCap's board pack says: "The ten-year capital plan is optimal (MIP gap 0.01%) on a $2B portfolio." It reads like a guarantee that nobody could improve the plan. A board member who has sat through a few of these asks three questions. 0.01% of what, and how much money is that? Does "optimal" mean the projects on the list are the right ones? And how sure are we of the NPVs the plan was optimized on? GridCap is a fictional utility, and its numbers are teaching data.

This lesson answers each question with a program: what a solver means by "optimal", what 0.01% is in money and what it is measured on, how a constant in the objective quietly changes it, how much two plans within the gap can differ, and how the gap compares with the error in the data. It ends with how to choose the tolerance.

The Model

The project-selection MILP of lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap, with the two changes this lesson makes marked. Each program uses the plain model unless it says otherwise.

Sets. $P$ the candidate projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, each with a parent $p(c)$; and, for the second change, $S^* \subseteq P$ the projects of the best plan.

Parameters. $v_i$, the NPV of project $i$ at its earliest start ($k); $c_{i,y}$, what project $i$ spends in year $y$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); and, for the first change, $K$, a constant added to the objective ($k), 0 unless stated.

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$: 1 if project $i$ is in the plan.

"Optimal" From a Solver Means Optimal Within a Tolerance

default_gap.py solves GridCap medium three times: HiGHS with no parameters at all, SCIP with no parameters at all, and HiGHS with relative_gap_tolerance=0.0. It also keeps HiGHS's log of the first run and prints its final gap line:

All three say OPTIMAL, and they do not mean the same thing:

  • HiGHS with its defaults stopped at a gap of $22k. Its log names the tolerance it applied when nobody set one: 0.01%, the documented default of its option mip_rel_gap. Its OPTIMAL means "no plan is worth more than 0.01% above this one". That is where the 0.01% on the slide most likely comes from: nobody chose it.
  • SCIP with its defaults closed the gap. On this model its default stop was the bound meeting the incumbent, after 12,663 nodes.
  • HiGHS, asked for a zero gap, closed it too, after 13,930 nodes instead of 1,662: more than eight times the work to prove the last $22k.

On this model, at this size, the default stop happened to return the same plan as the exact run: not one project differs. Nothing promised that. The default run proved only that its plan was within $22k of the best; that it was the best is something we know from the third run.

So the first answer for the board: "optimal" from a solver is optimal within the tolerances it ran with, and those default to different values in different solvers. A report that says "optimal" should name the tolerance, and a job should set its tolerances itself rather than inherit a default it never read.

0.01% Is an Amount of Money, Measured on the Objective

A relative gap is a share of the objective, so it becomes money only when you know the objective's value. scale.py does the arithmetic for the slide, then for GridCap's large portfolio, solved with HiGHS's defaults:

Three things to take from it:

  1. Ask what the $2B is. The gap is measured on the objective. If the slide's $2B is the objective (total NPV), 0.01% is $200k. If it is the portfolio's spend, it says nothing about the gap at all: GridCap large spends up to $9.95B but its objective is $6.0B of NPV, and 0.01% of that is $601k.
  2. A small share of a large number is a large amount. $601k is more than the NPV of 5,804 of the 10,000 candidate projects, and more than the median project's $435k. The tolerance therefore allows the best plan to be worth more than the reported one by more than a typical project.
  3. The guarantee is about the total, not the parts. "Within 0.01%" says the plan's NPV is close to the best NPV. It says nothing about which projects the best plan holds; the section after next shows how different two close plans can be.

This run stopped after a single node at a gap of $265k (0.0044%), below the tolerance. Whether closing the rest of it would be worth more time is the question of the last two sections.

A Constant in the Objective Shrinks the Relative Gap Without Improving the Plan

Objectives often carry a constant. A "total portfolio value" that includes the NPV of projects already committed, the value of existing assets, or fixed costs that every plan pays: each adds the same number $K$ to every plan's objective. The constant changes no decision, since the best plan is the best plan with or without it. It does change the relative gap, because the gap is divided by the incumbent, and the incumbent now includes $K$:

offset.py adds $K$ of 0, $2,000,000k and $20,000,000k to the medium objective and solves each with the same relative tolerance of 0.01%:

It prints the plan's NPV without the constant, the gap in $k, the relative gap the solver saw (constant included) and the relative gap on the NPV the plan actually earns:

With no constant the run ends with the best plan. With a $2B constant the solver divides by about $2.22B instead of $224M, so 0.01% of what it sees is ten times as much money: it stopped at a gap of $222k and a plan $111k worse. With $20B it accepted a plan $193k below the best, and reported a gap of 0.0021%, about ninety times smaller than the true 0.1873% on the NPV. The plans got worse, and the reported gap got smaller.

This is how a "0.01%" on a slide can hide a gap ten or a hundred times larger on the money the decisions control. Two fixes, both simple: keep constants out of the objective and add them in the report, or state the tolerance in money with absolute_gap_tolerance, which no constant changes (the bound and the incumbent move by the same $K$, so their difference does not).

  1. Coding practice

    Take the constant out of the gap

    • a constant in the objective adds to the solver's denominator, so the gap on the decisions is the absolute gap over the objective without it

Problem statement

A water utility's pump-replacement model reports its objective as "total network value": the value of the replacements it chooses plus a fixed amount $K$ for the network that already exists. The solver's relative gap divides by that total, so its reports look better than the decisions deserve. The analytics team wants every report to carry the gap on the part the decisions control.

Write true_gap(incumbent, bound, constant) in true_gap.py. incumbent and bound are the two numbers the solver reported, both including constant. Return a dict:

  • "absolute": $|\text{bound} - \text{incumbent}|$. The constant cancels out of this difference.
  • "seen": the absolute gap divided by $|\text{incumbent}|$, which is what the solver saw.
  • "on_decisions": the absolute gap divided by $|\text{incumbent} - \text{constant}|$, the value of the decisions alone.

The model may be a maximization or a minimization, and the constant may be negative (a sunk cost taken off every plan). Both ratios are 0.0 when the absolute gap is 0, and math.inf when their denominator is 0 and the absolute gap is not.

Plans Within the Gap Can Pick Different Projects


A gap is a promise about the plan's value. near_plans.py shows what it does not promise. It solves the medium model to a zero gap, then with a 1% tolerance, and compares the plans. Then it looks for the second-best plan: it adds one row that every plan except the best one obeys, and solves again.


The row works like this. Let $S^*$ be the best plan's projects. For the best plan itself, $\sum_{i \in S^*} x_i = |S^*|$ and $\sum_{i \notin S^*} x_i = 0$, so the left side is $|S^*|$, one more than the row allows. Any other plan either drops a project of $S^*$ (the first sum falls by at least 1) or adds one outside it (the second sum rises by at least 1), so the left side is at most $|S^*| - 1$ and the row holds. The row cuts off exactly one plan:






The second-best plan is worth $3k less than the best, a relative difference of about 0.0013%, well inside a 0.01% tolerance. It swaps two projects (P077 and P244, $116k of NPV between them) for one other (P218, $113k). A run stopped at 0.01% could have returned either plan, and both would be "optimal at 0.01%". The plan from the 1% stop differs from the best in 19 projects.


So the second answer for the board: the gap guarantees the plan's value, not its list of projects. "P077 is in the optimal plan" is a stronger claim than any run within a tolerance proves. When a planner's question is about one project ("must P077 be funded?"), answer that question directly: solve once with $x_{P077} = 1$ and once with $x_{P077} = 0$, and compare the two runs. If both closed their gaps, compare their values. If they stopped with gaps, compare their brackets (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap): funding P077 is proved better only when the forced-in run's incumbent is above the forced-out run's bound, since then some plan with P077 beats every plan without it. When the two brackets overlap, the runs have not settled the question, and tighter runs must.

The Forecast's Error Is Larger Than the Gap


The NPVs the plan is optimized on are forecasts: they depend on failure predictions, costs and a discount rate, none of them known to 0.01%. noise.py asks what a modest error does. It takes the plan HiGHS returns on the forecast NPVs, then three times moves every project's NPV by a random amount of up to 5% either way, re-solves, and compares:


  • best plan: the best plan for the moved NPVs (HiGHS's defaults, so within 0.01% of the best);
  • our plan: the original plan, valued at the moved NPVs;
  • lost: how much the original plan earns less than the best plan, if the moved NPVs are the true ones;
  • projects changed: projects in one plan and not the other.



The plan's own gap was $22k. If the NPVs are off by up to 5% at random, the plan leaves $37k to $196k on the table in these three trials, between 1.7 and 9 times its gap, and the best plan for the true numbers differs in 18 to 36 projects. The total NPV itself moves by up to $560k (seed 1: 224,944 against 224,384). Random errors in single projects partly cancel in a sum of hundreds; an error shared by all of them, such as a discount rate that is off, would not cancel.


So the third answer for the board: the last 0.01% of optimality is smaller than what the data can support. Spending eight times the nodes to close a $22k gap buys less than a better forecast would. This does not make gaps irrelevant; it sets their scale. A gap well below the data's error is enough, and the time saved is better spent on the forecast, or on re-solving the scenarios the planners worry about.


Choose the Tolerance in Money, Then Set Both


Putting the lesson together, a tolerance is a decision made with the planners, not a default:


  1. Ask what amount of NPV is too small to matter. A planner's answer is in money: smaller than the smallest project they would argue about, or smaller than the uncertainty in the forecast. Say $50k for GridCap medium.
  2. Set it as absolute_gap_tolerance, in the objective's units (here $k, so 50). It means the same thing whatever constants the objective carries and whatever the portfolio's size.
  3. Set relative_gap_tolerance explicitly too, so that no solver's default decides for you: 0 if the absolute rule should decide alone, or a small share as a second rule.
  4. Add a time limit as a safety net (lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count), and treat a run that stops on it as unsettled: report its plan with its bracket.
  5. Report the bracket in money: the plan's value, the bound, and the tolerance the run used.


With those, the slide would read: "The plan's NPV is $224.38M; no plan within these budgets and rules is worth more than $50k above it (HiGHS, absolute tolerance $50k). The NPV forecasts carry an uncertainty of several hundred thousand dollars." That is a sentence a board can act on.

Summary


  • "Optimal" means optimal within the tolerances the run used. HiGHS's default stopped at a 0.01% gap; SCIP's closed the gap on this model. Name the tolerance in every report.
  • 0.01% is an amount of money, measured on the objective. On GridCap large's $6.0B NPV it is $601k, more than the NPV of most single projects.
  • A constant in the objective shrinks the relative gap without improving the plan. With $20B added, a 0.0021% gap hid a 0.1873% gap on the NPV. Keep constants out, or use an absolute tolerance.
  • Plans within the gap can pick different projects. The second-best plan was $3k worse and swapped three projects. Answer questions about one project with two solves.
  • The forecast's error is larger than the gap. A 5% error in the NPVs cost $37k to $196k against a $22k gap.
  • Choose the tolerance in money, then set both tolerances and a time limit, and report the bracket.

Design Note: A Gap Is a Statement About One Model, With One Data Set


Every guarantee in this module is relative to the model and its numbers. A 0.01% gap proves the plan is nearly the best for these NPVs, these budgets and these rules. It cannot cover a rule that is missing from the model, or a forecast that is wrong. Teams that report gaps well report them next to what they were measured on, and spend their effort where the uncertainty is largest. Section 9 builds the tools for explaining a plan, and section 10 those for numerical trust; both start from this distinction.
