  16. Stopping rules and how a run ended

GridCap's planning job is moving to a nightly schedule. Run to the end, the medium selection MILP took HiGHS 13,930 nodes (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap), and the large portfolio will take longer. The planners need a plan on their desks at 6 a.m., and they have said what "good enough" means to them: a plan within 0.1% of the best, or within $100k of it, whichever comes first. The job needs rules that stop the solver, and it needs to know afterwards which rule stopped it, because a plan proved within 0.1% and a plan that merely ran out of time are not the same thing to hand a planner. GridCap is a fictional utility, and its numbers are teaching data.

This lesson sets each kind of stopping rule on the same model, reads how each run ended, shows which rules give the same answer every run, and checks which solvers accept which rules.

The Model

The project-selection MILP of lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap, unchanged. Only the rules for stopping its solve change in this lesson.

Sets. $P$ the candidate projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, each with a parent $p(c)$.

Parameters. $v_i$, the NPV of project $i$ at its earliest start ($k); $c_{i,y}$, what project $i$ spends in year $y$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$: 1 if project $i$ is in the plan.

Objective and constraints.

On GridCap medium: 400 binary variables and 85 rows. Every run below is HiGHS through MathOpt in OR-Tools 9.15 unless it says otherwise, and every number is what a program printed. Another solver, version or machine can print different ones.

A Solve Stops at the First Limit It Reaches

MathOpt's SolveParameters holds the stopping rules common to every solver. MathOpt passes each one to the solver only if you set it:

ParameterStops whenType
relative_gap_tolerancethe relative gap (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap) is at most thisa fraction: 0.001 is 0.1%
absolute_gap_tolerancethe absolute gap is at most thisthe objective's units ($k here)
time_limitthis much wall-clock time has passeda datetime.timedelta
node_limitthis many branch-and-bound nodes are donea whole number
solution_limitthis many plans have been founda whole number, at least 1

Set several, and the solve stops at whichever is reached first. Left unset, a limit is off, and a gap tolerance falls back to the solver's own default. So with none set, each solver still stops somewhere: at the end of the search, or at a tolerance of its own. HiGHS's are documented as options, mip_rel_gap $= 10^{-4}$ (0.01%) and mip_abs_gap $= 10^{-6}$; lesson What "optimal at 0.01%" means on a $2B portfolio shows the first at work.

A Gap Stop Ends OPTIMAL; Every Other Limit Ends FEASIBLE or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND

The two columns reason and limit hit come from result.termination: reason is termination.reason, and limit hit is termination.limit, printed as - when it is None.

Look at the first three rows. Each run stopped with a gap above zero, $420k, $223k and $99k, and each says OPTIMAL. That is not a bug. MathOpt's documentation of both gap tolerances says the solver may stop once the gap is within the tolerance and return OPTIMAL. So OPTIMAL means "optimal within the gap tolerances in force", not "the bound met the incumbent". The plan is proved to be within the tolerance of the best, which is exactly what the planners asked for.

Every other stopping rule is a limit, and a limit stop ends with one of two reasons:

termination.reasonMeanstermination.limit
OPTIMALthe bracket is within the gap tolerances; the plan is proved good enoughNone
FEASIBLEa limit stopped the run, and it holds a plan, not proved within the toleranceswhich limit: TIME, NODE, SOLUTION, ...
NO_SOLUTION_FOUNDa limit stopped the run before any plan was foundwhich limit
INFEASIBLEthe solver proved that no plan obeys every rowNone

The rows above show the first two: 100 nodes ends FEASIBLE with limit NODE, and one solution ends FEASIBLE with limit SOLUTION. The full list of reasons is in lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick. The full list of limits in OR-Tools 9.15 also has ITERATION, MEMORY, OBJECTIVE, INTERRUPTED and a few more, and one value that says nothing:

UNDETERMINED means the solver does not say which limit it hit; MathOpt's documentation warns that some solvers cannot fill the field in. Here CP-SAT, OR-Tools' constraint solver (section 4), stopped on its solution limit and reported it this way. So code that branches on termination.limit must handle UNDETERMINED, and never assume that a FEASIBLE run ran out of time.

The rule for reading a run, then, has two questions, in this order:

  1. Is there a plan? OPTIMAL and FEASIBLE hold one; NO_SOLUTION_FOUND and INFEASIBLE do not. result.has_primal_feasible_solution() answers it directly.
  2. Is the answer settled? OPTIMAL is settled (within the tolerances), and so is INFEASIBLE (a proof that no plan exists). FEASIBLE and NO_SOLUTION_FOUND are not: more time, nodes or solutions could change them. A dashboard that prints FEASIBLE next to a plan should also print the gap (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap) and the limit that stopped it.

    Read how a run ended

    • OPTIMAL and INFEASIBLE are settled, FEASIBLE holds an unproven plan, NO_SOLUTION_FOUND holds none, and termination.limit names the limit or UNDETERMINED

Problem statement

A port's berth-allocation MILP feeds a dashboard that shows one badge per run. The team wants the badge to answer two questions before anyone looks at the numbers: is there a plan to use, and could more search still change the answer?

Write verdict(termination) in verdict.py. termination is a mathopt.Termination, the termination field of a SolveResult. Return a dict with three keys:

  • "has_plan": True when the reason is OPTIMAL or FEASIBLE, and False for any other reason.
  • "settled": True when the reason is OPTIMAL (the bounds are within the gap tolerances, even if they are not equal) or INFEASIBLE (no plan exists), and False for any other reason.
  • "limit": the name of termination.limit ("TIME", "NODE", "SOLUTION", "UNDETERMINED", ...) when the reason is FEASIBLE or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND, and None for any other reason.

A Limit member's name is its .name attribute: mathopt.Limit.NODE.name is "NODE". Read the reason from termination.reason and compare it with the members of mathopt.TerminationReason.

Each Limit Answers a Different Question


Read the table again as five ways of saying "enough":


  • A relative gap says "enough when no plan is better by more than this share". At 1% the run stopped after a single node, with a plan $193k below the optimum of $224,384k (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap). At 0.1% it also stopped at the first node, with a better plan and a gap of 0.099%. One tolerance serves every model size, which is why it is the rule most jobs set.
  • An absolute gap says "enough when no plan is better by more than this amount", in money. A tolerance of $100k took 471 nodes and stopped at a gap of $99k. It is the rule to use when the planners think in money, and when the objective can be near zero, where a ratio means little.
  • A node limit says "enough work". It bounds the effort, not the quality: after 100 nodes the gap is whatever it happens to be, here $237k. It is the rule for repeatable runs (next section).
  • A solution limit says "any plan, fast". With a limit of one, HiGHS stopped before branching, with a plan $5,263k (2.4%) below its bound. Which plans a solver counts is its own choice: this run returned the 219,607 plan, the one on the R row of lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap's log, not the 41,050 plan on the first row of that log. MathOpt's documentation warns that a solution limit promises nothing about the objective value of the plans returned. Use it to check that a model has any plan at all, or to get a starting point quickly; never hand its plan over as an answer without its gap.
  • A time limit says "enough waiting". How good the plan is when it fires depends on how far the search got in that time, which depends on the machine.

How far off a first plan can be depends on the model. The OR-Tools MathOpt sample time_indexed_scheduling.py schedules jobs on one machine, each job taking one start time on a grid of whole time steps, and minimizes the sum of the jobs' completion times. Adapted here with HiGHS:


Sets. $I$ the jobs; $T = \{0, \dots, H - 1\}$ the time steps, with $H$ the latest release plus the total processing time. Parameters. $p_i$ the processing time and $r_i$ the release time of job $i$. Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$: job $i$ starts at $t$, fixed at 0 for $t < r_i$.



The machine row for time $t$ counts every job that would be running at $t$: one started in the last $p_i$ steps (from $s = \max(0, t - p_i + 1)$). The program solves the sample's own three-job test instance, then 15 random jobs, once to the end and once stopped at the first plan:

For a minimization the bracket is the other way round, bound below and plan above. The full solve needed only one node, and the first plan is one no planner should get.


The gap rules are the ones that say something about the plan; the limits only say how the run ended. A nightly job usually sets one of each kind: a gap tolerance that the planners agreed to, and a time limit as a safety net, so that a hard night still ends with whatever plan was found. When the safety net fires, the result says FEASIBLE and TIME, and the job reports the gap with the plan.

Problem statement


A school-bus routing team replays old solver runs to choose stopping rules for next term. For each past run they kept a trace: the solver's progress at a few moments, oldest first. Each row is a dict with these keys:


  • "seconds": wall-clock time since the start;
  • "nodes": branch-and-bound nodes done;
  • "solutions": plans found so far;
  • "incumbent": the best plan's objective value, or None before the first plan;
  • "bound": the best bound.


The model is a maximization, and every incumbent in a trace is positive.


Write where_it_stops(trace, limits) in first_limit.py. limits holds some of the keys "relative_gap", "absolute_gap", "nodes", "solutions" and "seconds". Return (index, rule): the index of the first row at which a rule in limits is met, and that rule's key. If no rule is met on any row, return (None, None).


A rule is met on a row when:


  • "relative_gap": the row has an incumbent and $(\text{bound} - \text{incumbent}) / \text{incumbent}$ is at most the limit;
  • "absolute_gap": the row has an incumbent and $\text{bound} - \text{incumbent}$ is at most the limit;
  • "nodes", "solutions", "seconds": the row's value is at least the limit.


A row with no incumbent meets no gap rule. When several rules are met on the same row, name the first in this order: "relative_gap", "absolute_gap", "nodes", "solutions", "seconds". A gap rule comes first because a solver that meets one reports the run as optimal.

Time Limits Are Not Repeatable; Node and Solution Limits Are


A time limit stops the search wherever it has got to after so many seconds. The same model on a faster machine, on a busier one, or next to another job gets further or less far in those seconds, so a time-limited run cannot promise the same plan twice. MathOpt's documentation adds that the time limit is not a hard limit: a solve may run slightly past it.


A node limit counts work, not seconds. repeatable.py runs the same 200-node solve twice:






The two runs did the same work and stopped in the same place, with the same plan, though their clocks read differently. MathOpt's documentation of node_limit says that for many solvers it can limit the computation deterministically, sometimes only with further settings such as a single thread. HiGHS here needed none. When another solver's node-limited runs differ, a single thread is the first setting to try; lesson Common vs. solver-specific parameters, and what each solver rejects covers what each solver accepts, and lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs reproducible CP-SAT runs.


Repeatability matters in three places: a test that checks a solve's output (this module's tests use node limits and gap tolerances, never time limits), an audit that must reproduce last quarter's plan from last quarter's data, and a bug report that someone else must be able to run.


Solvers Accept Different Limits


MathOpt passes each parameter to the solver, and a solver that cannot honour one refuses the whole solve. support.py asks each MILP solver for each stopping rule on the small portfolio and reports whether the solve was accepted:



The five rules of this lesson work on HiGHS and SCIP. CP-SAT refuses a node limit, and its solution limit can only be 1. The last three rows are rules this lesson has not used, and they are rarely what a nightly job needs: objective_limit stops as soon as a plan reaches a target value, best_bound_limit as soon as the bound proves a value, and cutoff_limit gives up once no plan can reach a value. None of the three solvers accepts the first two on a MILP in this version.


A refusal is an exception raised by mathopt.solve, not a result. In OR-Tools 9.15 that exception hides its own message: a bug in the Python wrapper turns it into an AttributeError about a missing canonical_code, and the solver's reason is only on the exception's __context__, as the HiGHS refusal in lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand printed. That is why support.py reads it there:



So stopping rules belong to a solver, not to a model. A job that can switch solvers builds its SolveParameters per solver, and its tests solve once with each set.


Summary


  • A solve stops at the first limit it reaches. Gap tolerances, a time limit, a node limit and a solution limit can be combined.
  • A gap stop ends OPTIMAL. OPTIMAL means within the tolerances in force, even with a gap above zero.
  • Every other limit ends FEASIBLE (a plan) or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND (none), with termination.limit saying which limit, or UNDETERMINED when the solver does not say.
  • Each limit answers a different question. Gaps say how good the plan is; node, solution and time limits only say how much was done.
  • Time limits are not repeatable; node limits are, on HiGHS here, and on many solvers with one thread.
  • Solvers accept different limits. CP-SAT refuses a node limit and any solution limit but 1; none of the three takes objective_limit on a MILP.

Design Note: Store the Stopping Rule With the Plan


A plan's value means little without the rules that stopped its run. Save the SolveParameters you passed (tolerances and limits), termination.reason, termination.limit and both bounds next to every plan the job produces. When someone asks why Tuesday's plan differs from Monday's, the first question is whether both were settled, and that record answers it.
