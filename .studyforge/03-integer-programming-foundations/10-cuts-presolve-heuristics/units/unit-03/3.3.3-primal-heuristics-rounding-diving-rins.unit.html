A GridCap planner runs the five-year plan with a 10-second limit before a board meeting. If the search has not finished, she still needs a plan to show, and the best one the solver has. Where does that plan come from, if branch-and-bound has not reached the bottom of its tree? From primal heuristics: fast procedures that try to build good plans from the LP relaxation, alongside the search. This lesson writes four of them, runs them on the five-year model, and compares what each finds with the true optimum. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers are teaching data.

The Model

The five-year selection model of lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first, unchanged; the heuristics only read it.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y = \{2027, \dots, 2031\}$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups.

Parameters. $v_i$ the NPV of project $i$ ($k); $c_{i,y}$ its spend in year $y$ at its earliest start ($k); $B_y$ the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$: 1 if project $i$ is funded.

On GridCap medium its optimum, proved by HiGHS with a zero gap tolerance in lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first, is 274,999, and its LP relaxation bound is 275,164.8. Every heuristic below is measured against those two numbers.

A Heuristic Finds Plans, and the Best One So Far Is the Incumbent

A heuristic is a procedure that usually finds a good answer fast and promises nothing: not that it finds a plan, and not that the plan is optimal. A primal heuristic finds feasible plans ("primal" because a plan is a point of the primal model, as opposed to a bound from the dual side).

The best feasible plan found so far is the incumbent, and its value is the primal bound: the optimum is at least that good. The LP relaxation gives the other side, the dual bound: the optimum is at most that. The gap between them says how far from proved the answer is. Lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap defines it exactly; this lesson uses HiGHS's form, $(\text{dual bound} - \text{incumbent}) / \text{incumbent}$.

Branch-and-bound (lessons Branching on a fractional variable to A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines) needs an incumbent to prune: a node whose LP bound is below the incumbent's value cannot hold a better plan and is dropped. With no incumbent nothing is pruned, so a good plan found early saves search everywhere. That is why solvers run heuristics before the first branch and keep running them during the search.

Rounding Down Keeps a Packing Plan Feasible, Rounding to Nearest Does Not

The LP relaxation's optimum is almost a plan: on the five-year model only five of 400 variables are fractional. The obvious move is to round them. There are two obvious ways:

EPS is $10^{-6}$: an LP solver returns 0.9999999 for a value that is 1 up to its tolerance, and round_down must count it as 1.

Rounding to nearest gives a "plan" worth more than the LP bound, which is impossible for a real plan: it overspends 2028 and 2029. Rounding down is feasible, and that is no luck. The budget and alternative rows have only nonnegative coefficients (rows like that are called packing rows): lowering any variable lowers the left side, so a point that fits keeps fitting. The after rows $x_c - x_{p(c)} \le 0$ survive too: if the parent is fractional, the child is at most that fraction, so both round to 0; if the parent is 1, the child's row allows anything. The must-do variables are already 1. So for this model, rounding down always gives a plan. For a model with $\ge$ rows (a minimum spend per region, say) it can break those, and no single rounding direction is safe.

The price is value: 271,019 is 1.5% below the LP bound. Rounding down dropped five projects, and the money they would have used is now unspent.

A Greedy Fill Spends What Rounding Left Unspent

Repair the rounded plan: go through the projects not funded, best value per $k first, and add each one that keeps the plan feasible.

The weight of a project is its total spend over the five plan years (max(..., 1) keeps a project with no spend in those years from dividing by zero). Every candidate is tried, not only until the first one that does not fit: a large project may not fit in what is left while a small one further down the list does.

Thirteen projects added, among them P137, one of the five that rounding dropped, and the plan is now within 0.27% of the LP bound. The true optimum, 274,999, is 581 above it. Rounding plus a greedy repair costs one LP solve and a pass over the projects, and on this model it gets within a third of a percent.

Problem statement

A district picks transformer replacements under one budget: maximize $\sum_j v_j x_j$ subject to $\sum_j c_j x_j \le B$, $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$, with every $c_j > 0$. A node LP of the search returned a point $x^*$, and the district wants a plan from it now.

Write round_and_fill(values, costs, budget, x) in fill.py. Return the sorted list of the items in the plan built in two steps:

  1. Round down. Keep item $j$ when $x^*_j \ge 1 - 10^{-6}$; drop every other item.
  2. Fill. Go through the items not kept, in order of $v_j / c_j$ from highest to lowest (ties by item name), and add each one whose cost fits in what is left of the budget. An item that does not fit is skipped, and the next one is tried.

The point $x^*$ satisfies the budget row, so the rounded-down plan does too.

Diving Fixes One Variable, Re-Solves the LP, and Repeats


Rounding decides all five fractional variables at once, from one LP point. Diving decides them one at a time and lets the LP react to each decision: fix one variable, solve the LP again with that fixing, look at the new point, fix another. It is a single path down the branch-and-bound tree, taken without ever coming back.


Which variable, and which way? This dive, fractional diving, picks the variable closest to a whole number (the least fractional, $\min(x_j, 1 - x_j)$ smallest) and rounds it to its nearest value: that is the decision the LP is most sure of. If the fixing makes the LP infeasible, it fixes the variable the other way.



Relaxation.solve(fixed) sets the bounds of every fixed variable to its value, every other to $[0, 1]$, solves the LP with GLOP, and returns (None, {}) if the LP has no solution. The dive ends when the LP optimum has no fractional variable left: that point satisfies every row and is 0-1, so it is a plan.



Read three things in it. First, new fractional variables keep appearing: P333 was 0 at the root and is 0.065 at depth 3, because fixing P275 and P152 moved the LP point. That is why the dive takes 123 fixings to settle five fractional variables. Second, the "LP bound" column falls at every step. It is the bound of the LP with the fixings so far, so it bounds only the plans the dive can still reach, not the whole model; once it drops below a known incumbent, the dive cannot beat it and a solver would stop it early. Third, the result: 274,886, better than rounding and filling by 468, at the cost of 124 LP solves instead of one.

Problem statement


A substation-upgrade model has binaries $x_j$, and an LP solver you can call with some of them fixed. Write the dive that turns LP points into a plan.


Write dive(lp) in dive.py. lp is a function: lp(fixed) takes a dict from variable to 0 or 1 and returns the LP optimum with those variables fixed, as a dict from every variable to its value, or None when that LP has no solution. Repeat:


  1. Call lp(fixed), starting with fixed = {}. If the point has no fractional variable (every value within $10^{-6}$ of 0 or 1), return (plan, steps): plan maps each variable to its value rounded to 0 or 1.
  2. Otherwise pick the fractional variable with the smallest $\min(x_j, 1 - x_j)$ (ties by name), and set target to 1 if $x_j \ge 0.5$, else 0.
  3. Call lp with fixed plus $x_j$ = target. If that returns None, use the other value instead. Add the fixing to fixed, record (variable, value) in steps, and go back to step 1 with the point you got.


If both values of a variable give None, return (None, steps). A failed fixing is not a step: steps holds only the fixings that were kept, so the variable whose two values both failed is not in it. The reference solution appends to steps only after a call returns a point. The tests give you an lp that answers only the calls a correct dive makes.

RINS Solves a Small MIP Where the Incumbent and the LP Disagree


Once there is an incumbent, a different idea works. Compare it with the LP optimum. Where both say 1, or both say 0, the decision is probably right. Where they disagree, it is in doubt. RINS (Relaxation Induced Neighborhood Search) fixes every variable on which they agree and solves the MIP that is left, which is small, exactly:



A MIP solved inside a heuristic is a sub-MIP. Its neighbourhood is the set of plans it can reach: those that keep every fixed variable at its value. The incumbent is one of them, so RINS never returns a worse plan. Run from both incumbents found so far:



From the filled plan, the 17 free variables hold no better plan: the incumbent was already the best in its neighbourhood. From the dive's plan, 12 variables are free, and the sub-MIP swaps two decisions for a plan worth 274,922, 36 more. Neither run reaches 274,999: the better plans need changes to variables that both the LP and the incumbent agree on, and RINS never touches those.


The size of the neighbourhood is the dial. Too many free variables and the sub-MIP is as hard as the whole model; too few and it holds nothing new. So a RINS step inside a search should give its sub-MIP a node or time limit, and is worth running only when the incumbent and the LP disagree on enough variables to leave something to find.

Problem statement


A feeder-reinforcement model maximizes $\sum_j v_j x_j$ subject to $\sum_j c_j x_j \le B$ and $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$. The search has an incumbent plan $\hat x$ and an LP optimum $x^*$. Write the RINS step for it.


Write rins(values, costs, budget, incumbent, x) in rins.py. incumbent maps each item to 0 or 1 and x maps it to its LP value.


  1. Fix where they agree. Item $j$ is fixed at $\hat x_j$ when $|x^*_j - \hat x_j| < 10^{-6}$; every other item is free.
  2. Solve the neighbourhood. Over every 0-1 choice of the free items, with the fixed items at their values, keep the choices whose total cost (fixed items included) is at most $B$, and return the sorted list of the items in the most valuable one. On a tie, keep the one found first when the free items, sorted by name, are tried with itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=...).


If no choice fits the budget (it happens only when the items fixed at 1 already cost more than $B$, so the incumbent itself broke the budget), there is no plan in the neighbourhood: return None, not an empty list, since the empty plan is not in the neighbourhood either. The reference solution does exactly that.


The tests have at most four free items, so trying every choice is fast.

Solvers Run Many Heuristics and Say Which One Found Each Plan


The three ideas above have many relatives in every MIP solver: feasibility pump, shifting, local branching, RENS (a cousin of RINS that needs no incumbent: it fixes the variables the LP already has at whole values). They run at the root and at some nodes, and a solver tracks which one found each new incumbent. Switching them off through MathOpt's common heuristics parameter, on SCIP:




The answer is the same, since heuristics never change the optimum, only how soon good plans appear. Without them SCIP needed 1,333 nodes instead of 718 and nearly twice the LP iterations. That is what the pruning rule predicts: with fewer good plans found early, fewer nodes can be dropped.


HiGHS's log names the source of every new incumbent in its first column, with a letter: R for randomized rounding, L for a sub-MIP heuristic (the family RINS belongs to), J for feasibility jump, and so on. On the five-year model, the incumbents HiGHS found before its first branch came from five different sources, among them randomized rounding and sub-MIPs. HiGHS's option list has a switch for several of its heuristics: feasibility jump, RINS and RENS are on by default, ZI Round and shifting off, and mip_heuristic_effort (0.05 by default, on a scale from 0 to 1) sets the effort spent on them. Lesson Reading a full MIP log line by line reads that log line by line.


Summary


  • A heuristic finds plans, and the best one so far is the incumbent. Its value is the primal bound; the LP relaxation's value is the dual bound; the gap between them measures what is left to prove.
  • Rounding down keeps a packing plan feasible, rounding to nearest does not. On the five-year model, nearest overspends two years; down gives 271,019.
  • A greedy fill spends what rounding left unspent: 274,418, within 0.27% of the LP bound.
  • Diving fixes one variable, re-solves the LP, and repeats: 274,886 after 123 fixings.
  • RINS solves a small MIP where the incumbent and the LP disagree: from the dive's plan, 12 free variables give 274,922. It never returns a worse plan.
  • Solvers run many heuristics and say which one found each plan. SCIP without them needed 1,333 nodes instead of 718.

Design Note: Give the Solver Your Own Plan


Planners often have a plan already: last year's portfolio rolled forward, or a ranking a team agreed on. Handing it to the solver as a starting incumbent (a MIP start, or "hint") gives the search a primal bound from the first node and RINS a neighbourhood to search, at no cost. Lesson Re-solving after the data changes covered warm starts for LPs; for a MIP, MathOpt passes such a plan as a solution hint, ModelSolveParameters(solution_hints=[...]), and a solver that accepts only one hint takes the first. HiGHS's documentation describes what it does with a plan supplied through its own interface: it solves the model with the plan's integer values fixed, and a feasible result gives the search its first primal bound. A plan the business already trusts is also a good test: if the solver's answer is worse than it, the model is wrong.
