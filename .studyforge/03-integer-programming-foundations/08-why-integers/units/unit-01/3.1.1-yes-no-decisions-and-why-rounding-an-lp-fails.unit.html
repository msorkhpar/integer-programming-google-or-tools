  16. Yes/no decisions and rounding

GridCap's planning team has 40 candidate projects in its small portfolio and a capital budget of $38,390k. An analyst who has just finished section 2 solves the selection as a linear program, because LPs are fast, and gets back a plan that funds 20 projects whole and 52.2% of a tower set repair. "Round it," says the planning lead, "we'll either do the repair or we won't." This lesson shows what each way of rounding does to that plan, and why the answer is a different kind of model, in five rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. None of its numbers describe a real company.

A Yes/No Decision Is a Variable That May Only Be 0 or 1

A decision variable is a number the solver chooses. A yes/no decision, such as "fund project P022", is a variable $x_{P022}$ that may take only two values: 1 for yes and 0 for no. Such a variable is called binary, and its domain (the set of values it may take) is written

Lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model introduced binaries for GridCap's selection; this lesson looks at what happens when that domain is weakened. Binaries are the most common case of an integer variable, one whose domain is whole numbers. A count, such as "how many crews to hire", is an integer variable with domain $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ and usually an upper bound. A mixed-integer linear program (MILP) is a linear program in which some variables must take whole-number values; lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick met it on GridCap tiny.

In OR-Tools MathOpt, a variable's domain is its lower bound, its upper bound, and whether it must be integer. The selection model of this module builds every $x_i$ in one line, with the integer requirement as a switch:

With is_integer=True, lb=0 and ub=1, the only values left are 0 and 1, which is a binary. With is_integer=False, the same line allows every number from 0 to 1.

Why not simply leave the variable continuous and read "0.7" as "probably yes"? Because a funded fraction has no meaning for the business: GridCap cannot repair 52.2% of a tower set and get 52.2% of its value. The value and the cost of a project arrive whole or not at all. A model that allows a fraction is answering a different question from the one the planner asked.

The LP Relaxation Drops the Whole-Number Rule, and Answers With Fractions

Take a MILP and replace each $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ by $0 \le x_i \le 1$, keeping every other row exactly as it was. The result is a linear program called the model's LP relaxation: "relaxed" because one requirement was loosened, and an LP because every variable is now continuous. Lessons Your first LP: splitting a maintenance budget across programs and Solving a 2-variable LP by picture solve LPs of this kind; GLOP is OR-Tools' own LP solver, and HiGHS solves both LPs and MILPs.

Here is GridCap small's selection model (written out in full in The Model below), relaxed and solved with GLOP, and then solved as the MILP with HiGHS:

The LP funds 20 projects whole and 0.522 of P022, and every other $x_i$ is 0. The comparison > 1 - 1e-6 is there because a solver returns floating-point numbers, so "1" may come back as 0.9999999; section 10 treats these tolerances.

Why does a fraction appear at all? The LP is free to use every last dollar. Once the projects it funds whole leave less room than the next project costs, buying part of that project still adds value, so the LP does. The plan that results is the best point of the relaxation, and lesson Convex sets, polyhedra and extreme points showed that such a best point sits at a corner of the region the rows cut out. Many of those corners have fractional coordinates. Lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap computes this fraction by hand.

The MILP's best plan is worth $25,336k, less than the LP's $25,451.27k. That is no accident, and lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap is about the difference. For now the question is the planning lead's: can we get a plan by rounding the LP's answer instead of solving the MILP?

Rounding Up, or to the Nearest Whole, Can Break the Budget

There are three obvious ways to turn the LP's $x_i$ into 0s and 1s. Rounding up funds every project the LP touched at all. Rounding to the nearest funds a project when its share is 0.5 or more. Rounding down funds only what the LP funded whole. Each is one test on the LP's value:

Each rounded plan is then checked against the model's rows. broken_rows returns the label of every row the plan breaks: budget, must_<id>, parent_<id> or alt_<group>, the same names the model gives its constraints:

P022's share is 0.522, so rounding up and rounding to the nearest both fund it whole. The LP had already spent the budget to the dollar: its 20 whole projects cost $37,060k (the rounded-down spend) and its 0.522 of P022 the remaining $1,330k. Paying for all of P022 instead adds $1,220k, so the plan spends $39,610k against $38,390k. It is worth $26,167k, more than the LP's value, and that is exactly the warning sign: no plan that keeps the budget can be worth more than the LP (lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap proves it), so a rounded plan worth more must break a row.

One portfolio could be bad luck, so the same three roundings ran on 30 GridCap small portfolios, seeds 1 to 30 (a seed is the number the generator starts from; each seed gives a different portfolio of the same size):

Rounding up broke the budget every time. In 3 portfolios it also funded two projects of one alternative group. The row $x_a + x_b \le 1$ lets the LP split the group's one allowed project between two alternatives, say 0.3 of one and 0.7 of the other, and rounding up turns both shares into funded projects. Rounding to the nearest broke the budget 13 times in 30: every share it rounds up adds spend the LP never had. A rounding that breaks the budget in 13 of 30 portfolios is not a planning method.

Rounding Down Keeps This Model's Rows, and Still Misses the Best Plan


Rounding down never broke a row in the 30 runs. That follows from the shape of this model's rows, not from luck:


  • budget: every capex is positive, so lowering any $x_i$ from a fraction to 0 lowers spend. If the LP point was within budget, the rounded-down plan is too.
  • alt: lowering a variable lowers the left side of $\sum_{i \in g} x_i \le 1$.
  • parent: if $x_c \le x_{p(c)}$ holds and both are rounded down, it still holds: a parent below 1 forces its child below 1 too, so both become 0, and a parent at 1 stays 1.
  • must: $x_i = 1$ is already whole, so rounding leaves it alone.


So rounding down gives an allowed plan here. It is not the best plan:



The rounded-down plan leaves $1,330k unspent and is worth $665k less than the optimum. More telling is the last two lines: the optimum is not "the rounded-down plan plus something". It drops two projects the LP funded whole (P008 and P026) to make room for P022 and P029. Rounding looks only at one point, the LP's answer, and the best plan can sit somewhere else entirely. Rounding down keeps $2 of a possible $100. A has the better value per dollar, so the LP takes it first, and B no longer fits whole. Multiply both projects' figures by a thousand and the same thing happens to a real-sized budget.


Rounding down is safe only for rows like these, where lowering a variable can never break them. A ≥ row (a minimum to reach) is the opposite. Suppose a vegetation program must clear at least 400 km of corridor at least cost:



Rounding down drops the 0.67 of D and the plan clears half the requirement. A model with both kinds of row, such as a budget and a minimum spend per region, has no safe direction to round.


The same goes for a $\le$ row with a negative coefficient, and for integer variables that are not yes/no. The OR-Tools documentation's "MIP with arrays" example (its sample mip_var_array.py) has both: five whole-number variables with no upper bound, four $\le$ rows, and two negative coefficients. Adapted to MathOpt, with the integer requirement as a switch as above:







Rounding up breaks three rows and rounding to the nearest breaks one. Rounding down happens to keep every row here, but nothing guaranteed it: $x_4$ has coefficient $-7$ in (c3), so lowering it raises that row's left side, by 3.21, and only the drop from rounding $x_0$ and $x_1$ down kept (c3) within 315. The best whole-number point is worth 260 against round-down's 250, and it uses $x_2$ and $x_3$, which the LP left at 0. The OR-Tools page prints a different point with the same value 260, $(10, 16, 4, 4, 3)$: this model has more than one best plan, and neither is a rounding of the LP's answer.


Rounding Down Then Filling Gives an Allowed Plan, Never a Proof


Rounding down leaves money unspent, so a natural repair is to fill: walk through the projects not yet funded, best value per dollar first, and add each one that fits and keeps every rule.






The filled plan is allowed and worth $25,290k, only $46k below the optimum. A method like this, which builds an allowed plan quickly with no promise that it is the best, is called a heuristic. Lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS shows the heuristics MILP solvers run inside their search.


But notice how we know it is $46k short: the MILP was solved. Without that solve, all the filled plan tells you is that $25,290k is achievable. What it cannot tell you is whether $25,336k, or $27,000k, is. The LP gives half the missing information: its value, $25,451.27k, is a ceiling no plan can pass, so the filled plan is at most $161.27k short. Whether that ceiling is close enough to trust is the subject of lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap, and closing the distance to a proof is what a MILP solver does (lessons Branching on a fractional variable to A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines).

Problem statement


The water utility's analysts keep a fallback for the day the MILP solve is unavailable: round the LP relaxation down, then spend what is left. They want it written once and tested.


Write round_down_and_fill(shares, projects, budget) in fill.py. shares maps each project id to its value $x_i$ in the LP relaxation's answer ($0 \le x_i \le 1$). The function returns the set of funded ids, built in two steps:


  1. Round down. Keep every project whose share is 1. A solver returns floating-point numbers, so treat any share greater than $1 - 10^{-6}$ as 1.
  2. Fill. Walk through all projects in decreasing order of $\text{value}_i / \text{cost}_i$, once. Add a project not yet in the plan if all three hold: the plan's cost stays $\le B$; its parent, if it has one, is already in the plan; and no other project of its group, if it has one, is already in the plan.


The result keeps every row of the model in the Name the rows a rounded plan breaks practice: the LP's whole projects already did, and each added project is checked. Must-do projects come back at 1 from the LP, so step 1 keeps them. Nothing here proves the plan is the best one.

Summary


  • A yes/no decision is a variable that may only be 0 or 1. In MathOpt that is lb=0, ub=1, is_integer=True; a fraction of a project has no business meaning.
  • The LP relaxation drops the whole-number rule and answers with fractions. On GridCap small it funds 0.522 of P022.
  • Rounding up, or to the nearest whole, can break the budget. Rounding up broke it on all 30 small portfolios tried; rounding to the nearest on 13.
  • Rounding down keeps this model's rows and still misses the best plan. It is safe only for rows that lowering a variable cannot break, loses 3.1% on average here, and can lose almost everything; the best plan may share little with the LP's answer.
  • Rounding down then filling gives an allowed plan, never a proof. Only a bound, or a MILP solve, says how far from the best it is.

Design Note: Solve the MILP, Keep the Heuristic


A team that rounds an LP usually does it because the MILP "might be slow". On GridCap small the MILP is solved by HiGHS at the first node of its search (lesson Why MILP is hard: combinatorial growth in numbers counts the nodes), and the cost of rounding is a plan that either breaks the budget or leaves value behind with no one knowing how much. The defensible order is the reverse: solve the MILP, and keep a heuristic such as round-down-and-fill as a fallback that is always allowed, reported together with the LP bound so the reader knows how far from the best it can be.
