  16. Why MILP is hard

GridCap's IT lead has a capacity question before the planning tool goes to the medium portfolio: "The small model solved instantly. Medium has ten times the projects. Will it take ten times as long? A hundred? Should we buy a bigger server?" Lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model showed that brute force doubles with every project. This lesson puts numbers on the rest of the story: how many plans there really are, how a search with a bound avoids almost all of them, and why the size of a model is a poor guide to how hard it is, in five rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course. None of its numbers describe a real company.

Plans Double With Every Project, and the Rules Thin Them but Do Not Tame Them

Each yes/no decision has two values, so $n$ binaries have $2 \times 2 \times \dots \times 2 = 2^n$ combinations: every project added doubles the count. Lesson Decisions, objectives and constraints: turning a planner's question into a model turned that into years of brute force. A fair objection is that most combinations break a rule, so perhaps the plans worth checking are few. Count them:

On tiny every one of the 256 combinations was checked with broken_rows (lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails), and 32 keep every rule: the rules removed seven in eight. On small and medium, checking every combination is out of the question, so the program counts a part of them it can count exactly. A free project is one that no rule but the budget touches: not must-do, without a parent, in no alternative group. Fund the must-dos, spend the rest of the budget on any set of free projects that fits, fund nothing else, and every rule holds. So the number of such sets is a lower bound on the number of allowed plans; the true number is larger.

Even this lower bound is enormous: almost 900 million allowed plans on small, and on medium a number with 93 digits. (2.56e+92 is scientific notation: 2.56 times ten to the power 92.) The rules thin the plans by a large factor, but a large factor of $2^{400}$ is still astronomically many. No rule set that leaves planners real choices makes enumeration practical.

Problem statement

A port authority wants to know how many berth-repair plans its rules leave open, to judge whether checking them one by one is realistic. Each candidate is a Project with a cost ($k), a must_do flag, an optional parent and an optional group of alternatives.

Write two functions in count.py. A plan is a set of project ids, and it is allowed when it keeps every row of the selection model, with $x_i = 1$ for each id in the plan:

  • allowed(plan, projects, budget) returns whether one plan keeps all four kinds of row.
  • count_allowed(projects, budget) checks every one of the $2^n$ subsets of the project ids, from the empty set to the full set, and returns how many are allowed.

itertools.combinations(ids, k) gives every subset of size k. The tests use at most five projects, so checking every subset is fast. About fifteen lines.

A Table Over Budget Amounts Counts Plans Without Listing Them


How did the program count 900 million plans without listing them? It never looks at a plan. It keeps a table, ways, with one entry for every budget amount from 0 to $B$: ways[b] is the number of sets of the projects seen so far that cost exactly $b$.




Before any project, only $0 can be spent, in one way: the empty plan. Then each project of cost $c$ is added in turn. Every set that costs $b$ either leaves the new project out (there are ways[b] of those, already counted) or includes it, and then the rest costs $b - c$ (there are ways[b - c] of those). So ways[b] += ways[b - c]. The loop runs downward over $b$ so that ways[b - c] still holds the count from before this project; running upward would let a project join a set that already contains it. On three projects costing 2, 3 and 4 with a budget of 6:



The six plans are $\{\}, \{2\}, \{3\}, \{4\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,4\}$; $\{3, 4\}$ costs 7 and is out. The answer is the sum of the last row. GridCap's costs are all multiples of $10k, so the program divides every cost and the budget by their greatest common divisor first, which makes the table ten times shorter.


This method is called dynamic programming: build the answer for many sub-budgets from answers already computed. Its work is one step per project per budget amount, $n \times B$ steps, about 9.6 million for medium's 307 free projects and 31,156 budget steps. That is fast here, and it is not a way around the exponential growth of the next rule. $B$ is a number, and the work grows with its size, not with the number of digits it takes to write it down. A budget written in dollars instead of $10k units makes the table ten thousand times longer; costs with cents make it longer again. An algorithm whose work grows with the size of the numbers in its input, rather than with their digits, is called pseudo-polynomial. The same table can also find the best plan under one budget, but it breaks down once the model has two budgets (the table needs one axis per budget) or many other rows.

Exponential Growth Outruns Every Polynomial


An algorithm's running time is how its work grows with the size of its input, $n$. When the work grows like a fixed power of $n$, such as $n^2$ or $n^3$, it is polynomial; when it grows like $2^n$, it is exponential. The difference is not a constant factor:



At $n = 10$, $n^3$ and $2^n$ are about equal. At $n = 400$, $n^3$ is 64 million, a fraction of a second for a computer, and $2^n$ has 121 digits. Doubling $n$ multiplies $n^3$ by 8; it squares $2^n$.


This is the heart of "why MILP is hard". Linear programs can be solved in polynomial time: there are algorithms (the interior point methods of lesson Simplex, interior point and PDLP: GLOP, HiGHS, PDLP) whose work is bounded by a polynomial in the model's size for every LP. For integer programs, no such algorithm is known. Computer science classifies the 0/1 knapsack and integer programming in general as NP-hard: a polynomial-time method for them would also give one for a large family of problems that have resisted every attempt for decades, and most researchers believe none exists. So no MILP solver can promise to finish every model of 400 binaries quickly.


That is a statement about the worst case. It does not say GridCap medium is slow, and the next two rules show why it is not.


A Search Tree With a Bound Visits a Sliver of the Plans


Picture the plans as a tree. The top node has decided nothing. Its two children decide the first project: fund it, or not. Each of theirs decides the second, and so on; after $n$ levels each of the $2^n$ bottom nodes is one complete plan, and the tree has $2^{n+1} - 1$ nodes in all. Brute force visits every node.


A search can skip a node, and everything below it, when it can prove nothing below it beats the best plan found so far. Lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap provides the proof: the LP bound of the projects not yet decided, computed by hand, is a ceiling on any completion of the node. If the value so far plus that ceiling is no more than the best plan already found, the whole subtree is skipped:




The program counts the nodes visited on the first $n$ projects of GridCap medium with a budget of half their cost, three ways: the full tree, a search that only skips projects that do not fit, and the search with the bound:



The budget check alone saves about half. The bound saves almost everything: 114 nodes instead of two million at $n = 20$, and 5,862 nodes for all 400 projects, against a tree with 121 digits. (The budget-only search is not run beyond 25 projects: its count roughly doubles with each project, from 1.1 million at 20 to 36 million at 25.) This search is the core of branch-and-bound, the method MILP solvers such as HiGHS are built on; lessons Branching on a fractional variable to A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines build it properly, and this lesson only counts its nodes.


Size Does Not Decide Difficulty; the Bound's Strength Does


The bound did the work above because GridCap's projects differ in value per dollar, so the LP ceiling quickly shows that most branches cannot win. Give the same search the same costs but make every project worth exactly its cost, so all ratios are equal, and use a budget no plan can spend exactly:



Same sizes, same costs, and the search now visits 16.8 million nodes at $n = 25$ instead of 106. With equal ratios the ceiling at a node is roughly "everything that is left fills the budget", which is almost always above the best plan, so nothing is skipped. The difficulty is in the data, not the count of variables.


A real solver shows the same thing. HiGHS reports how many nodes its search explored in result.solve_stats.node_count. Compare GridCap's selection with an invented puzzle, not GridCap data: pick projects so that each of several spend columns comes to exactly half its total. Each column is one equality row:




Both kinds of model were given a limit of 100,000 nodes:



GridCap medium, 400 binaries, is solved at the first node: its LP bound is within 0.006% of the best plan (lesson The integrality gap, measured on GridCap), and HiGHS closed that last sliver without branching at all. Lessons Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand to Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS show the techniques solvers use at a node for that: cuts, presolve and heuristics. The split puzzle with 30 binaries and 4 rows explored 100,000 nodes and stopped with neither a plan nor a proof that none exists. INFEASIBLE on the smaller puzzles is a proof that no exact split exists, and proving it took 20 and then 3,584 nodes.


Size is a poor guide in the other direction too. The OR-Tools "MIP with arrays" example of lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails has only five integer variables, and neither solver proves its optimum at the first node (mip_arrays.py prints both counts):



The OR-Tools page reports 13 branch-and-bound nodes for its own run, with SCIP through the older linear solver wrapper. Three runs, three counts: a node count belongs to one solver, version and setting, not to the model, which is one more reason to measure rather than predict.


Why is the puzzle so hard? Its LP relaxation is nearly useless. Setting every $x_j$ to 0.5 puts each row at half its total, within half a unit of its target, and a small shift in one variable closes that. So the relaxation is satisfied easily at almost every node, and the bound rules out almost nothing until most variables are fixed. The puzzle has no objective, so there is no value bound to prune with either. That is the pattern to remember: a MILP is hard when its LP relaxation is weak, and size matters much less.


So the answer for the IT lead: model size alone does not predict solve time. Measure on realistic instances (section 11 does it on GridCap large), and keep the formulation strong (section 6), because the bound is what makes the search short.


The Model


Two models appear in this lesson. The first is GridCap's selection model of lesson Yes/no decisions, and why rounding an LP fails, solved in the last rule; every count in this lesson is a count of its plans or of its search.


Sets. $P$ the projects; $M \subseteq P$ must-do; $C \subseteq P$ children with parent $p(c)$; $\mathcal{G}$ alternative groups. Parameters. $\text{capex}_i$, $\text{npv}_i$ ($k); $B$, the capital budget ($k). Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for each $i \in P$.



The second is the invented exact-split puzzle. Sets. $J$ the items, $R$ the spend columns. Parameters. $a_{r,j}$, the spend of item $j$ in column $r$ (whole numbers from 0 to 99). Variables. $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$: item $j$ is picked. Objective. None: any plan that keeps the rows will do (a feasibility problem). Constraints.



Summary


  • Plans double with every project, and the rules thin them but do not tame them. GridCap small has at least 8.94e+08 allowed plans and medium at least 2.56e+92.
  • A table over budget amounts counts plans without listing them. Dynamic programming takes $n \times B$ steps: pseudo-polynomial, fast for small integer budgets and no escape from exponential growth.
  • Exponential growth outruns every polynomial. LPs are solvable in polynomial time; MILPs are NP-hard, so no solver can promise to be fast on every model.
  • A search tree with a bound visits a sliver of the plans. 5,862 nodes for 400 GridCap projects, against a tree of $2^{401} - 1$.
  • Size does not decide difficulty; the bound's strength does. GridCap medium (400 binaries) is solved at the first node; a 30-binary puzzle with a weak relaxation is not solved in 100,000.

Design Note: Benchmark on Your Data, Not on Your Size


"How long will 400 projects take?" has no answer in general: GridCap medium needs one node and a 30-binary puzzle more than 100,000. The question that has an answer is "how long does this model take on these instances", and the way to answer it is to run it on realistic data, several seeds, and to keep the formulation's bound strong. Section 5's benchmarking lesson (Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean) and section 11 make that a method.
