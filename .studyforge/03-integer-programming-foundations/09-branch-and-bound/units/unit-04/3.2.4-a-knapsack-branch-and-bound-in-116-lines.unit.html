  15. A knapsack branch-and-bound

The search of lessons Branching on a fractional variable to Node and variable selection called GLOP at every node. That made it general (any rows at all), at the price of an LP solve through MathOpt for every node, hundreds of them on small. GridCap's planners often start from the simplest question the capital plan asks: with the must-do projects paid for, which other projects give the most value for the budget that is left? That question has one row, the budget, and an LP with one row needs no LP solver. It is solved by sorting.

This lesson builds a complete branch-and-bound for that one-row model in plain Python: the bound in about 25 lines, the search in about 25 more, and the whole file, with its data classes, a brute force check and the GridCap loader, in 116 lines (knapsack.py). The two small programs that run it and trace it, knapsack_run.py and knapsack_trace.py, add 27 more: 143 lines in all, inside the title's 150. It then checks the result against brute force and HiGHS, and solves the medium portfolio's 361 discretionary projects in 743 nodes.

The Model

The budget-only selection, a 0-1 knapsack: items with a value and a cost, a capacity, and the question of which items to take. The name comes from packing a bag.

Sets. $P$, the projects; $M \subseteq P$, the must-do projects; $D = P \setminus M$, the discretionary projects.

Parameters. $v_i$, the value of project $i$ ($k); $c_i > 0$, its capex ($k); $B$, the capital budget ($k); $B' = B - \sum_{i \in M} c_i$, the budget left after the must-do projects.

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in D$: 1 if project $i$ is funded. Must-do projects are funded by definition and get no variable.

Objective and constraint.

This model drops the parent and alternative-group rows of lesson Branching on a fractional variable. Dropping a row is a relaxation (lesson Branching on a fractional variable, first rule), so on the same portfolio this model's optimum is at least the full model's, and its plan may break the dropped rules. The full model needs the GLOP-based search of lessons Branching on a fractional variable to Node and variable selection; this lesson's point is the engine. In code, the must-do projects are paid for before the search starts:

A One-Row LP Is Solved by Sorting: Dantzig's Bound

Sort the items by ratio, value per dollar $r_i = v_i / c_i$, best first. Then fill the budget in that order: take each item whole while it fits, and take the fraction of the first item that does not fit that uses up what is left. Stop there. The value of that fill is the LP relaxation's optimum, and it is called Dantzig's bound, after George Dantzig, whose simplex method lesson Simplex as walking between vertices (a tiny tableau implementation) built.

Why is the fill optimal for the LP? Every dollar of budget buys value at the rate of the item it is spent on. Suppose some LP solution spends a dollar on an item with a lower ratio while an item with a higher ratio is not fully taken. Move that dollar to the higher-ratio item: the cost is the same and the value rises (or stays the same, on a tie). Repeat until no such move is left, and you have the sorted fill. So no LP solution beats the fill, and since the fill obeys the row and the bounds $0 \le x_i \le 1$, it is the LP optimum. At most one item, the split item, ends up at a fraction.

On GridCap small, after the four must-do projects are paid for:

Seventeen projects fit, and the eighteenth, P022, fits only 12.2% of the way. That fraction is the LP's fractional variable, and it is the only one: a one-row LP has a single split item. So in this search the variable rule of lesson Node and variable selection has nothing to choose. The split item is the branching variable.

Problem statement

A substation team picks spare transformers to stock: each candidate has a value (avoided outage cost) and a price, and one budget covers them all. Their branch-and-bound needs the LP bound at a node without calling an LP solver.

Write bound(items, budget, fixed) in bound.py. items is a list of (name, value, cost) tuples with every cost above 0, in any order. fixed maps some names to 0 or 1. The node's LP is

Compute it with Dantzig's fill: pay for the items fixed at 1; skip the items fixed at 0; then go through the free items by ratio $v_i / c_i$, highest first (ties by name), taking each one whole while it fits, and taking the fraction of the first one that does not fit that uses up what is left. Stop there.

Return (value, split): the LP value, and the name of the item taken in part, or None when no item is taken in part. Return None instead of a pair when the items fixed at 1 cost more than the budget.

A budget used up exactly is not a split. When the whole items leave exactly 0 of the budget, the next item would be taken to the fraction 0, which is not taking it at all, so the reference returns (value, None). Fixings that cost exactly the budget are allowed too: they leave 0, not less, so the node is feasible and its bound is the fixed items' value.

Fixing an Item Changes the Budget, Not the Order


A node of the search has some items fixed at 0 and some at 1. Its LP is the same fill with two changes: the items fixed at 1 are paid for first (whatever their ratio), and the items fixed at 0 are skipped. The free items keep their ratio order, so the list is sorted once, at the start, and never again.



Relaxed holds the LP value, the items' values in the LP solution, and the split item, or None when the solution is whole. Three cases end the loop. If the items fixed at 1 already cost more than the budget, the node is infeasible and the function returns None. If an item does not fit and some budget is left, that item is the split. If an item does not fit and the budget is exactly used up (share is 0), or every free item fit, the LP solution is whole: an integral node.


Is this really the LP? GLOP can say. The same knapsack as a MathOpt LP (knapsack_model in selection.py), solved at the root and at the two children of the split item:






The same three values to the last digit printed, from a loop over a sorted list instead of an LP solver. With $n$ items the fill takes at most $n$ steps, after one sort.


The Search Is a Stack of Fixings


With the bound in hand, the search is lessons Branching on a fractional variable and Bounds, incumbents and pruning put together: depth-first (a stack, lesson Node and variable selection), branching on the split item, and the three prunes.



Line by line against the earlier lessons:


  • A node is a dict of fixings, as in lesson Branching on a fractional variable; the root fixes nothing.
  • result.value is the incumbent, starting at $-1$ so that any plan, even one worth 0, beats it.
  • The bound test rounds the LP value down, because GridCap's values are whole $k (lesson Bounds, incumbents and pruning). The 1e-9 keeps a value such as 3,021.0000000002 from rounding below 3,021 by accident.
  • The integral case takes the LP solution as the new plan. It is always better than the incumbent here, because the bound test ran first.
  • The up child is pushed last, so it is popped first: the search dives by taking the split item.


The log lines are only for printing. On tiny, with P002 (must-do) paid for first, the whole search is nine nodes:



At the root, P007 and P004 fit (cost 3,290), leaving 1,370, and P001 takes 1,370 / 2,150 = 0.637 of itself. Node 2 takes P001 whole, which pushes P004 out to a fraction. Node 3 takes both: 2,150 + 3,000 is over 4,660, infeasible. Node 4 skips P004 and the fill is whole: P007, P001, P006 and P005 cost exactly 4,660, worth 3,021, the first incumbent. On the other side, P001 = 0, the bound starts above 3,021 (3,252.2) and falls below it after two more splits, so nodes 8 and 9 are pruned by bound.


With must-do P002's 871 added, the plan is worth 3,892, more than the full model's 3,627 from lesson Branching on a fractional variable. That is the relaxation at work: this plan funds both P006 and P007, which are alternatives, and the knapsack has no row to stop it.

A search that prunes wrongly still returns a plan, just not the best one, so nothing crashes to warn you. Two independent checks catch it. Brute force tries all $2^n$ plans and is exact for small $n$:



Each mask from 0 to $2^n - 1$ is a plan: bit $k$ says whether item $k$ is taken. For larger $n$, HiGHS solves the same knapsack as a MILP. On the three GridCap sizes:



The medium portfolio's 361 discretionary projects have $2^{361}$ plans, a number with 109 digits, and the search proves the best one in 743 nodes. The bound is very tight on medium: with many small projects, the split item is a small part of the budget, so the LP value is close to the best whole plan (the root bound is 203,114.25 against the optimum 203,107). Close bounds prune early. On small, 11 plans were found, each better than the last, before the final one was proven.

Summary


  • A one-row LP is solved by sorting: Dantzig's bound. Fill the budget in ratio order and take a fraction of the first item that does not fit; the exchange argument proves it optimal.
  • Fixing an item changes the budget, not the order. Items fixed at 1 are paid first, items fixed at 0 are skipped, and the one sort at the start serves every node.
  • The search is a stack of fixings, branching on the one split item, with the three prunes of lesson Bounds, incumbents and pruning and the bound rounded down for whole-dollar values.
  • Check the search against brute force and against HiGHS, on many instances: a wrong prune returns a worse plan without any error.

Design Note: What a MILP Solver Adds to These 116 Lines


This search and HiGHS run the same algorithm at heart: an LP bound, branching on a fractional variable, pruning by infeasibility, integrality and bound. A production MILP solver adds four layers on top, each the subject of a later lesson. Presolve shrinks the model before the search (lesson Presolve: what the solver removes first). Cutting planes add rows that cut off fractional LP points without losing a whole plan, so bounds fall faster (lesson Cutting planes: Gomory and cover cuts by hand). Primal heuristics find good plans early, as challenge 2 starts to do (lesson Primal heuristics: rounding, diving, RINS). And gap limits stop the search when the plan is provably close enough (lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count). Building the bare search first is what makes those layers readable: each one makes a part of this loop prune sooner.
