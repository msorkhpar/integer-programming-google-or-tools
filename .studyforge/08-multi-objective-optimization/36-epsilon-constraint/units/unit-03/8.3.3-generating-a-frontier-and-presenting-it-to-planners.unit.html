Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points left GridCap with 65 Pareto-optimal plans between the NPV plan and the risk plan. The planning director has twenty minutes in the Pre-Opt review (lesson The planning cycle: Initial, Pre-Opt, Post-Opt, Budget Lock) and asks for one page: "How much NPV does each extra step of risk reduction cost us, and where does it stop being worth it?" Sixty-five rows of numbers do not answer that, and neither does a chart nobody can quote in the minutes. This lesson turns a generated frontier into a page a planner can act on, in five rules: generate it with a check that every point is non-dominated, show a short table, state each step as a price, mark the knees, and say it in one sentence.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its assets, projects and budgets describe no real company.

Generate the Frontier Once, and Check That No Point Dominates Another

The generator is exact_frontier from lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points, on the model of lesson The ε-constraint method with the roles that make it exact: risk removed is maximized, and NPV, whose values are whole $k, is the floor, stepped by 1. Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points lists the conditions for exactness in full; the one that depends on the data, a reward smaller than the closest gap in risk between neighbouring plans, was checked there for this frontier.

The model solved at each floor $v$ (lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points's AUGMECON problem with the roles swapped):

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years of project $i$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ (whole $k) and $\text{rr}_{i,t}$ ($k) as in lesson The ε-constraint method; $c_{i,k}$ and $B_y$, cost profile and yearly budget ($k); $v$, the NPV floor ($k); $r_2$, the NPV range of the payoff table ($k); $\rho = 10^{-3}$, the weight of the slack reward (lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$; slack $s \ge 0$ ($k of NPV above the floor).

Objective.

Constraints.

Generate once, and keep the result: every table, price and knee below is read from the same list of points, with no further solve.

A frontier that goes to a planner must be checked before it is read. The defining property is that no point dominates another, and it is cheap to test: compare every pair.

Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points's run printed pairs where one plan dominates another: 0 for these 65 plans, and the module's tests assert it. The check has to forgive solver noise: dominates counts two values as equal when they are within SAME ($10^{-3}$, one dollar), because the same plan solved twice can come back with an NPV of 17,064 and 17,064.00001. Without that, a plan would "dominate" itself.

The library takes any MathOpt model and any two objectives, both maximized; nothing in it names GridCap. Lesson Parametric analysis: the budget-value frontier (Sensitivity for MIP) builds a budget-value frontier, and passes value as the first objective and minus the budget as the second.

    A frontier generator for library upgrades

    • a generator that steps a whole-number floor and breaks ties returns every Pareto score once
    • a frontier is checked by comparing every pair before anyone reads it

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A county library system has a budget for branch upgrades. Each upgrade adds opening hours per week and reading-room seats, both whole numbers, and has a cost. The board wants to see every choice worth considering: the full list of (hours, seats) scores that no other affordable choice beats on both or ties on one and beats on the other.

Write frontier(upgrades, budget) in libraries.py. It returns every Pareto-optimal score as a list of (hours, seats) pairs, sorted by hours (fewest first), each listed once. The starter builds the model, its budget row and the two objective expressions; you write the generator. Use any method from the lesson; whichever you pick, make sure a tie on one objective cannot leave a dominated score in the list.

If you pick AUGMECON (a seats floor with a slack, and a reward $(\rho / r_2) \cdot s$ on the slack, as in lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points), you need $r_2$. A lexicographic payoff table gives the exact range of seats, which bounds every slack when the first floor is the seats of the most-hours end, as in lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points. Any number at least as large as every slack your sweep can produce works as well, for example the total seats of all upgrades, which also covers a sweep that starts its floor at 0. With such an $r_2$, $s / r_2 \le 1$, so the reward is at most $\rho$ ($10^{-3}$ in the lesson), less than one hour; hours are whole numbers, so the reward can never pay for an hour and only chooses among choices tied on hours. Too small an $r_2$ breaks that: the reward can then outweigh an hour and trade hours for seats. The reference needs no $r_2$: at each floor it maximizes hours, then holds the hours and maximizes seats, two solves per floor.

Write down first what you solve at each floor (objective, floor row, any tie-breaking), and why it cannot miss a score. The tests list every affordable choice by hand and check your list against it, including that no score in it dominates another. About fifteen lines.

Show a Short Table, Each Row a Plan the Planner Could Pick


Sixty-five rows are too many for a review, and picking every fifth row hides the rows that matter. Choose rows by what a planner would ask about: the two ends, the best plan at round risk targets (every $1,000k), and the knees (defined below). Each row is a real plan, so each can be opened project by project. The target lookup needs no solve: among the points that reach the target, take the one with the most NPV.




How to read a row. "NPV given up" is against the NPV plan. "vs max NPV plan" counts projects added (+), dropped (−) and moved to another start year (~); a planner reads it as how much of the familiar plan changes. "weighted sum finds it" says whether the plan is supported (lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points): four of the round-target rows are not, so a tool built on weighted sums would never have shown them.


Two things in the table deserve a sentence in the review. The $13,000k row, lesson The ε-constraint method's answer to the board, keeps 23 of the NPV plan's 27 projects but moves 10 of them to another start year, so much of the change is timing, not selection. And the last row: the final $532k of risk (from $15,554k to $16,086k) costs more NPV ($3,984k) than all the steps before it together ($1,390k).


State Each Step as a Price: NPV Given Up per Dollar of Risk Removed


A planner compares options by what the next one costs. Between two plans on the frontier, the price of moving from the first to the second is the NPV given up divided by the risk gained:




Walk the steps between consecutive supported plans, the hull, not between all 65:



Along the hull the price only rises. That is what being the upper-right convex hull means: walking it toward more risk, each segment gives up at least as much NPV per dollar of risk as the one before it. Along all 65 plans it does not: 29 of the 64 steps are cheaper than the step before, because an unsupported plan sits in a dent, and the step into a dent is cheap while the step out of it is dear. A price list built from all 65 points would zig-zag and confuse the reader. Quote the hull's prices, and show the unsupported plans as options between them.


The prices are the exchange rates that weights imply (lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean), read off the frontier instead of chosen in advance. A weighted sum that values a dollar of risk at $w$ dollars of NPV, $\max\, \text{NPV} + w \cdot \text{risk}$, gains $w \cdot \text{gain} - \text{cost}$ by a step, so it takes every hull step priced below $w$ and stops where the next step costs more.


Mind the direction when you compare these prices with lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean's. There, the price $p = w_N / w_R$ is in $k of risk removed per $1k of NPV: how much risk a dollar of NPV is worth. Here a price is in dollars of NPV given up per dollar of risk removed: what a dollar of risk costs. One is the reciprocal of the other, and the $w$ above is $1/p$. Problem statement


A state forestry agency has generated the frontier of its wildfire fuel-reduction plans: each plan is scored (homes, left), the homes protected and the budget left unspent ($k), and both are worth having. The agency's board will not pay more than max_price $k of unspent budget per extra home protected, for any step.


Write go_until(points, max_price) in wildfire.py. Starting from the plan with the most budget left, walk toward more homes along the supported plans only (the starter's upper_hull returns them, fewest homes first), and take each step whose price, $k given up per extra home, is at most max_price. Stop before the first step that costs more. Return the plan you stop at.


upper_hull keeps a plan that lies exactly on the straight segment between two others: a weighting that ties the two ends ties it too, so it is supported (lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points's definition). The two steps into and out of it have the same price, so the walk takes both or neither.


The tests use their own frontiers, with unsupported plans among the points. About six lines.

A Knee Is Where the Price Jumps


A knee of a frontier is a plan where the price of the next step jumps: before it, risk is cheap; after it, dear. This course defines a knee as a supported plan where the price of the step after it is at least twice the price of the step before it (the factor is a parameter, and two is a judgment, not a standard):




Three knees, and they are not equal. At the first two the price doubles or so, from cents to cents. At the third it multiplies by more than six, from $0.66 to $4.19 of NPV per dollar of risk. That is the knee the director asked about: past $15,554k of risk removed, each further dollar of risk costs several dollars of NPV.


A knee is a place to look, not an answer. Whether $4.19 is too much depends on what a dollar of discounted failure cost is worth to GridCap, which is the swing-weighting question of lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in. What the frontier adds is that the question can now be asked about one specific step, with both plans on the table.

Design Note: Why No Chart


A frontier chart is the natural picture, and a learner who runs the programs can draw one in a few lines. But a chart is hard to quote: the review's minutes, a rate-case filing and an email to finance all carry text and tables. A chart also invites reading between the dots, and between two Pareto plans there is no plan; the next option is the next dot. The table lists only plans that exist, the prices put the slope into numbers, and the knees say where to look. Draw the chart for yourself, and hand the planners the table and the sentence.
