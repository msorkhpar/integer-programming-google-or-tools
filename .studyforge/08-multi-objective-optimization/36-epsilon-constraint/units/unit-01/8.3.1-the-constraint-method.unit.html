GridCap's planners have two plans from lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year. The NPV plan earns $24,260k of NPV and removes $10,577k of discounted failure risk; the risk plan removes $16,086k and earns $18,886k. Now the board brings a question in its own words: "We told the regulator we would remove at least $13 million of failure risk over the window. What is the most NPV we can keep while doing that?"

A weighted sum (lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean) cannot take that question as asked. It wants a price, so many dollars of NPV per dollar of risk, and then picks whatever plan that price favors, which may remove $11 million or $15 million. The board did not name a price. It named a floor. The ε-constraint method turns the question into a model directly: keep one objective as the objective, and make the other a constraint with a floor $\varepsilon$ (the Greek letter epsilon, by tradition). This lesson builds it on GridCap small in five rules.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its assets, projects and budgets describe no real company.

Keep One Objective, and Turn the Other Into a Floor

The two objectives are lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's: the plan's NPV, $\sum \text{npv}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$, and the risk it removes, $\sum \text{rr}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$. The ε-constraint problem for the board's question maximizes NPV and adds one row: risk removed must be at least $\varepsilon$. Everything else is the start-year model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years, unchanged.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k, whole numbers); $\text{rr}_{i,t}$, the discounted risk it removes over the ten-year window if started in $t$ ($k, lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $\varepsilon$, the least risk the plan must remove ($k).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$.

Constraints.

The module's objectives.py builds the rows of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years and sets no objective; it hands back the two objectives as MathOpt linear expressions, two.npv and two.risk. (MathOpt is the OR-Tools modeling layer of section 4: add_linear_constraint adds a row, maximize sets the objective, and solve runs a solver, here HiGHS at a zero gap.) The ε row is then one line:

That is the board's answer: keeping the $13 million promise costs $445k of NPV against the NPV plan ($24,260k − $23,815k). Notice that the plan removes $221k more than it was asked to. The floor is a minimum, not a target. Projects come whole, so the best plan above $13,000k need not land on it.

Which objective becomes the floor is a modeling choice. Put the one the stakeholder states as a commitment ("at least $13M of risk", "no less than 95% of the best NPV") in the floor, and the one they want as much of as possible in the objective.

The Payoff Table Sets the Range of ε

Not every $\varepsilon$ is worth trying. Solve each objective alone first. The two plans, each scored on both objectives, form the payoff table:

The table fixes the useful range of the floor, from the NPV plan's risk ($10,577k) to the most risk any plan can remove ($16,086k):

  • Below the range the floor does nothing. At $8,000k the NPV plan already clears it, so the answer is the NPV plan.
  • Inside the range each floor gives a plan between the two ends (at $14,000k: NPV $23,670k).
  • At the top the floor forces the risk plan itself.
  • Above the range no plan can remove that much risk. The solve finds no plan, and the function returns None instead of a plan:

The row is added, the model is solved, and the row is deleted again with delete_linear_constraint, so one model serves every floor. The function is written for any two objectives, both maximized: first is the one the solve maximizes, second the one held at the floor. Every Answer Is at Least Weakly Pareto-Optimal


Three words, defined here because the rest of the section depends on them. Both objectives are maximized.


  • Plan $A$ dominates plan $B$ when $A$ is at least as good on both objectives and better on at least one.
  • A plan is Pareto-optimal (also called efficient) when no feasible plan dominates it. The set of Pareto-optimal plans, drawn as points (NPV, risk), is the Pareto frontier.
  • A plan is weakly efficient when no feasible plan is strictly better on both objectives. Every Pareto-optimal plan is weakly efficient, but not the other way round: a weakly efficient plan can be dominated by a plan that ties it on one objective and beats it on the other.


The ε-constraint answer is always at least weakly efficient. Suppose plan $Q$ were strictly better on both objectives than the answer $A$. Then $Q$ removes more risk than $A$, which is at least $\varepsilon$, so $Q$ satisfies the floor; and $Q$ has more NPV than $A$. So $A$ was not the best plan with risk at least $\varepsilon$, a contradiction.


When the ε problem has only one optimal plan, that plan is Pareto-optimal: a plan dominating it would satisfy the floor and have at least its NPV, so it would be a second optimal plan. When the problem has several optimal plans that tie on NPV and differ in risk, the solver may return the one with less risk, which another optimal plan dominates. Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points shows this happening on a GridCap variant, and fixes it.


ε-Constraint Reaches Plans No Weighted Sum Can


Lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points showed that a weighted sum only finds supported Pareto points: those that are optimal for some positive weights, which are the points on the upper-right convex hull of the frontier, its corners and any point lying exactly on a segment between two corners. A Pareto-optimal plan that sits in a dent of the frontier, below the straight line joining two neighbours, is unsupported, and no choice of weights picks it.


The ε-constraint has no such blind spot. The floor cuts away every plan below it, and the best NPV among the plans that remain can sit in a dent. Problem statement


A city transit agency is replacing diesel buses. It is wrapped for you as solve_at(floor), which returns the plan's score as a pair (riders, tonnes_cut), or None when no plan cuts that much.

It asks solve_at at n floors evenly spaced from low to high (both included, n >= 2), in increasing order, and returns the list of different scores it got back, in the order first found. A floor that returns None adds nothing.

A Sweep Finds at Most One Plan per Floor, So Its Steps Must Fit the Data


A common summary says "sweeping ε gives the exact Pareto frontier". The output above says otherwise. Each floor returns one plan, so 11 floors find at most 11 of the 65 plans, and 21 floors found only 20, because two floors fell between the same pair of neighbours and returned the same plan.


A floor sweep finds every Pareto plan only when its steps are finer than the gaps in the data. Sort the Pareto plans by risk. Plan $k$ is the answer for every floor above plan $k-1$'s risk and up to its own, so a grid misses it when no floor lands in that interval. The same program measures the narrowest interval:



Two neighbouring Pareto plans differ by $91 of discounted risk on a range of more than $5.5 million. A grid on risk with steps that fine needs over 60,000 solves, and on other data the narrowest gap could be smaller still, with no way to know it before the frontier is found. So the claim is true only with a step fine enough for the data. Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points gets the same 65 plans in 65 solves, by putting NPV, whose values are whole $k, in the floor and stepping by $1k, and by skipping floors that cannot change the answer.


Summary


  • Keep one objective, and turn the other into a floor. Maximize NPV subject to risk removed $\ge \varepsilon$ and every rule of the model. On GridCap small, a $13,000k floor keeps $23,815k of NPV and removes $13,221k.
  • The payoff table sets the range of ε. Below the NPV plan's risk the floor does nothing; above the most removable risk ($16,086k) no plan exists.
  • Every answer is at least weakly Pareto-optimal, and Pareto-optimal when the ε problem has a single optimal plan.
  • ε-constraint reaches plans no weighted sum can. 57 of GridCap small's 65 Pareto plans are unsupported; an 11-floor sweep found 6 of them.
  • A sweep finds at most one plan per floor, so its steps must fit the data. 21 floors found 20 of 65 plans; a risk grid sure to find them all needs 60,544 floors.

Challenges


  1. Swap the roles: maximize risk removed subject to NPV $\ge 23{,}000$. Predict whether the answer is on the frontier, and whether its NPV lands exactly on 23,000.
  2. The regulator adds a second commitment: at least $2,000k of the risk removed must be in the Coast region. Write the extra row. How many floors does a sweep now have to choose?
  3. A grid of 11 floors returned 11 different plans on GridCap small. Is there a number of floors above which a grid on risk is sure to find no new plan? What does it depend on?


Design Note: Why Not Just Report the Two Ends


Many planning tools show the NPV plan and the risk plan and stop. The payoff table is cheap, two solves, and it does bound everything. But the interesting plans are in between: on GridCap small, $445k of NPV buys $2,644k more risk removed at the $13,000k floor, while the last $485k of risk on the way to the risk plan costs $3,789k (lesson Generating a frontier and presenting it to planners measures each step). A planner who sees only the ends cannot see that the first steps are cheap and the last ones dear. The floor form is also the one the board speaks in, so the model's answer can be read back to them as their own sentence.
