  16. Supported and unsupported points

Lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean gave the GridCap board eight plans, one for each range of prices between NPV and risk removed. The planners come back with a question of their own: "Keep NPV within 5% of the best, and remove as much risk as you can." The best NPV is $24,260k, so the floor is $23,047k, and the answer is a plan with $23,104k of NPV that removes $14,837k of risk. It is not one of the eight. The board asks the natural follow-up: which weights give that plan, so we can state our price? None do. No weighting of NPV and risk, at any price, returns it, although no other plan beats it on both counts. This lesson shows why, proves it for that plan, counts how common such plans are on GridCap small (57 of its 65 Pareto-optimal plans), and shows that the integer variables are what create them. Five rules, one per heading.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its projects, assets and budgets describe no real company.

A Supported Point Is One Some Weighted Sum Returns

Lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean defined dominance and Pareto optimality. Two more terms split the Pareto-optimal plans in two:

  • a Pareto-optimal plan is supported when it is an optimal plan of the weighted sum for some positive weights, and its objective values are a supported point of the front;
  • it is unsupported when it is Pareto optimal but no weighting, positive or with a zero weight, makes it optimal.

Two edge cases decide where the line between the two falls, and this course settles both the same way everywhere.

  • Zero weights add nothing for a Pareto-optimal plan. Suppose a Pareto-optimal plan is optimal for weights (1, 0): it has the most NPV, and since nothing dominates it, the most risk removed among the plans with that NPV. Give risk a weight $\varepsilon > 0$ instead of 0. A plan with less NPV loses by at least the smallest NPV difference between plans, which a small enough $\varepsilon$ times a risk difference cannot make up, because there are finitely many plans; a plan with the same NPV removes no more risk. So the plan is also optimal for the positive weights $(1, \varepsilon)$, and it is supported. Every Pareto-optimal plan is therefore exactly one of the two, supported or unsupported. A plan that is optimal only at a zero weight ties on NPV and removes less risk than another: it is weakly Pareto optimal (lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean), not Pareto optimal, and neither word applies to it.
  • A point on a straight stretch counts as supported. A Pareto point lying exactly on the segment between two others ties with both at that segment's price, so it is optimal for those positive weights and is supported, even though a solver that breaks the tie may return an end instead.

The smallest example needs three plans, of which a planner may pick exactly one. Plan A scores (10, 1) on two objectives to maximize, B scores (1, 10) and C scores (5, 5):

All three are Pareto optimal: A has the most of objective 1, B the most of objective 2, and C beats A on the second and B on the first. Now weight them, with share $s$ on objective 1:

(At 0.5, A and B tie at 5.5 and max keeps the first.) C never wins, and not because the grid is coarse: C's score is always 5, while the better of A and B scores $\max(1 + 9s,\ 10 - 9s)$, which is never below 5.5. C is the compromise many planners would choose, and it is unsupported.

Supported Points Lie on the Upper Convex Hull of the Front

Draw the Pareto points with NPV across and risk removed up. For a share $s$, the plans with equal weighted score $sN + (1 - s)R$ lie on a straight line, and maximizing the score pushes that line up and to the right until only the last point it touches remains. So a weighted sum can only return a point that some straight line touches with every other point on or below it. Those points form the upper convex hull of the front: the polygon you get by stretching a string over the points from the leftmost to the rightmost, keeping it above all of them. A point inside the string, below the segment joining two hull points, is unsupported.

That gives a test that needs no solver once the front is known. Walk the points in order of NPV and keep a chain; drop the last kept point whenever it lies below the line from the one before it to the new point:

The test inside the loop is the sign of a cross product: positive when the new point $p$ lies above the line through the last two kept points, which means the last kept point is under the string. A point exactly on a segment between two hull points is kept: it ties with both ends at the segment's break-even price, so it is supported, though a solver may return either end instead.

GridCap Small Has 65 Pareto-Optimal Plans, and Weighted Sums Reach 8

To test every Pareto-optimal plan, first find them all. The program does it with the ε-constraint method, the subject of lesson The ε-constraint method (The epsilon-constraint method): maximize risk removed with NPV held at or above a floor, then raise the floor past the plan just found and solve again. Two details make the sweep exact here, and lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points explains both in depth. A second pass maximizes NPV among the plans with that much risk removed, so a plan that only ties on risk and earns less NPV (weakly Pareto optimal) is never recorded. And GridCap's NPV coefficients are whole $k, so a floor $1k above the last plan's NPV skips no plan; with fractional coefficients a fixed step could jump over a point.

The sweep takes two solves per point, plus a last one that finds no plan above the highest floor, and returns the whole front; the hull test then sorts it:

The eight supported points are exactly the eight plans lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean's sweep of 101 weightings found, which is what the rule predicts: every weighting returns a hull point, and every hull point here holds over some range of prices. The other 57 Pareto-optimal plans, seven in eight, can never come out of a weighted sum. The deepest, with $24,156k of NPV, removes $640k less risk than the straight line between its supported neighbours would, yet nothing beats it on both counts.

Finding the supported points does not need the whole front. A weighted sum returns hull points directly, and choosing each new weighting from the two hull points already known finds every corner of the hull with a few solves. (A point exactly on a segment ties with the corners at its ends at the only share where it is optimal, and a solver may return a corner there instead, so a search by weights is only sure to find the corners.) The next practice builds that search, because a fixed grid can miss a hull point whose price range is narrow: in lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean, the plan with $23,573k of NPV holds only for shares 0.61 to 0.62.

Problem statement

A wind-farm developer chooses which turbine sites to build, scored on energy produced and on wildlife impact avoided, both to maximize. The full model takes minutes to solve, so the developer's analysts can afford a handful of weighted solves, not a sweep of a hundred. They want every point a weighted sum can return, each one found, none missed because its range of shares fell between two grid values.

You get best_for(s): it solves the weighted sum with share $s$ on the first measure and $1 - s$ on the second, and returns the (first, second) values of a best option. When several options tie it returns the one with the larger first value, and among those the one with the larger second value. Write supported_points(best_for) in hull.py: the list of points a weighted sum can return, sorted by first value, each once.

Two consequences of that tie rule. At $s = 1$ only the first value counts, so best_for(1.0) returns the option with the most of the first measure and, among several with that much, the one with the most of the second: a Pareto-optimal end, never one that only ties on the first value (best_for(0.0) is the same with the roles swapped). And a point lying exactly on the straight segment between two points you have found ties with both of them at the share where they tie; best_for then returns the end with the larger first value, so no share ever returns that point. It is supported (lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points), but the search cannot see it, and the reference does not list it: the list holds the corners of the hull, each a point that some share returns.

Think in the plane first. Once you know two points $a$ and $b$ that a weighted sum returns, which share makes them tie? If a solve at that share returns a point that scores more than both, what does that tell you, and where can the remaining points be? If it scores no more, can any point lie between them on the hull? Write the share as a formula in $a$ and $b$ before coding.

Problem statement


A city parks department has a small budget and a short list of projects: new trees, a pond, play courts and so on. Each costs money and adds two things the department reports on, square metres of tree canopy (shade) and extra visits a week, both to maximize. The council has asked for "the best plan at our weights", and the department suspects that the plan it would actually recommend is one no weights would ever pick. It wants to find such a plan and show the council a proof it can check by hand.


Write unreachable(projects, budget) in parks.py. A plan is any set of projects whose total cost is within budget. Return a triple (plan, a, b) of sets of project names, or None:


  • plan must be dominated by no allowed plan;
  • a and b must be allowed plans with less and more shade than plan, and the straight line from a to b must pass strictly above plan (more visits at plan's shade);
  • return None only when every unbeaten plan is picked by some weighting.


An unbeaten plan that lies exactly on the straight line between two others counts as picked: at the share where those two tie, it ties with them, so that weighting makes it optimal (it is supported, lesson Supported and unsupported Pareto points). Such a plan is never the answer, and when every unbeaten plan is a hull corner or on a straight stretch between corners, the answer is None. The proof's line must pass strictly above plan, as the second rule says.


The lists are short enough to try every set. Work it out on paper first: which points of the shade-visits picture can a weighted sum return, and why does a line from a to b above plan prove that no share picks plan? Then decide which two plans make a proof that always works.

Integer Variables Create Unsupported Points; an LP Has None


Go back to A, B and C. If the planner could take half of A and half of B, that mix would score (5.5, 5.5):



C is unsupported because the mixes that would beat it are not allowed: a project is funded or not. In a linear program, where every variable is continuous, the mixes are allowed. Any average of two feasible LP solutions is feasible (the constraints are linear), and its objective values are the same average of theirs. So the set of reachable $(N, R)$ values is convex, no point can sit below the segment between two others, and every Pareto-optimal point of a multi-objective LP is supported. That is a theorem (Isermann, 1974, as quoted by Könen and Stiglmayr): for linear programs, the weighted sum finds every efficient solution.


GridCap shows it on the LP relaxation of the same model, each $x_{i,t}$ in $[0, 1]$ instead of $\{0, 1\}$ (build(small, integer=False)). Ask the planners' question at $q$'s NPV, read the dual value of the NPV floor (the risk the last $1k of floor costs: its shadow price, lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver), and use it as the price of a weighted sum:








The LP's answer scores exactly the weighted optimum at price 0.0522, so that weighted sum supports it (the solver may return another plan with the same score; the price makes them tie). The LP gets there with 7 fractional starts, fractions of projects that cannot be built, and it removes $16,176k of risk at that NPV, more than any integer plan removes at any NPV. That is the gap the integer restriction opens, and the unsupported points live in it.


One claim to correct while here: integer fronts are sometimes described as "non-convex". The front of an integer program is a finite set of points, and the precise statement is that some of them can lie strictly inside the convex hull of the others. How many is an empirical matter: the paper above reports that in combinatorial problems unsupported points typically outnumber supported ones, and on GridCap small they are 57 of 65.


Choose the Method by the Question


The two lessons together give a working rule for multi-objective planning:


  • To show the trade-off as prices, use weighted sums and report each supported plan with its break-even prices (lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean). Every plan is Pareto optimal and comes with an argument a board can read: "best for any price between 0.239 and 1.505".
  • To answer a question with a floor or a cap ("keep NPV within 5%", "remove at least $15M of risk"), use a constraint on one objective and optimize the other: the ε-constraint method of lessons The ε-constraint method to Generating a frontier and presenting it to planners. It reaches unsupported plans, which are most of the front here.
  • To put one objective strictly first, solve in priority order (lessons Hierarchical objectives with tolerances and Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve, Lexicographic objectives, and lesson Prioritized objectives with add_auxiliary_objective).


A weighted sum is not wrong for integer programs; it answers a narrower question than it seems to. It finds the plans that are best at some price, and the planners' "within 5%" plan is best at none.


Summary


  • A supported point is one some weighted sum returns. An unsupported point is Pareto optimal and returned by no weighting; with three plans (10, 1), (1, 10) and (5, 5), the compromise (5, 5) is never picked.
  • Supported points lie on the upper convex hull of the front. A point below the segment between two hull points is unsupported.
  • GridCap small has 65 Pareto-optimal plans, and weighted sums reach 8. They are the eight plans of lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean's sweep.
  • No weight reaches a point below the segment between its supported neighbours. Beating $a$ needs $s \ge 0.7539$ and beating $b$ needs $s \le 0.4209$ for the planners' plan.
  • Integer variables create unsupported points; an LP has none. The LP relaxation's answer at the same NPV is reached by the weighted sum with its dual price, 0.0522.

Design Note: Keep the Front, Not Only the Plan


The sweep in this lesson cost 131 solves (two per point and a last, infeasible one), and it bought something a single solve cannot: a list of every plan nothing beats, against which any later question can be checked without solving again. Is the planners' plan Pareto optimal? Look it up. Is it supported? Look it up. What is the most risk any plan removes above $24,000k of NPV? Read it off.
