Lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year left the GridCap board with two plans for the small portfolio. The NPV plan earns $24,260k of NPV and removes $10,577k of discounted failure risk. The risk plan removes $16,086k of risk and earns $18,886k. The board wants one plan, and someone proposes the most common way to get it: "count both, 50/50". This lesson builds that plan, then asks what "50/50" actually told the solver. The answer is less than it sounds: the weights set one number, a price between the two objectives, and the plan only changes when that price crosses one of a few break-even values. Five rules, one per heading.

GridCap is a fictional utility generated for this course; its projects, assets and budgets describe no real company.

A Weighted Sum Folds Two Objectives Into One

A model has one objective. To optimize two at once, the simplest move is to add them up, each multiplied by a weight, a nonnegative number saying how much it counts. That is a weighted sum (also called linear scalarization: "scalar" because two numbers become one). Everything else in the model stays as it was.

The Model

The start-year model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years, whole, with the objective replaced by the weighted sum of lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year's NPV and lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's risk removed.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k, from lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year); $\text{rr}_{i,t}$, the discounted risk it removes over the ten-year window ($k, from lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $w_N \ge 0$ and $w_R \ge 0$, the weights on NPV and on risk removed, not both zero.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$.

Objective. With $N(x) = \sum_{i,t} \text{npv}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$ the plan's NPV and $R(x) = \sum_{i,t} \text{rr}_{i,t}\, x_{i,t}$ the risk it removes:

Constraints.

The right-hand form of the objective is the one to remember: a weighted sum of two linear objectives is one linear objective, whose coefficient on $x_{i,t}$ is the weighted sum of the two coefficients. The model stays a MILP of the same size.

In MathOpt

The module's library builds the model of lesson Cash-flow spreading across years with no objective and keeps the two objective expressions. MathOpt's maximize takes any linear expression (as in the MathOpt basic example), so the weighted sum is one line, and each call replaces the previous objective:

MathOpt's own word for several objectives is something else. add_auxiliary_objective (lesson Prioritized objectives with add_auxiliary_objective) stores objectives with priorities, solved one after another, not added up; lessons Hierarchical objectives with tolerances and Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve (Lexicographic objectives) build on it. A weighted sum needs no special API at all. Gurobi does offer one, a "blended" objective whose parts carry weights (ObjNWeight), and it is the same sum computed by the solver.

Weights (1, 0) and (0, 1) give back lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's two plans; the board's 50/50 gives a third:

The 50/50 plan is not "halfway" between the two: it keeps 94.3% of the best NPV and 96.7% of the most risk removed. The weights said nothing about halves of anything. The next rules say what they did say.

Only the Ratio of the Weights Matters

Multiply both weights by the same positive number $c$ and every plan's weighted score is multiplied by $c$, so the order of the plans, and the best one, does not change. A weighting is therefore one number, not two. Two equivalent ways to state it:

  • the share on NPV, $s = w_N / (w_N + w_R)$, with $1 - s$ on risk (50/50 is $s = 0.5$);
  • the price $p = w_N / w_R$, in $k of risk removed per $1k of NPV: the objective counts $1k of NPV as worth $p$ $k of risk removed. 50/50 is $p = 1$, and $p = s / (1 - s)$.

The first three rows are the same price, so they must give the same plan. The last two are a different price, 0.25, and give the same plan too. That is not the scaling rule; it is the fourth rule of this lesson at work, and it is worth noticing now: a board member who fought for "80% on risk" instead of 50% would have changed nothing.

The Ratio Is a Price, So It Depends on the Units

Because a weight multiplies a quantity, its meaning depends on the unit of that quantity. Lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in showed this for a value function: a weight is an exchange rate between units. In GridCap's two objectives the units happen to match, both are discounted $k, so 50/50 means "a dollar of expected failure cost avoided is worth a dollar of NPV", a statement a board can argue about.

Now state the same objectives differently and apply "50/50" again. Dividing an objective by a scale before weighting it is the same as dividing its weight by that scale, so each line below is a weighted sum on the $k objectives with the price the restatement implies:

The "range" is an objective's best value minus its value in the other objective's best plan (24,260 − 18,886 for NPV, 16,086 − 10,577 for risk), and the "best value" is its maximum alone. Both are common ways to make objectives comparable before weighting them.

Read the second row. A team that stores risk in $M and NPV in $k, and weights them 50/50, has told the solver that $1k of NPV is worth $1,000k of risk removed. The solver obeys and returns the NPV plan, giving up $5,509k of risk removed (16,086 − 10,577) that the board believed it was buying. Nothing in the output warns about it. The two normalized rows imply prices of 1.025 and 0.663, close enough to 1 that on this data they give the same plan; on another portfolio they would not have to.

So "50/50" is not yet a statement about the plan. "We would give up $1k of NPV for $1k of expected failure cost avoided" is. Weights are often described as arbitrary scaling factors. They are not arbitrary, they are prices, and a weight that was set without saying in which units is a price nobody chose.

    Price a tonne of CO2 in riders

    • a weighted sum of two linear objectives is one linear objective on the same model

Problem statement

A city transit agency can upgrade some of its bus depots this year. Each upgrade costs money and brings two benefits: more daily riders (better service from the depot) and less CO2 a year (electric buses charged there). The data team stores CO2 in kilograms a year, as the meters report it. After a long meeting the agency's board settled its trade-off in one sentence: "cutting one tonne of CO2 a year is worth as much to us as riders_per_tonne extra daily riders."

Write weighted_choice(upgrades, budget, riders_per_tonne) in depots.py: the set of upgrade names to fund, at most once each, with total cost within budget, that is best by the board's own trade-off. The model's variables and the budget row are written; the objective is yours.

Write the objective first, in notation: with $U$ the upgrades, $x_u \in \{0, 1\}$ whether upgrade $u$ is funded, $r_u$ its riders, $g_u$ its CO2 cut in kg and $\pi$ the board's price, what are the two weights, and in which units is each term? Then code it.

The Plan Moves in Jumps as the Weights Change


Sweep the share on NPV from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.01, 101 solves, and record which plan each weighting returns:






A hundred and one weightings produce eight plans. Each plan holds over a range of shares and then gives way, all at once, to the next. The 50/50 plan holds from a share of 0.20 to 0.60, which is why the board's "80% on risk" ($s = 0.2$) changed nothing. A move from 0.60 to 0.61 changes a lot:



Why jumps? The model can only return one of finitely many plans, and each plan's weighted score $s\,N + (1 - s)\,R$ is a straight line in $s$. The weighted sum returns whichever line is on top at the chosen $s$, and the top changes only where two lines cross.


The crossing is a break-even price. Take two plans, $A$ with less NPV and more risk removed and $B$ with more NPV and less risk removed. $B$ scores at least as high as $A$ when



The right side is the risk given up per $1k of NPV gained by moving from $A$ to $B$ ($k of risk per $1k of NPV, the units of $p$): the price at which the move is a fair trade. Below it, the weighted sum stays at $A$; above it, it moves to $B$. For the sweep's neighbours:






Each computed share falls between the last grid share of one plan and the first of the next (0.6008 between 0.60 and 0.61, for instance). The 50/50 plan is the answer for every price from 0.239 to 1.505: anyone who values $1k of NPV at between $239 and $1,505 of risk removed gets that plan, and no finer agreement about the weights is needed. The first row is the cheap trade a board should see: the last $485k of risk removed costs $3,789k of NPV, so only a price below 0.128 keeps the pure risk plan.


Lesson Generating a frontier and presenting it to planners quotes the same steps the other way up, as NPV given up per $1 of risk removed, because that is how a planner asks ("what does the next dollar of risk cost?"). Its prices are the reciprocals of these: the step between the pure risk plan and its neighbour (NPV $18,886k and $22,675k) is 0.128 $k of risk per $1k of NPV here, and 1/0.128 = 7.81 dollars of NPV per dollar of risk there. Check the units before comparing a price from one lesson with a price from the other.


This is the useful way to present a weighted sum to planners: not "we used 50/50", but "this plan is best for any price between these two; above 1.505 we switch to the next plan, which gains $703k of NPV and gives up $1,058k of risk removed".

Positive Weights Give a Pareto-Optimal Plan


Some vocabulary first, because the next lesson rests on it. Plan $y$ dominates plan $x$ when $y$ is at least as good in every objective and strictly better in at least one; here $N(y) \ge N(x)$ and $R(y) \ge R(x)$, with one of them strict. A plan no feasible plan dominates is Pareto optimal (also called efficient, or nondominated), and the set of their objective values is the Pareto front (or frontier). A plan is weakly Pareto optimal when no plan is strictly better in every objective; that is a weaker promise, because a weakly optimal plan can still be beaten in one objective at no cost in the other.


Rule. If both weights are positive, every optimal plan of the weighted sum is Pareto optimal. The argument takes one line: if $y$ dominated the weighted optimum $x$, then $w_N N(y) + w_R R(y) > w_N N(x) + w_R R(x)$ (one difference is positive, the other is not negative, and both are multiplied by a positive weight), so $x$ was not optimal.


With a zero weight the promise weakens. Weights (1, 0) ask only for the most NPV. If two plans tie on NPV and differ in risk removed, both are optimal, and the solver may return the one that removes less risk: a plan that is only weakly Pareto optimal. Lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year's NPV plan, the share-1.00 row above, happens to be Pareto optimal (the next lesson lists it on the front), but a zero weight does not promise it. Two fixes: give the other objective a small positive weight, small enough never to outweigh a real difference in the first, or solve lexicographically (lessons Hierarchical objectives with tolerances and Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve, Lexicographic objectives), which is what "first NPV, then risk among the ties" means. Lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points (The epsilon-constraint method) meets the same problem from another side.


So the weighted sum is a safe tool in one direction: every plan it returns with positive weights is a plan no other plan beats on both counts. The next lesson, Supported and unsupported Pareto points, asks the opposite question: does every Pareto-optimal plan come out of some weighting? On GridCap small, the eight plans above are all the weighted sum can ever return.

Summary


  • A weighted sum folds two objectives into one. $\max\, w_N N(x) + w_R R(x)$ is a linear objective with coefficient $w_N \text{npv}_{i,t} + w_R \text{rr}_{i,t}$; in MathOpt it is one maximize call. On GridCap small, 50/50 gives 30 projects, $22,870k of NPV and $15,554k of risk removed.
  • Only the ratio of the weights matters. Scaling both weights changes nothing; a weighting is one number, the share $s$ or the price $p = w_N / w_R$.
  • The ratio is a price, so it depends on the units. Stating risk in $M instead of $k turns 50/50 into a price of 1,000 and returns the NPV plan.
  • The plan moves in jumps as the weights change. 101 weightings gave 8 plans; the plan changes where the price crosses a break-even value, the risk given up per $1k of NPV gained between neighbours.
  • Positive weights give a Pareto-optimal plan. A zero weight only promises a weakly Pareto-optimal one.

Design Note: Report the Price Range, Not the Weights


The weights a model was solved with are a poor record of the decision. They depend on units, they can be scaled freely, and most nearby weightings give the same plan. The break-even prices around the chosen plan are what a reviewer needs: they say how far the board's view would have to move before the plan changes, in units the board uses. Keep the weights in the run's record for reproducibility, and put the price range in the report.
