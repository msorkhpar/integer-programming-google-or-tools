Lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year left GridCap's planners with two plans for the small portfolio. The plan with the most NPV earns $24,260k and removes $10,577k of discounted failure cost (risk). The plan that removes the most risk removes $16,086k and earns $18,886k. Lessons Weighted sums and what the weights really mean–Supported and unsupported Pareto points blended the two objectives into one with weights. GridCap's board asks for something else, in words:

NPV is what the rate case is judged on. Keep it within 1% of the best NPV we can reach. Within that, remove as much risk as you can.

That request has no weights in it. It ranks the objectives (NPV first, risk second) and says how much of the first one the second may spend (1%). This is a lexicographic, or hierarchical, objective with a tolerance. Lesson Prioritized objectives with add_auxiliary_objective showed MathOpt's API for it, with total spend as the second objective. This lesson is about the method itself: what the order means, what a tolerance does to the second objective (measured, as a relative share and as an absolute amount), and how to choose one. Lesson Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve then implements it three ways and checks that they agree. GridCap is a fictional utility; its numbers come from a seeded generator, not from any real company.

The Model

The start-year model of lessons Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows–Cash-flow spreading across years, with two objectives in a fixed order.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ (in $k, from lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year); $\text{rr}_{i,t}$, the discounted risk it removes over the ten-year window (in $k, from lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k); $a \ge 0$, the absolute tolerance on NPV ($k), and $r \ge 0$, the relative tolerance on NPV (a share of the best NPV).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$.

Objectives, in priority order.

The two passes. A lexicographic objective is solved as a sequence of ordinary models. Pass 1 finds the best NPV, $\Gamma_1$. Pass 2 maximizes risk removed over the plans whose NPV is within the allowed loss $\delta_1$ of $\Gamma_1$:

The row $\text{keep\_npv}$ is the only thing pass 2 adds to the model. Its name in the code is keep_npv. With $a = r = 0$ it says "NPV stays at its best value", and the second objective can only choose among plans that tie on NPV.

A Lexicographic Order Lets a Later Objective Choose Only Among Plans the Earlier Ones Accept

The module's helper, solve_levels, runs the passes. Each objective is a Level: a name, a sense, and its two tolerances (both 0 unless set). Lesson Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve opens the function; here only its results matter. Five runs on the small portfolio:

Read the first two rows together. With no tolerance, the risk pass is allowed only plans with NPV exactly $24,260k, and it found none that removes more risk than the NPV plan already does. The same holds the other way round: putting NPV second changed nothing about the risk plan. That is typical of a model like this one. NPV and risk are sums of many different dollar amounts, so two different plans almost never tie exactly on the first objective, and the second objective has nothing to choose between. With zero tolerance, a lexicographic objective is mostly a tie-breaker.

The order is the planner's decision, and it decides everything. NPV first gives the NPV plan; risk first gives the risk plan. The second objective never outvotes the first, however large its numbers are. That is the difference from the weighted sums of lesson Weighted sums and what the weights really mean, where a large enough weight on risk always wins.

The last row is the first sign of what a tolerance does. Allowing risk removed to fall by 1% of its best (1% of $16,086.1k is $160.9k) let the NPV pass find a plan that gives up $147.2k of risk and earns $599k more NPV.

A Tolerance Turns the First Objective Into a Floor

The board's request, in code, is two levels: NPV with a 1% relative tolerance, then risk.

Pass 1 finds $\Gamma_1 = 24{,}260$. The allowed loss is $\delta_1 = 0.01 \times 24{,}260 = 242.6$, so the keep_npv row is $f_1(x) \ge 24{,}017.4$: a floor on NPV. From then on NPV is not an objective anymore; it is a constraint. Pass 2 maximizes risk removed above that floor and finds a plan with NPV $24,019k, $241k (0.99%) below the best, that removes $12,750.5k of risk. That is $2,173.9k more risk removed than the NPV plan, 20.6% more, for less than 1% of NPV. This plan is section 8's milestone: the lexicographic plan with a 1% NPV tolerance.

What changed between the two plans is what a planner will ask about:

Three projects leave the plan, three join it, and two start in a different year. The biggest single move is P027, a transformer repair that removes $1,313.3k of risk. Each joining project has a positive NPV, so the NPV plan did not leave it out for being unprofitable: within the budgets, that money earned more NPV elsewhere. The 1% of slack is what lets pass 2 fund it.

With a tolerance, the second pass can return a plan that a third pass would improve. Pass 2 looks only at risk. If two plans removed exactly the same risk and both cleared the floor, pass 2 could return the one with less NPV, and that plan would be dominated: another plan is at least as good on both objectives and better on one. A third pass settles it: maximize NPV again, with risk kept at the value pass 2 reached.

On the small portfolio pass 3 changes nothing: the 1% plan is not dominated. The check costs one more solve, and it is the lexicographic form of the problem lesson AUGMECON: avoiding weakly efficient points (AUGMECON) solves inside a single model.

The Allowed Loss Is a Ceiling, and the Plan Moves in Steps

How much risk does each extra share of NPV buy? tolerance_sweep.py solves the same two levels with eight relative tolerances and six absolute ones. "Allowed" is $\delta_1$, "taken" is the NPV the final plan actually gives up, and "gained" is the risk removed beyond the NPV plan's $10,576.6k:

Four things to read from this table:

  • The allowed loss is a ceiling, not a price. Every "taken" is at or below its "allowed". At 10% the plan gives up $1,585k of the $2,426k it could: risk removed is already $15,600.7k, close to the risk plan's $16,086.1k, and no plan above the floor removes more.
  • The plan moves in steps. Plans are made of whole projects, so the risk removed jumps from one plan to the next. A 0.25% tolerance ($60.6k) and a $50k one return the same plan, because no plan with more risk removed loses between $25k and $60.6k of NPV.
  • Equal allowed losses give equal plans, whatever their form. 1% of $24,260k is $242.6k, and the relative 1% row and the absolute $242.6k row are the same plan. Pass 2 sees only the floor.
  • Each extra dollar of tolerance buys less. The first $241k of NPV given up buys $2,173.9k of risk removed, about 9.0 dollars of risk per dollar of NPV. Going from 1% to 2% gives up $244k more for $699.1k more risk (2.9 per dollar), and from 5% to 10% $429k more for $763.5k (1.8 per dollar).

The sweep is the answer to give the board, rather than one plan. The tolerance is a business decision, and this table shows what each choice costs and buys. It also shows that the 1% the board asked for sits just past a large step: at 0.5% the gain is $854.1k, at 1% it is $2,173.9k.

    The steps of a tolerance sweep

    • reading a tolerance sweep as steps: what each larger allowance actually gives up and buys

    Not startedPassed

Problem statement

A transit agency plans next year's bus-fleet upgrades with two ranked objectives: first the most riders served (thousands of trips), then the most emissions cut (tonnes). Its analyst has already run the lexicographic solve once per allowance in a sweep: each run let riders served fall by at most allowed below the best value, best, and reported the plan's riders served (first) and emissions cut (second).

The board does not want the raw sweep. It wants the steps: the allowances at which the plan actually gets better on emissions, and what each step costs and buys.

Write steps(best, sweep). sweep is a list of (allowed, first, second) tuples, in any order, and always includes the row with allowed == 0. Return a list of (allowed, taken, gain) tuples, one per step, in increasing order of allowed:

  • a row is a step if its second is larger than the second of every row with a smaller allowance; a row that reaches no more than an earlier row is the same plan bought at a higher price, and is left out;
  • taken is the riders served the plan gave up, best - first;
  • gain is the emissions cut beyond the zero-allowance row's second.

The zero-allowance row is always the first step, with a gain of 0. About ten lines.

A Relative Tolerance Follows the Scenario; an Absolute One Stays Put


A relative tolerance is a share of $|\Gamma_1|$, so its dollar value moves whenever the best NPV moves: in another budget scenario, another portfolio, or next year's data. An absolute tolerance is the same dollar amount everywhere. scenario_tolerance.py scales every year's budget to 85%, 100% and 120% and solves both ways, with the 1% and the $242.6k that agreed on the base case:






With smaller budgets, 1% is only $212.6k, and the plan removes $699.1k less risk than the fixed $242.6k allows. With larger budgets, 1% grows to $296.7k and the relative tolerance removes more. Neither is right in general; they answer different requests:


  • Relative when the request is a share: "no more than 1% of the best NPV". It scales with the scenario, so the planner's intent holds in every scenario of a study.
  • Absolute when the request is an amount: "no more than $250k of NPV", for example a figure committed in a rate case. It means the same dollars in every scenario.


Two cases follow from the formula $\delta = \max(a, r \cdot |\Gamma|)$. When $\Gamma$ is close to zero (an objective such as net cost, which can be near zero or negative), a relative tolerance allows almost nothing, and only an absolute one gives room. The absolute value keeps the allowed loss positive when $\Gamma$ is negative. When both tolerances are set, the larger allowed loss applies, as lesson Prioritized objectives with add_auxiliary_objective showed for MathOpt; Gurobi's documentation says the same (lesson Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve).

Problem statement


A city scores a handful of road-resurfacing packages by hand before it commits to a model. Each package has a value for every objective, for example {"safety": 42.0, "cost": 3.1, "noise": -6.0}. The council ranks the objectives and gives each one a sense and tolerances, as a list of levels in priority order:



The packages the council accepts are found level by level. At each level, take the best value $\Gamma$ of that objective among the packages still accepted, and keep only the packages within the allowed loss $\delta = \max(\text{abs\_tol},\ \text{rel\_tol} \cdot |\Gamma|)$ of it: at least $\Gamma - \delta$ for a maximized objective, at most $\Gamma + \delta$ for a minimized one.


Write accepted(packages, levels). packages maps a package name to its values; return the sorted list of the names that survive every level. About ten lines; no solver is needed for a handful of packages.

Summary


  • A lexicographic order lets a later objective choose only among plans the earlier ones accept. With zero tolerance on GridCap, the second objective changed nothing: exact ties are rare.
  • A tolerance turns the first objective into a floor. 1% of NPV ($242.6k) bought $2,173.9k more risk removed (20.6%); a third pass confirmed no plan beats it on both objectives.
  • The allowed loss is a ceiling, and the plan moves in steps. Show planners the sweep: the gain per dollar falls from about 9.0 to 1.8 as the tolerance grows.
  • A relative tolerance follows the scenario; an absolute one stays put. Pick the form that matches how the request was stated.

Design Note: A Tolerance Is a Question for the Planner, Not a Solver Setting


It is tempting to treat the tolerance like a gap or a feasibility tolerance: a small number an engineer sets once. It is not. It states how much of the first objective the organization will give up for the second, and the sweep shows that answer can move risk removed by thousands of dollars. Ask the planner for it, in the form they think in (a share or an amount), record it with the run, and show the sweep around it so they see what the next step would buy.
