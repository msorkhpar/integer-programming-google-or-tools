  19. A regional spend share

The living specs of the last lesson describe the planner as it is. The first real change arrives from the rate-case settlement for the Plains region:

Request 2. Plains should get its fair share of the work: at least a quarter of the capital spend, every year. The settlement is signed; the plan for the next cycle must keep it.

It reads like ten minutes of code, one row a year. Spec-driven development makes it a change first: a folder, openspec/changes/add-regional-share/, with a proposal, a spec delta, a design and tasks, reviewed before any code. This lesson takes the change through the whole loop. The code before it is snapshot steps/01; after it, steps/02. GridCap and its settlement are fictional.

The Planner Has No Share Rule, and Plains Gets Nothing in Four Years of Five

regional_share.py plans the 40 invented projects with the code of steps/01, and reports the Plains share of each year's spend:

Plains projects are worth less per $k than the others in this portfolio, so a planner that only maximizes value leaves them for last. No living requirement says otherwise: the capital-plan spec has a budget, a start window, a cap and the most valuable plan, and nothing about regions.

Two Questions Go Back to the Planners Before the Proposal

"A quarter of the capital spend, every year" leaves two choices open, and each is a different rule:

  • A share of what? Of the yearly budget, or of what the plan actually spends that year? With the budget, a year that spends little must still give Plains a quarter of the whole budget.
  • When? The note says every year; a share over the whole plan would let Plains catch up later.

The modeler does not choose. The questions go back to the planners, who own the rule, and their answers go into the change: the share is of what the plan spends, and it holds in every plan year. The design records both answers under its Context, so a reviewer can see what was asked and decided.

The Change Folder: Proposal, Delta, Design and Tasks

The proposal says why and what, in the planners' words:

Under Capabilities, it lists capital-plan as modified: this change adds requirements to an existing living spec. The spec delta, specs/capital-plan/spec.md, has one section, ## ADDED Requirements, with two requirements. The first states the rule:

Each scenario pins the rule where a careless reading breaks it. The first sits on the boundary: $250k of $1,000k is exactly 25%, and $250k of $1,010k is under it, so a rule that is a little off in either direction fails it. The second is the year with no spend, where the share, a ratio, is not defined. The third checks the plan, not only a fixed list. The second requirement, Shares are checked before solving, has three scenarios: a share outside 0 to 100, shares adding up to more than 100, and a share for a region with no project are refused with a message.

The design, design.md, is where the formulation goes. OpenSpec keeps the how out of the spec and in the design; for a model, the how is the rows. Its decisions, in short:

Integer coefficients matter twice here. They make the boundary exact, $100 \times 250 = 25 \times 1{,}000$ with nothing to round, and CP-SAT asks for them: its documentation says every constraint must be stated with integers, scaled up if need be. The tasks then order the work, tests first:

Review the Proposal, Then the Deltas, Then the Tasks; Validate the Format

OpenSpec's guide to reviewing a change reads the files in the order that lets a reviewer stop earliest: the proposal first (is this the right problem?), then the spec deltas (is "done" defined correctly, and what is missing?), the design for a bigger change, and the tasks last. Here the review is the planners' and the modeler's. The planners check the scenarios against the settlement; the modeler checks that every scenario can be a test. The Model

The planner's model with the share. Changes from the last lesson are marked.

Sets. $P$, the projects; $Y$, the plan years; $T_i \subseteq Y$, the years project $i$ may start; $R$, the regions, and $P_r \subseteq P$ the projects of region $r$ $\leftarrow$; $S \subseteq R$, the regions the request names $\leftarrow$.

Parameters. $c_i$, cost ($k); $v_i$, value ($k); $B$, the yearly budget ($k); $K$, the start cap; $s_r \in \{0, 1, \dots, 100\}$, the least share of region $r \in S$, in whole percent, with $\sum_{r \in S} s_r \le 100$ $\leftarrow$.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$. The share adds none.

Objective.

Constraints. With $\text{spend}_y = \sum_{i \in P :\, y \in T_i} c_i \, x_{i,y}$ and $\text{spend}_{r,y}$ the same sum over $P_r$ only:

In a year with no spend the share row reads $0 \ge 0$ and holds, which is the second scenario. A share of 0 sets no minimum and gets no row. The rows are named share_plains_2027 to share_plains_2031. Lesson Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend writes a plan-wide program share the same way.

Tests First: Six Scenarios, Six Tests, Six Failures on the Old Code

Each scenario becomes a test in tests/test_capital_plan.py, named after it. A test of a rule pairs a plan the rule allows with one it refuses, so that a model with no rule at all cannot pass it:

The budget of $5,000k is one no test plan comes near, so only the share can refuse. The three tests of the second requirement use a helper, refusal, that returns the message of the ValueError a call raises, or None when it raises none. Run on the code of steps/01, where Request.shares is a stub the model ignores, every new test fails on its assertion:

Read where each failed. In the two boundary tests the allowed plan passed and the refused one was let through (assert not True): a model with no share allows everything. So a test with only the allowed plan is too weak, even one sitting exactly on the boundary: it passes on code with no share at all. The plan test got the two North projects, the old optimum. The three checks raised nothing.

Shares that add up to more than 100, or a share for a region with no project, leave only plans that spend nothing; the model would return such a plan with no word about why, so the check refuses the request with a message instead:

What the Share Costs, and What the Planners Should See

The same 40 projects, with the code of steps/02 and a 25% share for Plains:

Every year with spend gives Plains at least 25%. And 2027 spends nothing at all: no Plains project may start in 2027, so the only 2027 that keeps the share is an empty one. That is exactly what the second scenario says, and it costs a whole year of work. The scenarios made it visible on paper; the run shows its price, $5,380k of value. It goes back to the planners as a finding, not a bug: if the settlement meant "in every year with Plains work available", that is a new change, with its own delta.

In the share line, - marks a year that spends nothing, where a share is not defined. The project's own report, main.py, prints a line only for the years the plan spends anything at this step, with the share rounded to a whole percent (f"{pct:.0f}"). Two lessons on, in Carry-Over: A MODIFIED Budget Rule and a RENAMED Requirement, the report prints every plan year, and a year with no spend shows plains 0%.

What the Step Did to the Code

The planner grew from 101 to 125 lines, and build from 5 to 9 on the branching count: a loop over the named regions, the skip of a zero share, a comprehension that filters a region, and a loop over the years. The specs grew by 2 requirements and 6 scenarios, and the tests by 6, one per scenario.

Practice. This lesson's project practice starts from the code of steps/01, with the specs and tests of steps/02. It is graded by those tests and by a check that main.py prints steps/02/expected/report.txt.

Problem statement

The rate-case settlement for the Plains region sent the second request:

Request 2. Plains should get its fair share of the work: at least a quarter of the capital spend, every year. The settlement is signed; the plan for the next cycle must keep it.

The planners answered the two open questions: the share is of what the plan spends, not of the budget, and it holds in every plan year. The change is proposed and archived in openspec/: its proposal, its delta with the regional share and the checks of shares, its design and its tasks, and the living capital-plan spec now states both requirements. The specs, the tests (one per scenario, named after it) and the expected report are given and read-only.

You start from the planner of the last lesson, where Request.shares is a stub the model ignores. Make the plan give each named region at least its share, in whole percent, of what the plan spends in every plan year, with integer coefficients, since CP-SAT takes integers only. Refuse, before solving, shares that no plan with any spend could keep. Count each region's share in the report apart from the model.

Run executes main.py, which plans a tiny portfolio: 12 projects of GridCap, a fictional utility, drawn by capplan/portfolio.py from seed 58, over three years with $1,500k a year and a 25% share for Plains, and prints each year's starts, spend and Plains share, then the line about the specs; expected/report.txt is the report it should print. Submit runs every test in tests/, the earlier steps' included, and compares Run's report with the expected one.

Summary


  • A request becomes a change folder before it becomes code: a proposal (why, what, which capabilities), a spec delta, a design for the formulation, and tasks with tests first.
  • Open questions go back to the rule's owners. Share of the spend or the budget, each year or over the plan: the planners answer, and the design records it.
  • A share is a ratio, and the row is linear: $100\, \text{spend}_{r,y} \ge s_r \, \text{spend}_y$, with integer coefficients, which CP-SAT requires and which make the boundary exact.
  • Each scenario is a test that pairs an allowed and a refused plan, and each fails on the old code before the code changes.
  • Archive appends the ADDED requirements to the living spec and keeps the change for its history.

Challenges


  1. The planners come back: the share should hold only in years where at least one project of the region may start. Write the new requirement text and the scenario for 2027, and say which delta section it goes in.
  2. A colleague writes the share row as 4 * spend(members) >= spend(projects) for 25%. Which shares can that form state, and which can it not?
  3. Write a scenario for a share of exactly 100%, and predict what the plan of the 40 projects does with it.


Design Note: The Formulation Lives in the Design, Not the Spec


The spec says "at least its share, in whole percent, of what the plan spends in every plan year". The row, $100\, \text{spend}_{r,y} \ge s_r \, \text{spend}_y$, is in design.md. Had the spec held the row, the planners would have been asked to sign a piece of algebra, and the choice it encodes (spend, not budget; every year, not the plan) would have been made by whoever wrote the row, before the planners could see that there was a choice. OpenSpec's own test for what belongs in a spec fits: if the implementation can change without changing what anyone can observe, it is not spec. The row could be written another way; the share could not.
