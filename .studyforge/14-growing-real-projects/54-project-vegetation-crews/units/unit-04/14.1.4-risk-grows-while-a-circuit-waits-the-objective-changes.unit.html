  17. Risk grows while a circuit waits

The crew schedule of the lesson A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals reached GridCap's reliability engineer, who asked a question the model had never been asked:

Your plan trims C13 in the first five weeks and leaves C12 for weeks 17 to 23. C12 is the one I worry about: its trees are growing into the lines, and every week we wait its risk goes up. A trim in September does not take out a year of risk; it takes out what is left of the year, and the circuit carried the rest. Order the work so that we carry the least risk over the season. And a circuit we skip waits half a year more for next season's crews; count that too.

Until now the objective was "risk removed a year", the same for a trim in week 0 and in week 23. The engineer's rule makes when matter, and it changes which plan is best. (GridCap and its seasons are fictional, generated by the project's seeded generator.) This lesson takes the project from steps/03 to steps/04.

The Old Objective Is Blind to Order, So It Cannot Prefer Early Work

In steps/03 the objective is $\sum_c \text{risk}_c \sum_k p_{c,k}$. No start week appears in it. Any two schedules that trim the same circuits score the same, so the solver returns whichever order it finds first, and swapping C12 to the front of the season would change nothing it can see. The model cannot answer the engineer because the engineer's quantity is not in it: nothing a test or a tweak to the rows can fix. The objective itself has to change.

Risk Carried Is a Table Indexed by the Week a Trim Ends

The rule, stated as data. Each circuit's weekly risk starts at its yearly risk divided by 52 and grows by $g_c$ dollars every week the circuit waits (a new inventory column, growth, in $k a week). A trim that ends at week $e$ leaves the circuit carrying risk for weeks $0, \dots, e - 1$:

A circuit left for next season waits until week $H + D$, with $D = 26$ weeks until next season's crews can reach it, and carries $R_c(H + D)$. risk_plan.py prints C17's table:

Finishing C17 in week 8 instead of week 24 saves $281k; not trimming it this season costs $800k more than finishing in week 24. $R_c$ is not linear in $e$ (the growth term is quadratic), so how should a model hold it? The options:

OptionWhat it givesWhy not, or why
Approximate $R_c$ by a straight line, a weight times the end weeka linear objective, the weighted-completion objective of lesson Scheduling vegetation crews across circuitswrong for fast growers: it undercounts late weeks, which is the engineer's whole point
Piecewise-linear segments with extra variables (lesson The lambda (SOS2-style) formulation built from binaries)exact at the breakpointsmany variables per circuit to state a table that is already exact
The exact table, and CP-SAT's element constraint: $\rho_c = R_c[e_c]$exact, one constraint per circuitchosen: the end week is an integer from 0 to 50, so the whole function is a list of 51 integers (lesson Element, table and allowed-assignment constraints)

The element constraint says expressions[index] == target, in the words of add_element's docstring: the target equals the entry of a list at a position given by a variable. With the end week as the index and the risk table as the list, the target is the risk carried, exactly.

The Model

Before this lesson the objective was $\max \sum_c \text{risk}_c \sum_k p_{c,k}$, with the once, crew and budget rows of the lesson A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals. After, in full:

Sets. $C$, circuits; $K$, crews; weeks $0, \dots, H - 1$, $H = 24$.

Parameters. $\text{cost}_c$, $B$ ($k); $w_c$ (weeks); $R_c(e)$ for $e = 0, \dots, H + D$, the risk carried if the trim ends at $e$ ($k, integers); $D = 26$. ← this lesson: $R_c$, from $\text{risk}_c$ and $g_c$

Variables. $t_c \in \{0, 1\}$, 1 if $c$ is trimmed this season; ← this lesson $p_{c,k} \in \{0,1\}$, $s_{c,k} \in \{0, \dots, H - w_c\}$ and the optional interval $I_{c,k}$, as before; $e_c \in \{w_c, \dots, H\} \cup \{H + D\}$, the week the trim ends, or $H + D$; ← this lesson $\rho_c \in \{0, \dots, R_c(H + D)\}$, the risk $c$ carries. ← this lesson

Objective. ← this lesson

Constraints.

The two implications are enforced constraints (lesson Native logic: AddBoolOr, AddImplication, OnlyEnforceIf, only_enforce_if): a linear equality that must hold only when its literal is true. A circuit is either trimmed by exactly one crew, which fixes its end week, or not trimmed, which puts its end at $H + D$; the domain of $e_c$ allows nothing in between.

TrimModel gains three variable families, trimmed, end and carried, and the old trimmed method becomes a dict of Booleans, because the waits row needs a literal to negate, and a sum of presences is not one. All three are keyed by circuit id:

trimmed[c] is $t_c$, end[c] is $e_c$ and carried[c] is $\rho_c$; present, start and work are as before. build before (the lesson A trim takes weeks on one crew: from MILP to CP-SAT intervals) had one block per circuit for its crew options; after, each circuit also gets its end week, its two enforced rows and its table. The variables come first, with deferred set to h + DEFER_WEEKS, which is $H + D$:

The rows that tie the end week to the schedule and the risk to the end week are the rest of the loop, and the page leaves them to the practice. They are four rows of The Model above, each marked with its label in a comment:

  • (ends$_{c,k}$), inside the crew loop, right after the interval: $e_c = s_{c,k} + w_c$, enforced only when $p_{c,k}$ is true (only_enforce_if, lesson Native logic: AddBoolOr, AddImplication, OnlyEnforceIf);
  • (once$_c$), after the crew loop: the circuit's presence literals over its options sum to $t_c$, no longer "at most one";
  • (waits$_c$): $e_c = H + D$, enforced only when $t_c$ is false (the negated literal ~t);
  • (risk$_c$): the table $R_c$ as a list of c.carried(week) for every week from 0 to $H + D$, the variable $\rho_c$ in tm.carried from 0 to the table's last entry (named r_<cid>), and CP-SAT's element constraint add_element(index, table, target) (lesson Element, table and allowed-assignment constraints), which makes $\rho_c$ the table's entry at $e_c$.

The once row could not stay add_at_most_one: with an equality to $t_c$, the literal the waits row negates is true exactly when one crew trims the circuit.

Domain.from_intervals([[w, H], [H + D, H + D]]) gives $e_c$ its two-piece domain directly, so "ends in the season or waits" is a fact of the variable, not a row. A circuit too long for the season gets no options; once then reads $0 = t_c$, and the circuit waits.

For such a circuit the first piece of the domain is inverted: $w_c > H$, so $[w_c, H]$ holds no week. Domain.from_intervals drops an empty piece, so the domain is the one value $H + D$, and the model is valid. risk_plan.py builds the model of a one-month season with one 6-week circuit:

The end week's only value is week 30, validate() (which returns a description of what is wrong with a model, or an empty string) finds nothing, and the circuit waits, carrying its risk until next season ($1k a week for 30 weeks). The objective line becomes tm.model.minimize(sum(tm.carried.values())), and the budget row reads $t_c$.

make_plan changes one line: the plan records risk_carried=round(solver.objective_value).

vegcrew/report.py. The status line shows risk carried instead of risk removed.

The Small Season Gets a Better Plan, and a Harder Proof

On the small season the change is larger, and so is a new problem. The planner's 10 seconds are no longer enough to prove the best plan: the objective now depends on the order of the work, and orders are many. So that this page's numbers repeat, risk_plan.py solves the small season with one worker, a fixed seed and a limit of 20 units of deterministic time (lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs), rather than with the planner's own settings:

The new plan carries $3,989k against the old plan's $4,297k, $308k less, and it changes the set: C02 is in and C13 is out. C17, the fastest-growing large risk, now starts in week 0. The Step Added Twenty Lines and Changed the Model's Character

Twenty lines of code, and the tests grew by 43: the rule is written twice, once in data.py for the model and once in tests/rules.py for the checks, on purpose. The code's size hardly moved; its character did. Before, the model was a packing problem that CP-SAT proved optimal in well under a second on the small season. After, it is a scheduling problem with an order-dependent objective, and the proof does not finish. That is a property of the problem, not of the code, and it changes how the code must be run and tested from now on.

Problem statement

The crew schedule reached GridCap's reliability engineer:

Your plan trims C13 in the first five weeks and leaves C12 for weeks 17 to 23. C12 is the one I worry about: its trees are growing into the lines, and every week we wait its risk goes up. A trim in September does not take out a year of risk; it takes out what is left of the year, and the circuit carried the rest. Order the work so that we carry the least risk over the season. And a circuit we skip waits half a year more for next season's crews; count that too.

You start from the crew scheduler of the lesson before. Read each circuit's weekly growth of risk, and have the generator draw it. A circuit carries its weekly share of its yearly risk, plus its growth for every week it has waited, until its trim ends; a circuit left for next season carries it until 26 weeks past the season's end. Plan the least risk carried in total, within the budget and the crews, and report it. A trim that fills the whole season still fits.

The report. Only the status line changes: it ends risk carried $<risk carried>k, with a thousands comma, in place of risk removed $<risk removed>k a year. main.py still sets one worker.

build in vegcrew/planner.py

Summary


  • The old objective is blind to order. No start week appears in it, so it cannot prefer early work; only a new objective can.
  • Risk carried is a table indexed by the week a trim ends. CP-SAT's add_element holds the exact table; enforced equalities tie the end week to the chosen crew's interval or to next season.
  • Order now matters, and on the small season the plan carries $308k less than the knapsack-era plan, with C17 first.
  • The proof got harder: FEASIBLE with a 12.0% gap. Tests move to seasons small enough to prove.

Design Note: When the Objective Changes, the Old Tests Change Too


Two tests of the lesson The first request: which circuits to trim this year encoded the old objective: the brute force compared against the best risk removed, and its numbers were right only for that rule. Leaving it in place would have failed for a correct reason and taught the team to ignore failures. Rewriting it to the new rule, as a brute force over every order, kept its purpose (an answer computed without a solver) while changing its content. When the definition of "best" changes, review every test that knows what "best" means.
