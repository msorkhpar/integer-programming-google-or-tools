  16. From MILP to CP-SAT intervals

GridCap's crew supervisor booked the month plan of the lesson Crews and months: capacity over time and came back within a day:

Month 1 says C12, 7 crew-weeks, out of 8. That means both my crews on C12 for three and a half weeks each. I don't split a circuit between crews: the crew that starts a circuit finishes it, one week after another, and it hands nothing over. And C12 takes one crew seven weeks, so it can't sit inside one month anyway. Give me a crew and a start week for every trim.

Two facts break the month model at once: a trim belongs to one crew, and it occupies a run of consecutive weeks that can cross a month boundary. This lesson takes the project from steps/02 to steps/03 and, on the way, from a MILP solved by HiGHS to a CP-SAT model built from interval variables. (GridCap and its seasons are fictional, generated by the project's seeded generator.)

The Month Model Promises Work One Crew Cannot Do, and Refuses Work One Crew Can

The month model treats a month's crew-weeks as one pool. month_flaw.py asks both models about two hand-made seasons with a single 6-week trim, and lists every trim of the small season's month plan that only fits by splitting it across crews:

The model is wrong in both directions. With one crew and two months it refuses the trim, because no single month holds 6 crew-weeks, although one crew working weeks 0 to 5 does it easily. With two crews and one month it accepts the trim, because 6 is less than the month's 8, although no one crew can work 6 weeks in a 4-week month. On the small season, five of the nine trims are booked in a way the supervisor has just said is impossible. A model that is wrong in both directions cannot be fixed by tightening one row: the unit of the model, a month of pooled crew-weeks, is the wrong unit.

A Trim Is an Optional Interval on Each Crew, and a Crew's Intervals Never Overlap

What the supervisor described is a scheduling problem: jobs (trims) with fixed durations, machines (crews) that do one job at a time, and a choice of machine per job. Lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap built exactly this with CP-SAT interval variables. The design options:

OptionWhat it givesWhy not, or why
Keep the MILP, with one binary per circuit, crew and start week, and a row per crew and weekexact, and still HiGHS$20 \times 2 \times 24 = 960$ binaries on the small season and a row per crew-week; it grows with the horizon (lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap's design note weighs intervals against week-by-week Booleans)
A MILP with integer start times and big-M "either before or after" rows per pair of trims on a crewfew variablespairwise big-M rows with weak LP bounds (lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull)
CP-SAT: per circuit and crew, a Boolean "on this crew", a start week, and an optional interval tied to both; NoOverlap per crewthe supervisor's words, one constraint per word; the size does not grow with the number of weekschosen: CP-SAT reasons about intervals directly (lessons Interval variables and NoOverlap and When CP-SAT beats MIP, and when it doesn't)

An interval variable ties a start, a size and an end together (lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap). An optional interval also has a presence literal: when the literal is false the interval is "simply ignored by all constraints", in the words of the new_optional_fixed_size_interval_var docstring in the pinned OR-Tools. So each circuit gets one optional interval per crew, at most one of them is present, and NoOverlap on a crew's intervals (which, its docstring says, keeps all present intervals from overlapping in time) is the whole of "one trim at a time".

Changing solvers is a big step, and the requirement justifies it: the rows that remain (once, budget) are as easy in CP-SAT as in MathOpt, while the new rule is native to CP-SAT and awkward in a MILP. CP-SAT needs integer data, which this project already has: weeks, $k and crew counts are whole numbers.

The Model

The month model of the lesson Crews and months: capacity over time, before:

After, in full:

Sets. $C$, the circuits; $K$, the crews $\{K1, \dots\}$; weeks $0, \dots, H - 1$ with $H = 4 \times \text{months} = 24$. ← this lesson: crews are named, time is in weeks

Parameters. $\text{cost}_c$ ($k); $\text{risk}_c$ ($k a year); $B$ ($k); $w_c$, the weeks one crew takes to trim $c$. Only circuits with $w_c \le H$ get variables.

Variables. For every $c \in C$ and $k \in K$: $p_{c,k} \in \{0, 1\}$, 1 if crew $k$ trims $c$; $s_{c,k} \in \{0, \dots, H - w_c\}$, its first week; and the optional interval $I_{c,k} = [s_{c,k},\, s_{c,k} + w_c)$, present exactly when $p_{c,k} = 1$. ← this lesson: replaces $x_{c,m}$

Objective.

Constraints.

The crews$_m$ rows are gone: nothing pools crew-weeks any more. The season's end is now a bound on each start, $s_{c,k} \le H - w_c$, so every trim finishes inside the season. CP-SAT rows have no names here, so the code marks each with its label in a comment.

Every Trim Has a Crew and a Run of Weeks

crew_plan.py plans the small and tiny seasons with steps/03. (It sets the planner's WORKERS to 1 first, so the printed plan repeats from run to run; why four workers might not is the lesson The same plan every run: determinism and regression tests.)

The objective counts risk removed and nothing else, so on the tiny season any order of the three trims, with or without idle weeks between them, is as good as the one printed. Which one CP-SAT returns depends on the model as built, even with one worker. crew_plan.py builds the same model once more from the circuits in reverse order:

Another order, and an idle week 3. It is as correct a plan as the first, and keeps every rule; only the report differs. That is why this lesson's practice states the build order and the single worker its expected report comes from.

C12 runs on K1 for weeks 17 to 23, seven weeks straight; C04 crosses the month boundary at week 8. The plan is again the knapsack's nine circuits and its $2,114k a year: on this season the money binds and the crews, scheduled week by week, can do all of it. The month model's $5k loss in the lesson Crews and months: capacity over time was an artifact of pooling crews into months, not a real limit.

The model is also smaller than the month model's 120 binaries: 40 presence Booleans and 40 start weeks, one pair per circuit and crew, whatever the number of weeks. Its 63 constraints are the 40 intervals (CP-SAT counts each as a constraint), 20 once rows, 2 NoOverlaps and the budget.

The biggest change of the module so far left the code smaller. Interval variables say in one call what the MILP needed a family of rows for, and the HiGHS plumbing went with HiGHS. Problem statement

The crew supervisor booked the month plan and came back within a day:

Month 1 says C12, 7 crew-weeks, out of 8. That means both my crews on C12 for three and a half weeks each. I don't split a circuit between crews: the crew that starts a circuit finishes it, one week after another, and it hands nothing over. And C12 takes one crew seven weeks, so it can't sit inside one month anyway. Give me a crew and a start week for every trim.

You start from the month planner of the lesson before. Plan in weeks with CP-SAT instead: each trimmed circuit gets one crew and a run of consecutive weeks as long as the circuit's crew-weeks, inside the season, and no crew works two circuits in the same week. The budget still holds and the plan still removes the most risk. A trim may run across a month's end, and a circuit too long for the season is left out, never shared between crews. The report draws one line per crew, a character per week.

The report. The first line ends , crews <crews>, weeks <weeks> in place of the months, the weeks being the months times 4. The month lines go. After the status line comes one line per crew, K1 first: two spaces, the crew id, one space, the week chart, two spaces, and the crew's trims in order of first week, each as <cid>:<first>-<last> with both weeks included, one space apart. The chart has one character per week of the season: . while the crew is free, and the last character of the circuit id while it trims. The two spaces after the chart are printed even for a crew with no trims, so an idle crew's line ends in two spaces, here and in every later step.

Which order. The objective counts the risk removed whatever the order of the work, so the tiny season has several equally good plans, and the one CP-SAT returns depends on how the model is built and on how many workers search (the lesson shows the same model, built from the circuits in reverse order, giving another plan). expected/report.txt comes from one worker and this build order: for each circuit in the season's order that fits, and for each crew from K1 on, the presence literal, the start and the optional interval, then that circuit's at-most-one; after every circuit, one no-overlap per crew from K1 on, the budget row, and the objective. Keep TIME_LIMIT = 10.0 and WORKERS = 4 in planner.py, and have main.py set planner.WORKERS = 1 (after from vegcrew import planner) before it plans, as the lesson's main.py does. The tests accept any order.

Run executes main.py, which plans the tiny season (six circuits of GridCap, a fictional utility, drawn by vegcrew/generate.py from seed 54) and prints the report; expected/report.txt is the report it should print. Submit runs every test in tests/, the earlier steps' included, and compares Run's report with the expected one.

Summary


  • The month model is wrong in both directions. It refuses a 6-week trim one crew can do across two months, and accepts one that two crews could only do by splitting it.
  • A trim is an optional interval on each crew, and a crew's intervals never overlap. A presence Boolean, a start week and new_optional_fixed_size_interval_var per circuit and crew; add_at_most_one per circuit; add_no_overlap per crew.
  • Every trim has a crew and a run of weeks. The small season gets the knapsack's nine circuits back, scheduled on two crews.
  • Five new tests; the month tests retire with the months, and their rule lives on.

Design Note: Change the Solver When the Rule Changes Its Kind


The project changed solvers here, and only here. The knapsack and the month rows were linear inequalities over yes-or-no choices, the home ground of a MILP. "One crew, consecutive weeks, one job at a time" is a sequencing rule, and the honest MILP forms of it are either large (a binary per week) or weak (big-M pairs). When a requirement changes the kind of rule, it is worth asking whether the solver still fits; when it adds another rule of the same kind, it is not. Keeping Plan and Trim as the boundary made the switch cost one file.
