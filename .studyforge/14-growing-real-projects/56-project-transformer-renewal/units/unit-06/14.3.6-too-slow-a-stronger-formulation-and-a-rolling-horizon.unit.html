Request 6. Your plan is "within 2%" of the best. On 5,000 units that is about $100,000k of risk, more than the differences between the scenarios we want to compare next. We need plans within 0.1% of the bound, and the analysts need each one within fifteen minutes.

At 0.1% the Model Stops Being Fast, and the Gap Says Why

At 2%, the last lesson's 5,000-unit run finished in about 18 seconds. At 0.1% it is another model to solve. The run below (one run, on the machine described in the last lesson; your times will differ) gave HiGHS fifteen minutes:

After fifteen minutes the search stopped on its time limit (status FEASIBLE: a plan, not a proof of the target) at a gap of 0.115%, a little short of 0.1%. Nothing is wrong with the model's correctness; the question is why the solver needs so long to prove a plan good, and the first place to look is the LP relaxation, the bound the search starts from (lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound). stronger.py solves it for both fleet sizes:

The best plan the fifteen-minute search found removes $5,220,725k, and the search itself proved no plan removes more than $5,226,747k. The LP bound claims up to $5,518,227k is possible, 5.7% above that plan. Every point of that 5.7% is space the search must close by branching and cuts before it can stop at 0.1%.

The Aggregated Outage Row Lets the LP Buy a Fraction of an Outage

The outage lesson chose the aggregated link, one row per substation and year, and left a note: the per-unit form allows the same 0-1 plans and may have a tighter LP relaxation. Here is why it does. Take a substation with $n_s = 10$ candidates and replace one of them. The aggregated row reads

and with $x_{1,t} = 1$ and the rest 0 the LP is satisfied by $o_{s,t} = 0.1$, and by nothing less: one tenth of an outage, at one tenth of its $300k. The LP relaxation can replace one unit at a substation and pay $30k for the outage. The per-unit form writes one row for each unit,

and with $x_{1,t} = 1$ the LP must set $o_{s,t} = 1$ and pay the whole $300k. Each aggregated row is the sum of the ten per-unit rows, so every LP point the per-unit rows allow, the aggregated row allows too: the per-unit relaxation is contained in the aggregated one, and its bound can only be lower or equal (lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound). In integers they agree, because $o_{s,t}$ is 0 or 1 either way. The same comparison is in the big-M lessons: $n_s$ is the aggregated row's $M$, and the per-unit row is the same link with $M = 1$, the smallest $M$ a 0-1 variable allows (lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics).

Three times as many rows, the same variables, and an LP bound 5.3% lower at 5,000 units. The per-unit LP bound, $5,226,769k, is $22k above the bound of $5,226,747k that the aggregated search proved after fifteen minutes: the per-unit LP starts where the aggregated search ended.

The Design Decision: Strengthen the Formulation First, Then Decompose If Still Needed

Three levers were on the table, cheapest first:

LeverWhat it changesRisk
per-unit outage rowsthe formulation: a tighter LP bound, same 0-1 plansthree times the rows
a rolling horizon (lesson Rolling horizon and LNS heuristics)the method: solve a few years at a time, keep the firsta plan with no proof; end effects
more solver time or settingsnothing in the modelhides the cause

A stronger formulation is the cheapest and safest lever: it changes no plan, only how fast the solver can prove one. The project makes it the default (link="per-unit") and keeps the aggregated form as an option, because the reference builder and the tests of the scale lesson are written in it. The rolling horizon is built too, as the fallback if the strong form is still too slow, and measured against the same bound.

The Change, File by File

model.py: the link chooses its form. relax turns every variable continuous, for the LP bound:

and the outage block writes either form, chosen by the request's link. That block is yours to write in the practice. It stays inside the loops over by_substation and the plan years, and the outage variables, the region limit and the budget row are untouched:

  • link == "aggregated": the row of the scale lesson, $\text{outage}_{s,t}$ with $n_s$, named outage_<s>_<year>, unchanged, so the old model can still be built and measured.
  • Otherwise, the default "per-unit": the row $\text{outage}_{i,t}$ of the model above, one per candidate of the substation and plan year, $x_{i,t} \le o_{s,t}$, named outage_<tid>_<year>.

Each aggregated row is the sum of the per-unit rows of its substation and year (lesson Comparing formulations by their LP bound), so the two allow the same 0-1 plans, and the per-unit rows allow less in the LP relaxation.

request.py: the default becomes the strong form. The stub field of the last snapshot becomes real, with the new default; two stubs belong to the next lesson.

plan.py: the LP bound as a function. Tests and programs ask for it by name:

rolling.py: a rolling horizon. It follows lesson Rolling horizon and LNS heuristics, and its body is yours to write in the practice. rolling(fleet, request, window=3, keep=1) builds the whole model once, then walks the plan years from the first, keep years at a time:

  • Each window covers the years $[a, a + \text{window})$, cut at the last plan year. Before its solve, set the bounds of every replacement and outage variable (both are keyed by a pair whose second item is the year): a variable an earlier window decided is fixed to its kept value, one after the window's last year is fixed to 0, and the rest are free between 0 and 1.
  • Solve with the request's gap, as plan does. If the solve finds no plan, return that result's Plan at once.
  • Keep the values of the first keep years of the window, rounded to 0 or 1, as decided. The window that reaches the last plan year keeps all its years and ends the walk, and its Plan is what rolling returns.

Budget, crew and spares rows stay in for every year, so what a window decides is counted against all of them.

A rolling plan's value is exact, but the bound it returns is only the last window's, which says nothing about the whole plan. It is judged against the best bound known from a full solve.

check.py: every rule, checked apart from the model. A heuristic plan is not proved by a solver, so it gets checked by code that does not share the model's rows: violations recomputes budgets, outages, crews and spares from the plan in plain Python and returns one line per broken rule (the section 12 lesson Property-Based Tests With Hypothesis checks plans the same way).

Measured: the Strong Form Meets the Target; the Rolling Horizon Is Fast and Misses It

stronger_timing.py --full ran each method once on the 5,000-unit fleet, with a 0.1% gap, a fifteen-minute limit for the full solves, and a 0.1% gap in each rolling window. The times are from one run on the machine of the last lesson and change from run to run; the plan values and bounds are the solver's:

The per-unit form reaches the 0.1% target, status OPTIMAL at a gap of 0.082%, in about nine minutes, inside finance's fifteen; the aggregated form had not reached it when its fifteen minutes ran out. Both runs end at the same best bound, $5,226,747k: the per-unit LP relaxation starts there, the aggregated search has to work its way down to it. The plans differ by $1,748k, less than the gap of either, so the two forms' plans are equally good as far as these runs can tell; what differs is that one of them is proved within the target.

The rolling horizons are another kind of answer. They finish in 25 and 33 seconds, about 16 to 21 times faster, every plan keeps every rule (violations found none), and they land 0.65% and 0.36% below the best bound. Longer windows do better, as lesson Rolling horizon and LNS heuristics found: each window ends its view of the future at its last year, and a longer window sees more. Neither meets finance's 0.1%, so for this request the rolling horizon is not the answer. It stays in the project as the tool for what it is good at: a first plan in half a minute while the full solve runs, or a horizon too long to solve at once.

The Tests Pin the Bound, the Same Optimum, and the Rolling Plan's Rules

  • per-unit outage rows tighten the LP bound and keep the optimum: on four units at two substations, the per-unit LP bound is lower than the aggregated one, and both forms' exact plans have the same value.
  • a rolling plan keeps every rule and most of the value: on a 30-unit fleet with every rule, a rolling plan with two-year windows breaks no rule according to violations, and keeps at least 90% of the full model's value.
  • one window over the whole plan is the full model: with a window as long as the plan, the rolling horizon solves once and its value equals the full model's.
  • the checker names each broken rule and passes a good plan: a hand-made plan over budget in 2027, over the north's crews in 2028 and using a large spare that does not exist gets exactly those three lines; a good plan gets none.

The scale lesson's reference-model test still passes: its request pins link="aggregated", the form the reference builder writes, so the default's change does not touch it.

Blueprint

Write each method below. The tests check each one by itself and the whole program end to end, so you may change any other part of the code as you see fit.

build in renewal/model.py

  • Contract. As before, except the outage rows: with request.link == "aggregated" the row outage_<s>_<year> of the step before; otherwise ("per-unit", now Request's default) a row outage_<tid>_<year> per candidate and year, x[tid, year] <= out[s, year].
  • Modeling idea. Each aggregated row is the sum of the per-unit rows, so the per-unit form allows less in the LP relaxation and gives a tighter bound with the same integer plans.
  • Taught in 6.7.1 Comparing Formulations by Their LP Bound.

relax in renewal/model.py

  • Contract. The LP relaxation, in place: every variable of the model made continuous within its bounds; returns the same Built.
  • Modeling idea. Relaxing integrality gives the bound a branch-and-bound search starts from.
  • Taught in 6.7.1 Comparing Formulations by Their LP Bound.

lp_bound in renewal/plan.py

  • Contract. Builds the model, relaxes it, solves it, and returns the objective value: an upper bound on the risk any plan removes.
  • Modeling idea. Compare two formulations of one problem by their LP bounds: the lower the bound of a maximization, the stronger the form.
  • Taught in 6.7.1 Comparing Formulations by Their LP Bound.

violations in renewal/check.py

  • Contract. One line per broken rule, each starting with its rule's name and the offending key, in this order. First, per replaced unit in id order: <tid>: <year> is outside the plan when the year is not a plan year, and <tid>: younger than <min_age>, not a candidate when the unit is under the age screen. Then, per year in year order: budget <year>: <spend> > <budget>, with the spend of report.spend_by_year including the outage cost. Then, when request.outages is set, sorted by region and then year: outages <region> <year>: <count> > <outages>, where <count> is the number of the region's substations with an outage that year (report.outages). Then, when request.crew_weeks is set, sorted by region and then year: crews <region> <year>: <weeks> > <crew_weeks>, where <weeks> is the summed crew-weeks of the region's replacements that year. Last, when request.lead_times is set, per kind in KINDS order: spares <kind>: <early> > <stock>, a kind with no spares listed having a stock of 0. Every number is printed as a plain integer. An empty list when the plan keeps every rule.
  • Modeling idea. A checker that shares no code with the model catches a row the model lost.
  • Taught in 12.3.1 Unit-Testing One Constraint at a Time.

rolling in renewal/rolling.py

  • Contract. Builds the model once; from the first plan year, solves the years [a, a + window) with earlier years' replacement and outage variables fixed to their kept values and later years' fixed to 0, keeps the first keep years (every year of the last window), and moves on by keep years. Returns the last solve's Plan, or the first failed solve's.
  • Modeling idea. A rolling horizon trades the bound for speed: each window is a small model, and every row still counts what earlier windows decided.
  • Taught in 11.3.5 Rolling Horizon and LNS Heuristics.

Summary


  • A solve that is slow to prove is first a question about the LP bound. The aggregated outage row let the LP buy a tenth of an outage, and its bound sat more than 5% above every plan.
  • The per-unit link allows the same 0-1 plans and a smaller LP region, so its bound is lower; at 5,000 units it starts where the aggregated search ended after fifteen minutes.
  • A rolling horizon is a fallback with no proof. Judge it against the best bound known, and check its plan with code that does not share the model's rows.

Design Note: Strengthen Before You Decompose


A decomposition or heuristic changes what the answer means: a plan without a proof, judged against a bound from elsewhere. A stronger formulation changes nothing about the answer and only makes the proof cheaper. So the order is fixed: measure the LP bound against the best plan, strengthen the rows that leave the gap, and decompose only if the strong model is still too slow. Here the first step was enough, and the rolling horizon stays in the code, tested, for the next request that needs it.
