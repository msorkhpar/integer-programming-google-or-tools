With a plan proved close to the best, the asset-health team raised the question the plan rests on:

Request 7. The Weibull curves were fitted to few failures; the scales could be 15% off either way, and the shape too. Show us what the plan looks like if the curves are wrong, which units are in it whatever the curves, and give us one plan we can defend under all of them.

The code before this lesson is snapshot steps/06; after it, steps/07. The 5,000-unit fleet and its curves are invented (GridCap is fictional).

Every Coefficient Comes From the Curves, So the Plan Is Only as Good as Them

Every objective coefficient $\text{rr}_{i,t}$ is a sum of PoF differences read off a kind's curve (the ten-year lesson). A curve fitted to few failures is an estimate (lesson Probability of failure and hazard curves (Weibull)), and an error in $\eta$ moves every coefficient of that kind at once. The model so far has one set of curves built in, so it can answer only one question: the best plan if the curves are right. The request asks three others: how the plan changes, which part of it does not, and which plan to pick when the truth is not known.

A Scenario Is a Version of the Curves, and a Plan Is Valued Under Each

A scenario here is a version of every kind's curve: $\eta$ times a factor and $\beta$ times a factor, with a weight for how much the asset team believes it. The program uses four:

Scenario$\eta$ factor$\beta$ factorweightMeaning
base112the fitted curves
younger0.8511units fail about 15% younger
older1.1511units last about 15% longer
steeper11.251failures bunch closer to $\eta$

The coefficients under scenario $s$ are $\text{rr}^s_{i,t}$, the same formula on the scenario's curves. A finished plan can then be valued under any scenario by adding up its coefficients: that is exact arithmetic, no solver involved, and it is how plans are compared.

The Design Decision: A Plan per Scenario, an Expected-Value Plan, or a Worst-Case Plan

ObjectiveSaysModel change
one plan per scenariothe best plan if that scenario is truecoefficients only
expected valuethe best plan on average, by the weightscoefficients: the weighted mean $\sum_s p_s \text{rr}^s_{i,t}$
worst case (max-min)the plan whose worst scenario is besta variable $z$ and one row per scenario

The project builds all three, because the request asks for all three things: per-scenario plans show what changes, and the two combined plans are the candidates to defend. The expected-value objective needs no new rows: the mean of linear objectives is linear, so it is one set of coefficients. The worst case is not a mean; it is a minimum of several linear objectives, and the standard way to maximize a minimum is a new variable that every scenario's value must stay above.

A fourth option, two-stage stochastic programming, where some decisions wait until the truth is known, does not fit: the failure curves are not revealed during the plan, so there is no second stage to wait for.

Then either the weighted mean or the max-min, and that choice is yours to write in the practice; each branch is one objective of the model above:

  • request.objective == "worst": a continuous variable $z$, worst, from 0 to $\bar z$; one row $\text{worst}_s$ per scenario, worst <= removed(rr) with that scenario's table, named worst_<scenario name>; and worst maximized. $\bar z$ comes from the tables: for each, the sum over units of the largest entry of the unit's row, a negative entry counted as 0, and then the smallest of those sums over the scenarios.

worst gets an upper bound from the data rather than none: every variable in this course has a domain, and a bound computed from the coefficients is the tightest one the data give (lesson Coefficient ranges and scaling).

The Plans Differ in Timing, Hardly in Value

scenarios.py solves the 5,000-unit fleet six times: once per scenario, once for the expected value and once for the worst case. One solve at the 0.1% target took about nine minutes in the last lesson, so six would take most of an hour; the study runs at a 0.5% gap, and it keeps that in mind when it reads its numbers. The first block is each solve:

The risk at stake depends heavily on the curves: if units fail 15% younger, the same budget removes $8,646,771k; if they last 15% longer, $3,338,510k. That is a statement about the fleet, not about the plan. The plan question is the next block: each plan (row) valued under each scenario (column), exactly, by evaluate, with its regret, how much less it removes than that scenario's own plan in the scenario where that shortfall is largest:

Read it with the gaps in hand. Every plan removes, in every scenario, within 0.33% of that scenario's own plan; the largest share is the "steeper" plan's under the base curves, $17,206k short of $5,215,720k. The largest regret in money, $25,923k for the "steeper" plan, is in the "younger" column: the younger plan removes $8,646,771k there and the steeper plan $8,620,848k, a shortfall of 0.30% of $8,646,771k. Each scenario's own plan was solved only to within 0.5%, so none of these differences is evidence that one plan is better. The table even shows it directly: the worst-case plan removes more than the base plan does under the base curves, a negative regret, which an exact solve could not give. The base plan was not the best base plan; its solve stopped at 0.211%, and the worst-case solve happened to find a better one.

So, on this fleet, the uncertainty in the curves barely changes what the plan is worth. It changes which units go when:

1,474 units are in all four scenario plans: that is the core the asset team asked for, units worth replacing whatever the curves. 131 more are in some plans and not others: the margin. And many units change year without leaving the plan: 529 against the base plan under the steeper curves, the most of any scenario.

What to defend? The expected-value plan stays closest to the base plan (180 units placed differently) and its regret is within the noise; the worst-case plan gives up nothing measurable in any scenario here. Either is defensible on these numbers; the honest summary for the asset team is the core of 1,474 units, the margin of 131, and the fact that no plan in the table is distinguishable from another in value at this tolerance. Telling them which plan is "best" would need gaps much smaller than the regrets, and the regrets here are smaller than any gap this fleet can be solved to six times in a morning.

The Tests Pin the Scenario Curve, the Weighted Mean and the Max-Min

  • a curve that fails younger makes a replacement worth more: a scenario with factors of 1 gives the base coefficient exactly; an $\eta$ factor of 0.85 gives more, 1.15 less.
  • the expected objective is the weighted mean over scenarios: on three units, the plan's objective equals $(1 \cdot v_{\text{younger}} + 3 \cdot v_{\text{older}})/4$, with each $v$ computed by evaluate, outside the model.
  • the worst-case plan does best in its worst scenario: its objective equals its lowest value across the scenarios, and that lowest value is at least the expected-value plan's lowest.

The last snapshot accepts a scenario and ignores it, so the "younger" coefficient equals the base one, and its stub evaluate returns 0, so neither objective can match a value computed from it.

What the Step Did to the Code

24 code lines and one branch in build; the file count rises by two only because the next lesson's stubs arrive. Problem statement

The asset-health team saw the plans and sent the seventh request:

Request 7. The Weibull curves were fitted to few failures; the scales could be 15% off either way, and the shape too. Show us what the plan looks like if the curves are wrong, which units are in it whatever the curves, and give us one plan we can defend under all of them.

You start from the stronger model. A scenario is a version of the curves: every kind's Weibull scale and shape times its factors. Value a replacement under a scenario, value a finished plan under one, and let the request plan over several: by default for their weighted mean, with each scenario's weight over the total, and with objective="worst" for the worst of them, the most risk removed in the scenario where the plan removes least.

evaluate in renewal/risk.py

build in renewal/model.py

  • Contract. Summary


  • The coefficients come from the curves, so a scenario is a version of the curves, and a finished plan is valued under each by exact arithmetic.
  • An expected-value objective is one set of coefficients; a worst-case objective is a variable and a row per scenario. Per-scenario plans show what moves.
  • Read a regret table with the gaps in hand. Here every plan was within 0.33% of the best in every scenario, inside the 0.5% gap: the curves change the plan's timing, not, measurably, its value.
  • The answer to "what if the curves are wrong" is a core and a margin: 1,474 units in every plan, 131 in some.

Design Note: Value Plans Outside the Model


Every comparison in this lesson uses evaluate, a plain sum over a finished plan, never a solver's objective. Objectives come from different models (a mean, a minimum, one scenario) and from searches stopped at different gaps; a plan's value under a scenario is one number, the same however the plan was found. Comparing plans by evaluating them, not by their solves' objectives, is what makes a table like the one above mean anything at all.
