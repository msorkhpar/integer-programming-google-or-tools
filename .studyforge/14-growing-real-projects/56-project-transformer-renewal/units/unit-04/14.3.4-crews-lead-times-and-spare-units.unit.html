The bundled plan went to the regional managers and to procurement. Both had a problem with it:

Request 4. Each region has 12 crew-weeks a year for transformer work. A small unit takes 2 crew-weeks to swap, a medium one 4, a large one 8. And new units are not on a shelf: a medium unit takes a year from order to delivery and a large one three, so nothing ordered now arrives before 2028 or 2030. Until then, a replacement must come from the spares we hold: two medium units and one large. Small units are stocked by the supplier and arrive within the year.

The code before this lesson is snapshot steps/03; after it, steps/04. The fleet is the invented 500-unit one, and the 12 crew-weeks, lead times and spare counts are invented with it.

The Bundled Plan Needs More Crews Than Some Regions Have

crews_and_spares.py counts what the bundled plan of the last lesson asks of each region:

In 11 of the 40 region-years (four regions, ten years) the plan needs more than 12 crew-weeks, and one region-year needs 26, more than twice what the region has. The model never heard of crews, so this is not a surprise; it is the reason for the request. The lead times happen not to be broken by this plan (one medium unit before 2028, within the two spares), but nothing in the model makes sure of it, and a different budget or fleet would break them.

Each Resource Is a Row: Crews per Region and Year, Spares per Kind

Crews are a capacity per region and year, used by each replacement according to its kind. That is a knapsack row, one per region and year, written on the same $x_{i,t}$ as the budget: $\sum_{i \in I_g} w_{k(i)} x_{i,t} \le C$, with $w$ the crew-weeks of a kind and $C$ the region's crew-weeks a year.

Lead times need a design decision. A unit of kind $k$ ordered in 2027 arrives in $y_0 + L_k$. Three ways to say it:

OptionRowsAllows
cut the domain: no $x_{i,t}$ for $t < y_0 + L_{k(i)}$noneno early replacement at all; ignores spares
one row per kind: early replacements $\le$ sparesone per kindearly replacements up to the spares held
stock over time: spares, orders and deliveries as variables per kind and yearseveral per kind and yearrestocking, failures that use spares

The first is the simplest, and wrong: the utility holds spares precisely so that it can replace a unit early. The third is the full inventory model. It is the right one once spares are reordered and emergencies draw on them, and it adds a stock variable per kind and year (lesson Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps's carry-over is the same pattern). Procurement's request is about the units in stock today, so the project takes the second: one row per kind, counting the replacements that happen before that kind's lead time has passed. A small unit has a lead time of 0 and gets no row.

The Model

The model with crews and spares. Changes from the last lesson are marked.

Sets. $I$, the candidates; $Y$, the plan years; $W$, the risk window; $S$ and $I_s$, substations and their candidates; $G$, the regions, with $S_g$ their substations and $I_g$ their candidates $\leftarrow$; $K$, the kinds, with $I_k$ the candidates of kind $k$ $\leftarrow$; $E_k = \{ t \in Y : t < y_0 + L_k \}$, the years before a new unit of kind $k$ can arrive $\leftarrow$.

Parameters. $\text{rr}_{i,t}$, $c_i$, $B$, $F$, $O$, $n_s$ as before; $w_k$, crew-weeks to replace a unit of kind $k$ $\leftarrow$; $C$, crew-weeks per region and year $\leftarrow$; $L_k$, lead time of kind $k$ in years $\leftarrow$; $\sigma_k$, spare units of kind $k$ in stock $\leftarrow$.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for $i \in I$, $t \in Y$; $o_{s,t} \in \{0,1\}$ for $s \in S$, $t \in Y$. No new variables.

Objective.

Constraints.

The data: $w$ is 2, 4 and 8 for small, medium and large; $L$ is 0, 1 and 3; $\sigma$ is 2 medium and 1 large; $C = 12$. So $E_{\text{medium}} = \{2027\}$, $E_{\text{large}} = \{2027, 2028, 2029\}$, and small units have no spares row. The code names the rows crews_north_2029 and spares_large.

The Change, File by File

fleet.py needs no change. KINDS already held each kind's crew-weeks and lead time; the skeleton's author put them next to costs and curves, where the generator could see them. Data that describes a kind lives with the kind, so a rule that needs it does not need a new table.

request.py: three stubs become real. crew_weeks is $C$, lead_times switches the spares rows on, and spares is $\sigma$. None, False and an empty dict keep the last lesson's model. The stub scaled is the next lesson's.

lead_times is a separate switch, not "spares were given": a request with lead times and no spares must refuse every early medium and large replacement, and an empty spares dict says exactly that.

model.py: two row families. Each block is one line of the formulation, and both go after the outage blocks, before the budget rows. The crews block, $\text{crews}_{g,t}$, is yours to write in the practice:

  • Only when request.crew_weeks is not None. For each region in REGIONS and each plan year, the sum over the region's candidates of $w_{k(i)} \, x_{i,t}$, where $w_k$ is KINDS[t.kind].crew_weeks, at most request.crew_weeks, named crews_<region>_<year>.
  • A region with no candidates gets no row: its sum would be an empty sum, the number 0, which MathOpt refuses as a row. It is the same guard the region-limit block of the last lesson needs.

The spares block collects the early variables of one kind, $x_{i,t}$ for $i \in I_k$ and $t \in E_k$, and caps their sum:

if early: skips a kind with no early years, small, where the list is empty. Written plainly, the crews block finds a region's candidates by scanning every candidate inside its loops over regions and years: the same habit as the last lesson's outage block, now in a third place.

Crews Reshape the Plan; the Spares Rows Do Not Bind on This Fleet

With crews in the model, no region-year goes over 12 crew-weeks, and the busiest uses exactly 12. The plan replaces two more units than before, 142: 102 small, 37 medium and only 3 large, the first of them in 2034. A large unit uses 8 of a region's 12 crew-weeks, so a year that replaces one in a region has room for at most two small units more there.

The spares rows change nothing here: the plan wants no early large units and one early medium one, within the stock of two. That is a finding about this fleet and this budget, not about the rule; the tests below show the rows working where they bind. As in the last lesson, the drop in risk removed, $3,770k, is inside both runs' 2% gaps, so the runs do not show what crews cost. What they do show is the plan that keeps the new rules.

The Tests Pin Each Resource Where It Alone Binds

  • a region has only its crew-weeks each year: with 6 crew-weeks, a small and a medium unit in the north (2 + 4) and a small one in the south are allowed; a second small one in the north (8) is refused.
  • short crews push work into later years: two small units, 2 crew-weeks a year: one in 2027, one in 2028, though money allows both at once.
  • a unit needed before its lead time must be a spare: with no large spares, a large unit may be replaced in 2030 but not in 2029; with one spare, one large unit in 2027 is allowed, and a second in 2029 is refused.

The old model allows the over-crewed list, replaces both units in 2027, and allows a large unit three years before it can arrive. Each test fails on the first assertion the old code gets wrong.

What the Step Did to the Code

build now branches 23 ways, and the package has 26 more code lines; the file count rises by one only because the next lesson's stub module, reference.py, arrives. Every rule is still one block of build, in the formulation's order, which keeps the code easy to check against the model. But build has become the place where every request lands, and three of its blocks scan the whole candidate list inside a loop. On 211 candidates the 500-unit plan solves in seconds and the scans do not show. The next request is for the whole fleet.

Practice. This lesson's project practice starts from the code before this step, the whole package (snapshot steps/03). It is graded by the tests in the practice's tests/ folder and by a check that main.py prints the practice's expected/report.txt; the practice's statement gives the request main.py makes and the format of every line it prints. This step's main.py plans a tiny fleet of 25 units, drawn from seed 56, over five years, with outages, crews and spares.

You start from the model with outages. When the request gives crew-weeks, keep each region's crew work in each year within them. When it asks for lead times, a replacement of a kind before its first delivery year (2027 plus the kind's lead time in years) uses a spare, so those replacements of each kind must not outnumber the spares held of it, and a kind the request lists no spares for has none.

Summary


  • Each limited resource is a row on the same variables: crew-weeks per region and year, as a knapsack row weighted by kind.
  • Lead times with spares are a count: the replacements before a kind's lead time has passed may not exceed the spares in stock. Cutting the domain would forbid spares; a full stock model is for when spares are reordered.
  • Crews reshaped the plan (no region-year over 12 crew-weeks) and moved it toward smaller units; the spares rows did not bind on this fleet, and the tests show them binding where they should.
  • A difference in value inside both runs' gaps is not a measured cost.

Design Note: A Switch for Lead Times, Not a Sentinel in the Spares


lead_times: bool and spares: dict look redundant: why not "lead times apply when spares are given"? Because "no spares" is a real answer procurement can give, and it must mean "no early replacements", not "ignore lead times". When a missing value and a zero mean different things, give the difference its own field.
