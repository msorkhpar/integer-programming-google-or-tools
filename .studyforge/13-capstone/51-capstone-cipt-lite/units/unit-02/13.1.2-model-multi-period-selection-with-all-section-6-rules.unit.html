The snapshot of lesson Data: load, validate and snapshot the portfolio is accepted, and the planners ask the question CIPT exists for: which of GridCap medium's 400 projects should start, and in which of the ten years, to get the most NPV while every business rule holds? Section 6 wrote each rule on its own, often on a model that had only that rule. The milestone puts every rule GridCap's data carries into one model, gives every row the name the planners will read in a diagnosis, and then asks how anyone can trust what the model returns: the solve stops at a stated gap, the plan is checked by code that never sees the model, and hand-built plans test each rule from both sides. GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio.

The Model

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan years (2027 to 2036); $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years of project $i$, those in its window whose every year of work falls inside $Y$ (lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows); $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set $g \subseteq P$ (lesson Mutually exclusive alternatives (repair vs. replace)); $C \subseteq P$ the children, each with its parent $p(c)$; $R$ the regions and $P_r$ the projects in region $r$; $K$ the programs (transmission, distribution, vegetation) and $P_k$ the projects in program $k$; from the scenario, $F$ the projects forced in, $O$ the projects forced out, and $S$ the projects whose start year $t_i$ is fixed (lesson Manual overrides as fixed bounds).

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ the NPV of project $i$ if it starts in $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$ what it spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k), with $d_i$ years of work and $\text{capex}_i = \sum_k c_{i,k}$; $L_c$ the lag between parent and child (years); $B_y$ the year's budget and $B_{r,y}$ the region's ($k); $m_i$ the crew-months project $i$ uses in each year of work and $W_{k,y}$ the crew-months program $k$ has in year $y$; $\Phi_r$ the least region $r$ must receive over the plan ($k), a rate-case commitment (lesson Jurisdictional and portfolio minimum/maximum spend).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. Write $s_i = \sum_{t \in T_i} x_{i,t}$ (1 if the project is funded) and $\text{spend}_{i,y} = \sum_{t \in T_i,\; 0 \le y - t < d_i} c_{i,\,y-t}\, x_{i,t}$ (lesson Cash-flow spreading across years). Overrides are bounds: $x_{i,t} = 1$ if $t = t_i$ and $0$ otherwise for $i \in S$, and $x_{i,t} = 0$ for $i \in O$.

Objective.

Constraints.

The precedence row is the cumulative form of lesson Aggregated vs. cumulative (disaggregated) forms: by every year $t$, the child may have started only if its parent started at least $L_c$ years earlier. Each row's code name is its label with the indices filled in: must_P097, after_P012_2035, region_Coast_2031, crews_transmission_2029.

Every Rule GridCap's Data Carries Is a Named Row

The model has exactly the rules the snapshot has data for. Section 6 taught more forms (carry-over budgets in Carry-over budgets and multi-year caps, piecewise costs in Convex piecewise costs without binaries to Incremental and logarithmic formulations, symmetry rows in Symmetry and how to break it), and each needs data GridCap's export does not carry: a carry-over rate, a cost curve, identical assets. A rule without its data would be a row with invented numbers, so CIPT Lite leaves them out and says so.

A Run Stops at a Stated Gap, and Its Plan Is Checked Without the Model

How long to let HiGHS search is a decision, and CIPT Lite states it once, as its run setting: a relative gap of 0.1% and one thread.

HIGHS sets threads to 1 in HiGHS's own options, because HiGHS through MathOpt refuses the common threads parameter and fixes its count at the first solve of a process (lesson Seeds, threads and deterministic work limits, solver by solver). One thread is what lets milestone 5 promise the same plan on every run. The gap is the stopping rule of lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count: stop once the plan is proved within 0.1% of the best possible. Here is the run, and the same model solved to a zero gap for comparison:

OPTIMAL under a gap means "proved within the gap", not "the best plan" (lesson What "optimal at 0.01%" means on a $2B portfolio). The run setting's plan is $154k below the plan the zero-gap solve finds, and the zero-gap solve searched 26,516 nodes against 791 to prove that last 0.07%. On a $219,000k portfolio whose inputs are forecasts, CIPT Lite takes the 0.1% and reports the bound beside the plan. So "the objective equals the reference within tolerance" has a precise meaning in this milestone: the run's NPV is at least the reference times $(1 - 0.001)$.

The second line is the other half of trust. check reads only the snapshot, the scenario and the plan, and recomputes every rule from the data:

It returns the names of the rows the plan breaks, in the model's own names. A wrong row in build cannot approve its own plan, because check never looks at the rows (lesson Property-based tests with Hypothesis). Every milestone from here on runs check on every plan it reports, and the service of milestone 6 refuses to return a plan that fails it.

Problem statement

Milestone 2's solver hands back a plan, and the planning team will not take the solver's word that it is valid: a wrong row would approve its own plan. You write the checker that reads only the practice snapshot and the plan, with no model and no solver, and names every row the plan breaks. Milestone 5's properties will call it on hundreds of plans.

A plan is {project id: start year}. Project $j$ started in year $t$ spends cost[k] in year $t + k$ and keeps its crews crew-months busy in each of those years; years past the horizon are not counted. A region's floor is met by the whole cost (sum(cost)) of its funded projects.

Hand-Built Plans Test Each Rule From Both Sides


A solve tests the model at one point: the plan it returned. Lesson Unit-testing one constraint at a time showed that a rule can be wrong and still leave that plan unchanged, so the model needs testing on plans chosen by hand. allows fixes every start variable by its bounds (1 for each start in the plan, 0 for every other) and asks HiGHS whether the fixed model is feasible. A start the model has no variable for is refused before any solve. model_medium.py starts from the solver's plan and changes it so that one rule breaks (the fewest rows it can, searched from the data), then asks both the model and the checker:



Read the table from both sides. The first row is the allowed side: a valid plan must pass, or a rule is too strict, which no check of the solver's own plans can reveal. The other rows are the refused side, each naming the rule it targets. A row made stricter still refuses every plan built to break it, so the refused side cannot catch a rule that is too strict. P003's row breaks two rules: of all the additions of one alternative in one start year that the program tried, none broke the alternative row alone, and this one, which also needs a distribution crew-month the plan has already used in 2027, was the best it found. The test is still sound: the model refuses, and the checker names the rule the row was built to break.


The model and the checker agree on all six. They are two implementations of the same rules, written from the formulation independently, so their agreement on plans built to break each rule is the evidence that both are right. When they disagree, one of them has a bug, and the plan in that row is the smallest case to debug.

Problem statement


Your capstone's planning model now meets the practice portfolio: the same fictional utility, the same snapshot format, and every rule its data carries. Build the whole model in MathOpt and solve it with HiGHS to a zero gap. The tests do not read your rows. They fix whole plans by their variable bounds and ask the model to accept or refuse each one, so any correct formulation passes, and they compare the best plan's NPV with the reference.


Write the formulation first, on paper or in a comment, in the order lesson Model: multi-period selection with all section 6 rules uses: the sets (projects $J$, years $Y$, start years $T_j$, regions, programs, alternative groups, children with a parent); the parameters with their units (cost and npv in $k, budgets, region budgets, crew-months, floors, lags); the variables with their domains; the objective with its sense; and every row with its quantifier and its label.


The starter gives you the scaffolding: variables creates $x_{j,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for each start year of each window (so a start outside a window has no variable), solve, best (the optimal value and plan) and allows (a plan fixed by bounds, then solved). Summary


  • Every rule GridCap's data carries is a named row. Once, must, force, alternatives, cumulative precedence, yearly, regional and crew limits, and regional floors: 887 variables and 543 rows on GridCap medium, each row named by its label.
  • A run stops at a stated gap, and its plan is checked without the model. One thread and a 0.1% gap: $219,054k proved within 0.1%, $154k below the zero-gap plan. check recomputes every rule from the data and found nothing broken.
  • Hand-built plans test each rule from both sides. The solver's plan must be allowed; plans built to break one rule must be refused; the model and the checker agree on all of them.

Design Note: Two Implementations of One Formulation


build and check are written from the same formulation and share no code. That duplication is deliberate. A test that solves the model and checks the plan with the model's own rows can only confirm the model agrees with itself. A checker that recomputes spend, crews and precedence from the data is a second reading of the formulation, and the hand-built plans are where the two readings meet. The cost is keeping both in step when a rule changes; the formulation block is the contract both follow.
