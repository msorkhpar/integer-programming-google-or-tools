The model of lesson Model: multi-period selection with all section 6 rules maximizes NPV, and finance is satisfied. The asset team is not: a plan chosen on NPV alone funds what pays back fastest, and leaves aging breakers and towers whose failures cost far more than their NPV says. The board wants the plan chosen on a weighted value of two benefits, NPV and the risk the work removes, and it wants to see where every coefficient came from. This milestone builds the three coefficient tables, one entry per start variable, from the snapshot: NPV by start year (section 7.2), risk reduction from each asset's failure curve and its predicted RUL (section 7.3), and the weighted value that combines them (section 7.4). Then it swaps the objective and shows what the rules let each objective buy. GridCap is a fictional utility; its benefit forecasts and failure curves are invented.

The Model

The model of lesson Model: multi-period selection with all section 6 rules, unchanged except for its objective: the same sets, parameters, variables $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$, and the same rows $(\text{once}_i)$, $(\text{must}_i)$, $(\text{force}_i)$, $(\text{alt}_g)$, $(\text{after}_{c,t})$, $(\text{budget}_y)$, $(\text{region}_{r,y})$, $(\text{crews}_{k,y})$ and $(\text{floor}_r)$, written out in full there. New parameters, built in this lesson: $\text{rr}_{i,t}$, the discounted risk removed over a ten-year window if project $i$ starts in $t$ ($k); references $R_{\text{npv}}$ and $R_{\text{risk}}$ ($k) and weights $w_{\text{npv}} + w_{\text{risk}} = 1$. Changed: the objective.

NPV by Start Year Is One Cash Flow, Shifted and Discounted to One Date

GridCap's export carries an NPV for every start year, and CIPT must not take it on trust: the milestone rebuilds it from the benefit forecast finance hands over and checks that the two agree. GridCap's forecast gives each project one benefit in its start year, $\rho_i\, \text{capex}_i$, which shrinks by a share $\delta_i$ for each year the project waits after its earliest start $e_i$ (the risk carried meanwhile). Lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year's rule: shift the whole cash flow to the start year, and discount every amount to one date, the plan's first year $y_0 = 2027$:

rounded to a whole $k, with GridCap's rate $r = 7\%$. In code, one line per term:

value_medium.py rebuilds the whole table. GridCap's generator drew each project's $\rho_i$ and $\delta_i$ and kept only the NPVs, so the program replays it to recover the forecast, as lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year did; a real finance team would send the two columns.

Every entry agrees, so the export's NPV column is exactly this calculation and the model may use either. P086 shows the shape every row has: each year of delay costs one year of discounting on the benefit and the costs, plus 5.1% of the benefit, so the NPV falls by $948k, $822k and $711k a year. Discounting every start to 2027 is what makes delay show its cost; valuing each start from its own year would make waiting look free (lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year).

Problem statement

The capstone's practice utility (a fictional one) has a finance team that forecasts benefits differently from GridCap. Instead of one benefit in the start year, each project earns a stream: one amount per year of service, starting in the year after its last year of work, and every amount is smaller by a factor $(1 - \delta_i)$ for each year the project starts later than its own earliest start. Your milestone-3 job is the coefficient table the model will use: the NPV of every project for every start year in its window.

For project $i$ with cost profile $c_{i,0}, \dots, c_{i,d_i-1}$ ($k, $d_i$ years of work), earliest start $e_i$, benefit stream $b_{i,0}, b_{i,1}, \dots$ and decay $\delta_i$, started in year $t$, with the plan's first year $y_0$ and rate $r$:

Write, in npv.py:

  • npv(project, forecast, start, years, rate): the formula above, as a float. years is the snapshot's list of plan years; years[0] is $y_0$. discount is given.
  • npv_table(snapshot, forecast): {(project id, t): whole $k} for every $t$ from earliest to latest, each value rounded to the nearest whole $k (math.floor(v + 0.5)).

The ideas come from lesson Precomputing NPV coefficients by start year (shift the cash flow to the start, discount every start to one date, count benefits to the end of the asset's life and not to the end of the plan) and lesson Present value, NPV and discount rates (present value). The tests use their own small portfolio; snapshot["projects"][i] has cost, earliest and latest, and forecast[i] has benefits (a list) and decay.

Risk Reduction Starts From the RUL, Made a Point on the Weibull Curve


Each asset has a Weibull failure curve with shape $\beta$ and a catalog scale $\eta$ for its kind, and a remaining useful life (RUL) that the asset-health model predicted for this asset. Lesson Remaining useful life: what an ML model actually hands you showed that the RUL alone does not fix the probability of failure, and how to use it: keep the fleet's shape, and choose the scale that makes the RUL the median remaining life of an asset of this age $a$:



The asset's yearly risk at age $u$ is its consequence of failure times the expected failures of that year, $\text{CoF}\,(\Lambda(u + 1) - \Lambda(u))$ with $\Lambda(u) = (u/\hat\eta)^\beta$ (lesson Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF). Work started in $t$ and lasting $d$ years leaves the asset aging until it ends; from year $t + d$ the asset is $\kappa$ times the age it had reached, with $\kappa = 0$ for a replacement and $0.5$ for a repair, and ages again (lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year). The coefficient adds the difference over a fixed ten-year window from 2027, discounted:



The window is fixed, not the plan's remaining years after the start, so that a late start is charged for the risk it carries in the meantime instead of being compared over a shorter period. P086's breaker bay, both ways:



The asset-health model says A086 is in better shape than its kind's catalog curve: a median of 16 more years needs a scale of 41.3, not 37.8, so every year's expected failures are lower and the replacement removes about a fifth less risk at every start. That is the point of anchoring: the coefficient uses what is known about this asset, not only what is known about its kind.

The maker's catalog also lists a scale eta, fitted to new assets. Milestone 3 needs the second coefficient table: the risk each project removes, for each start year.


The curve. Use the scale that makes the RUL the median remaining life at today's age (lesson Remaining useful life: what an ML model actually hands you), not the catalog's:



One year's risk. With failures repaired as they come, the expected failures between ages $u$ and $u+1$ are $(\tfrac{u+1}{\eta})^{\beta} - (\tfrac{u}{\eta})^{\beta}$, and the year's risk is $\text{cof}$ times that (lesson Consequence of failure and risk = PoF × CoF).


Risk removed. Work of $d$ years started in $t$ ends in $t+d$. Without it, the asset's age in year $y$ is $a + (y - y_0)$. With it, the age is the same until $t+d$; from then on it is keep times the age reached at $t+d$, plus $y - (t+d)$. The coefficient sums, over a fixed window of $W$ years from the plan's first year $y_0$ (even past the plan's last year), the yearly risk without the work less the yearly risk with it, discounted to $y_0$ at the snapshot's rate (lesson Risk reduction from an intervention, year by year).


Write, in risk.py, scale_from_rul(age, rul, beta), risk_removed(asset, start, duration, years, window, rate) (years is the plan's list of years, years[0] is $y_0$) and risk_table(snapshot, assets, window), which returns {(project id, t): risk removed} for every start in each window, rounded to 3 decimals. expected_failures and age_in are given; age_in with start=None is the age without the work. A project's duration is the length of its cost list.

Weighted Value Divides by Fixed References and Weights by Swing Ratings


NPV and risk removed are both in $k, but they are not the same dollars: NPV is money the utility gets back, risk is expected failure cost avoided, and the board does not trade them one for one. Lesson Normalizing different units's rule makes each a unitless score by dividing by a reference, the swing from no benefit to a benefit the board can picture, fixed before any project is scored: $5,000k of NPV and $2,000k of risk removed. Lesson Weights, swing weighting and stakeholder buy-in's swing weighting turns the board's ratings of the two swings into weights: risk 100, NPV 60, so each rating over their sum.




Read P086's 2027 value back into its parts: $0.375 \times 5{,}875/5{,}000 = 0.441$ from NPV and $0.625 \times 97.2/2{,}000 = 0.030$ from risk. Section 7.4 built a value function of four measures (reliability, safety, environment, finance); GridCap's export carries the inputs of two, so CIPT Lite's value function has two, and a third measure is one more term with its own reference and rating.


The last line is how the milestone is graded. A coefficient table is data, so it gets a digest like the snapshot: every value rounded to three decimals, the rows sorted, the SHA-256 of the result. A test pins the three digests, so any change to a formula, a reference or a weight is a failed test and a reviewed decision, never a silent shift in every plan.

Design Note: Coefficients Are Inputs, So They Get Digests


The model sees none of the formulas of this lesson; it sees 887 numbers per table. A changed discount rate, a new reference or a fixed bug in the RUL anchoring all reach the plan only through those numbers, so the tables are inputs exactly as the snapshot is. CIPT Lite therefore treats them the same way: computed from the snapshot by code under test, and named by a digest that a test pins. Milestone 5's run manifest records the snapshot's digest and the objective a run used; a run that solves on these tables records their digests beside it, so a plan can always be traced to the coefficients that chose it.
