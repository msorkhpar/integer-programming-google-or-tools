Lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull ordered three jobs that need the same substation outage in GridCap small's Coast region: P011 (2 months), P016 (3) and P021 (5), within a 12-month year, finishing the jobs as early as possible. "P011 before P016 or P016 before P011" is a disjunction, and that lesson wrote it twice by hand, as big-M rows and as the hull, in MathOpt, with LP bounds of 10.00 and 13.98 and an optimum of 17. Writing a form by hand is where a wrong M or a missing copy creeps in. Pyomo's GDP (Generalized Disjunctive Programming) extension lets you state the disjunction once, as logic, and have a transformation write either form for you. This lesson ports the outage model to GDP, checks both forms against lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's numbers, and shows what the transformations need from you. GridCap is fictional, and the outage reading of crew_months is lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's.

Lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull is in section 6, which the course reaches later, and you do not need it here: The Model below states the model in full, and the big-M and hull forms are explained where Pyomo writes them. "Lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's numbers" are the lines that lesson recorded from its two hand-written MathOpt models, each solved as a MILP (with HiGHS) and as its LP relaxation (with GLOP):

An LP bound is the optimum of the LP relaxation, the same model with each binary allowed anywhere in $[0, 1]$; for this minimization no plan can finish earlier than it. The module's tests rebuild lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's two MathOpt models and compare with them directly.

The Model

Sets. $J = \{\text{P011}, \text{P016}, \text{P021}\}$ the jobs; $\Pi = \{(i, j) \in J \times J : i \text{ before } j \text{ in the list}\}$ the pairs that share the outage.

Parameters. $d_j$, job $j$'s duration in months (2, 3, 5); $H = 12$ months.

Variables. $0 \le s_j \le H - d_j$, the month job $j$ starts (continuous), for $j \in J$. For each pair and each of its two disjuncts, a Boolean $Y$ that is true when that disjunct holds; Pyomo creates these (indicator_var).

Objective.

Constraints. For every pair, exactly one of two disjuncts holds:

$\veebar$ is "exactly one of". This is the whole model: no M, no binary, no copy. Those are what a transformation adds.

A Disjunct Holds the Rows of One Option, and a Disjunction Picks Exactly One

A disjunct is a block of rows that hold together or not at all (here, one row each); a disjunction groups disjuncts and says exactly one of them holds. The shortest way to write one in Pyomo is a Disjunction whose rule returns a list of disjuncts, each a list of rows:

Disjunction comes from pyomo.gdp. Indexed by m.pairs, it is one disjunction per pair, and Pyomo creates the disjuncts as Disjunct components behind it:

XOR: True is the "exactly one" of $\veebar$, Pyomo's default for a disjunction. Each disjunct has an indicator_var, a BooleanVar (true or false, not 0 or 1), which is how you read or fix the choice. The disjuncts can also be declared one by one as Disjunct components with their own constraints, and a disjunction built from them with Disjunction(expr=[m.d1, m.d2]); the list form above is the same thing, shorter.

No solver can read this model yet: a MILP solver knows rows and integer variables, not "exactly one of these blocks". A transformation rewrites it into a MILP, in place: pyo.TransformationFactory("gdp.bigm").apply_to(m).

gdp.bigm Writes the Big-M Rows and Finds M From the Bounds

The big-M transformation gives each disjunct a binary, binary_indicator_var, adds one row per disjunction saying the binaries sum to 1, and relaxes each disjunct's rows by an M when its binary is 0 (lesson Deriving big-M from bounds's switch). It computes each M from the variables' bounds, and you can read back what it chose:

Every row got $M = 12$, the value lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull derived by hand: with $s_i \le H - d_i$ and $s_j \ge 0$, the most $s_i + d_i - s_j$ can be is $H$. (Each row has only an upper side, so its lower M is None.) The transformed row is lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's big-M row, with Pyomo's binary in place of $y_{ij}$. The optimum is 17, shortest job first, and first(m) reads the order back from the indicator variables:

The M is only as good as the bounds. Remove one bound, and the transformation has nothing to compute an M from. Both transformations raise GDP_Error, an exception class that pyomo.gdp exports:

The first line is big-M's; the second is the hull's, which the next section explains.

You can then pass an M yourself, apply_to(m, bigM=20), and the model solves (to 17 here). But a hand-picked M is exactly lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics's risk: too small and it cuts off real plans, too large and the LP bound and the numerics suffer, and nothing checks it for you. Bound the variables from the data and let the transformation compute M.

Problem statement

A town's utility must carry a peak load through its substation transformers this winter. Each transformer is either repaired (cheaper, and it then carries a lower rating) or replaced (dearer, with a higher rating). Find the cheapest choice per transformer that carries the load.

In replace.py, write build(assets, demand) and plan(assets, demand). assets maps a name to Asset(repair, replace, repaired, new): the two costs ($k) and the two ratings (MVA). The model, with one disjunction per transformer:

Write the disjunction with pyomo.gdp.Disjunction (repair as its first disjunct, replace as its second) and name the objective m.total. In plan, turn it into a MILP with gdp.bigm, solve with appsi_highs at a zero mip_gap, and return the total cost and a dict from each transformer to "repair" or "replace", read from the disjuncts' indicator_var. The fleets in the tests range from a few hundred $k to a few thousand. About twenty-five lines.

gdp.hull Writes a Copy per Disjunct and Needs Every Variable Bounded


The hull transformation writes lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's other form: a copy of every variable a disjunct mentions, per disjunct, bounded by the variable's bounds times that disjunct's binary, and the original variable equal to the sum of its copies. One statement, two transformations, then each model is solved as a MILP and as its LP relaxation:






The LP bounds are lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's, 10.00 and 13.98, and the module's tests check both against the MathOpt models of that lesson to $10^{-6}$. The hull is tighter for the reason that lesson gave: each disjunction's LP region is the convex hull of its two orders. The sizes differ from the hand-written ones (6 variables and 6 rows for big-M, 18 and 24 for the hull) because Pyomo gives each disjunct its own binary, 6 instead of lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's one $y_{ij}$ per pair, and adds a row per pair making the two sum to 1. The hull's 21 variables are those 9 plus 12 copies: two starts in each of the six disjuncts.


The order of the two transformations matters when you want an LP bound. The binaries do not exist until the GDP transformation creates them, so relaxing first relaxes nothing:






That "LP bound" is the MILP optimum, solved with binaries intact. Transform, then relax.


The hull needs bounds even more than big-M does, because each copy's bound is the variable's bound times the switch. With the bound on $s_{P011}$ removed, the loop above printed for the hull too:

The trade-off is lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's: the hull is tighter and bigger; for a disjunction over one variable's intervals a tight big-M is already as tight; when disjuncts tie several variables together, compare the two LP bounds, which with GDP costs one argument. Problem statement


The planners who wrote the repair-or-replace model now want to know how strong its LP relaxation is in each of the two forms Pyomo can write, because a weak bound makes branch-and-bound slow on the utility's full fleet.


The starter has the model, build(assets, demand), with one Disjunction per transformer. Planner Rules Go on the Disjuncts' Booleans


The indicator variables are model variables like any other, so a planner's rule about the choices is written on them. Fixing one fixes a choice: the planners want P021, the longest job, out of the way first, before P011:




A rule that links choices is a LogicalConstraint over the Booleans, with .implies(...), &, |, ~ and the counting functions atleast, atmost and exactly. "If P021 goes before P011, it goes before P016 too":






With only the fixed choice, the best order is P016, P021, P011 (finishes 3, 8 and 10, sum 21): P021 goes before P011 but not first. With the logical rule, P021 must lead both, and the order is P021, P011, P016 (finishes 5, 7 and 10, sum 22). The same gdp.bigm call turned the logical rule into rows on the binaries; nothing else changed in the code. (solve in gdp_rules.py applies gdp.bigm, solves, and prints the objective, starts and order.)


Summary


  • A disjunct holds the rows of one option, and a disjunction picks exactly one. Disjunction(m.pairs, rule=...) returning a list of lists; each disjunct's indicator_var is a Boolean.
  • gdp.bigm writes the big-M rows and finds M from the bounds. M = 12 on every outage row, lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's value; no bound, no M (GDP_Error), and a hand-given bigM= is unchecked.
  • gdp.hull writes a copy per disjunct and needs every variable bounded. LP bounds 10.00 (big-M) and 13.98 (hull), equal to lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull's; transform before you relax.
  • Planner rules go on the disjuncts' Booleans. Fix an indicator_var, or write a LogicalConstraint; the transformation handles both.

Design Note: State the Logic, Choose the Form Later


Lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull kept the business rule and its form apart by hand (add_big_m, add_hull). GDP makes that separation the language: the model says "one of these orders holds", and one line chooses how a MILP solver sees it. The habit that goes with it is to compare forms by their LP bound and size before committing, which here was a loop over two transformation names.
