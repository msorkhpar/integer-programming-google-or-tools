Two requests reach the Pyomo model this week. First, GridCap's transmission projects on the small portfolio need more crew-months than GridCap's own crews have, and the rest must be bought from a contractor whose price per month rises by tier, so the cost of the shortfall is a piecewise-linear function. Second, the planners are testing deep cuts to the 2030 and 2031 budgets, and some of the scenarios come back infeasible; they want to know which rules conflict. This lesson covers Pyomo's tools for both: its piecewise components, and its tools for explaining an infeasible model, alongside MathOpt's compute_infeasible_subsystem of lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem. Section 6, later in the course, writes piecewise curves by hand in MathOpt (lessons Convex piecewise costs without binaries to Incremental and logarithmic formulations) and checks them against Pyomo's Piecewise (lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal); this lesson does not need them, and says what each idea it borrows from them means where it uses it. GridCap and its contractor are fictional.

Piecewise Writes y Below, Above or Equal to f(x), and the Side Must Match the Push

pyo.Piecewise(y, x, pw_pts=..., f_rule=..., pw_constr_type=..., pw_repn=...) adds rows that tie a variable y to a piecewise-linear function of a variable x. pw_pts are the breakpoints in x and f_rule the values there (a list, or a function, or a dict of lists for an indexed Piecewise). pw_constr_type says which relation the rows state: "UB" for $y \le f(x)$, "LB" for $y \ge f(x)$, "EQ" for $y = f(x)$. pw_repn names the formulation; lessons The lambda (SOS2-style) formulation built from binaries and Incremental and logarithmic formulations build "CC" (the lambda form), "INC" (incremental) and "LOG" by hand; the default is "SOS2".

contractor_tiers.py builds the model once per setting, with GridCap's crews covering 40 crew-months, and prints what the Piecewise component added, the plan's net value, the months bought, the cost variable, and the curve's true cost at those months:

Start with the last line. The objective subtracts $c$, so it pushes $c$ down: the model wants to pay as little as it can. "UB" states $c \le f(b)$, which puts no floor under $c$, and the solver took the cheapest value the variable's bounds allow, 0, while buying all 40 months, whose true cost is $1,150k. The net value of $25,787k is not a plan anyone can buy. The other discount line, "LB" ($c \ge f(b)$), pays the curve exactly: 26 months for $870k. The rule is lesson Convex piecewise costs without binaries's in Pyomo's words: a cost the objective pushes down needs "LB" (or "EQ"), a value it pushes up needs "UB" (or "EQ"). "EQ" is always exact, and costs binaries for every shape.

Pyomo only writes the rows; it does not know which way your objective pushes. Check the cost variable against the curve, as the curve column does: the two agree on every line but the wrong one.

The x variable needs both bounds, and they should be the breakpoints' range. The contractor model declares m.bought = pyo.Var(bounds=(MONTHS[0], MONTHS[-1])), 0 to 40, the first and last breakpoints. piecewise_bounds.py shows what happens otherwise: the rising tiers on a model of their own, where each contractor month is worth $100k, more than any tier costs, so the objective buys as many months as it is allowed. bought gets three upper bounds, 40, 60 and none:

With no upper bound, Piecewise refuses to build: it logs an ERROR and raises ValueError. With an upper bound of 60, past the last breakpoint, it builds and prints a WARNING, and the rows it wrote hold bought to the curve's range: the plan stops at 40 months, although the variable's own bound allows 60 and the objective gains $100k for every month it buys. The model is then not the one its declarations say, and the only sign is a warning in the log. So bound x to exactly the first and last breakpoints, or add breakpoints until they cover the bounds the data needs.

A Convex Cost Bounded From Below Needs No Binaries

The first line has no binaries and no new variables, only 3 rows. When it builds the component, Pyomo checks the breakpoints: if the function is convex and the type is "LB", or concave and the type is "UB", it replaces the piecewise formulation by one row per segment. For the rising tiers those are the rows it wrote:

$c \ge 30b$, $c \ge 300 + 45(b - 10)$, $c \ge 750 + 70(b - 20)$: $c$ above every line, lesson Convex piecewise costs without binaries's epigraph of a convex cost, which needs no binaries because the minimizer lands on the highest line by itself. The plan buys 21 months for $820k, the curve's value (10 × 30 + 10 × 45 + 1 × 70). force_pw=True turns the check off and keeps the formulation you named: with "INC" it added 5 variables, 2 of them binary, and 6 rows, for the same answer. Leave the check on; it is free tightness.

The volume discount is concave, and a concave cost pushed down is the case lesson Convex piecewise costs without binaries showed needs binaries; Pyomo keeps the formulation ("INC": 2 binaries) and the answer is exact.

SOS2, the Default, Fails With HiGHS as Soon as Pyomo Writes It

pw_repn="SOS2" asks the solver to treat the breakpoint weights as a special ordered set of type 2: at most two weights nonzero, and they must be neighbours. HiGHS's Pyomo interface has no SOS constraints and refuses the model:

The table shows when that happens. The default is SOS2, and on the rising tiers it worked (first line), because Pyomo's convexity check replaced the formulation by plain rows and no SOS constraint was ever written. With force_pw=True, or with the concave discount (the fifth line), Pyomo writes the SOS2 constraint and HiGHS refuses it. So a model that leaves pw_repn at its default can run for months and then fail the day a curve changes shape. With HiGHS (and with MathOpt and CP-SAT, which have no SOS2 either), always name a formulation built from binaries, such as "INC", "CC" or "DCC"; the module's tests check that those three give the same optimum here, and lesson Lifecycle value curves for asset renewal ran "LOG" too. Pyomo's documentation lists CBC, SCIP, Gurobi and CPLEX among the solvers that accept SOS constraints.

Problem statement

A rail depot chooses which overhaul jobs to do this week. Each job takes hours and is worth an amount if done. Hours beyond the regular week are overtime, bought from a contractor whose price per hour changes by tier: some contracts get dearer with volume, and some give a volume discount. The depot wants the jobs that maximize value minus what the overtime costs.

Write plan(jobs, regular, hours, cost) in overtime.py. jobs maps a job to (hours, value); the overtime cost is the piecewise-linear curve through the points (hours[k], cost[k]). Return the net value, the overtime hours and their cost.

Write the curve row with pyomo.environ.Piecewise. The tests use both kinds of contract and solve with appsi_highs, so choose the arguments so that every curve is exact and HiGHS accepts the model. About fifteen lines.

pyomo.contrib.piecewise Separates the Function From Its Formulation


Newer Pyomo versions have a second piecewise API, pyomo.contrib.piecewise, built the way GDP is: you declare the function as a component, use it in ordinary constraints, and choose the formulation afterwards with a transformation. A PiecewiseLinearFunction can be made from a table of points:




Called on a number it evaluates the curve; called on a variable it gives an expression for a constraint. The transformations include contrib.piecewise.incremental, contrib.piecewise.disaggregated_logarithmic and contrib.piecewise.inner_repn_gdp, which writes the function as a GDP disjunction, one disjunct per segment, for lesson Pyomo.GDP: disjunctions, big-M and hull transformations's gdp.hull or gdp.bigm to finish:



$f(15) = 300 + 5 \times 45 = 525$ and $f(30) = 750 + 10 \times 70 = 1450$. All three agree with the Piecewise component, but each wrote binaries for a curve that needed none: the newer API, in these transformations, does not make the convexity check. Choose the API by what you need: the old Piecewise for one-dimensional curves with its free simplification, the new one for functions of several variables (it triangulates their domain) or when you want the GDP route.


The MIS Tool Explains an Infeasible Model With Any Solver, on Its LP Relaxation


Now the budget cuts. The model is lesson Concrete models with indexed Sets, Params, Vars and Constraints's timing model on GridCap small, unchanged except for two numbers: $B_{2030} = B_{2031} = \text{cap}$, with cap $4,000k and then $2,600k (from $8,610k and $7,850k). Three must-do projects work in those years: P017 spends 5,310 in its start year (2030 or 2031), P010 spends 130 (2030 or 2031) and P003 spends 140 (2031).


An irreducible infeasible subsystem (IIS) is a set of rows and bounds that has no solution together, while every proper subset of it has one (lesson Callbacks and compute_infeasible_subsystem). Pyomo's compute_infeasibility_explanation (in pyomo.contrib.iis; its documentation calls the result a minimal intractable system, MIS) finds one with any solver Pyomo can call, in two phases:


  1. Elastic filter. It turns every variable bound into a row, adds a slack variable to each row, and lets the solver use the slacks. Each feasible solve names rows whose slack was used, a "repair option", and those rows join a candidate set.
  2. Deletion filter. With the slacks gone and only the candidate rows active, it drops each row in turn; if the rest is still infeasible the row stays out, if the rest becomes feasible the row is part of the conflict and goes back. The rows left are the MIS.


The tool needs a solver that reports an infeasible solve in its results rather than raising, because it solves many infeasible models on purpose. Pyomo's HiGHS interfaces raise by default (lesson Solving with HiGHS and reading results), so the program hands it a small wrapper:




and the tool writes its findings to a logger, so explain captures that text:




The $2,600k cut:



The four rows say it: P017 and P010 must start (once is == 1 for a must-do), and together they need 5,310 + 130 = 5,440 across 2030 and 2031, more than 2 × 2,600 = 5,200. P003's row is not in the set, because the conflict holds without it. The 43 lower bounds are there because the tool treats bounds as rows too: if any project that spends in 2030 or 2031 could start a negative amount, it would free money, so each $x \ge 0$ is part of the conflict. They are true and uninteresting; read the rows first. Before the MIS, the log listed 46 repair options from the elastic filter.


Now the $4,000k cut:



HiGHS says the MILP is infeasible, and the tool says the model is feasible. Both are right about different models: the tool replaces every variable's domain by the reals (its source sets each variable's domain to Reals when it turns the bounds into rows), so it explains the LP relaxation. With $4,000k in each year, the relaxation can start half of P017 in 2030 and half in 2031 (2,655 each). The MILP cannot: P017 is one project and spends 5,310 in a single year. An infeasibility that only integrality causes is invisible to this tool, and it says so by raising.

Problem statement


A staffing team's models are LPs and small MILPs, and when one comes back infeasible they want the rows to blame without a commercial solver. HiGHS can tell them whether a model is feasible; the deletion filter turns that yes/no answer into an irreducible infeasible subsystem.


Write deletion_filter(model) in iis.py. It receives an infeasible Pyomo model and returns the sorted names of the rows of one irreducible infeasible subsystem; variable bounds always stay.


  1. Deactivate the objective: only feasibility matters. Objectives and constraints both have .deactivate() and .activate(); for the objective the reference calls objective.deactivate() on each one model.component_data_objects(pyo.Objective, active=True) yields.
  2. Take each active row in turn (model.component_data_objects(pyo.Constraint, active=True)). Call row.deactivate() and ask feasible(model) (in the starter). If the model is now feasible, the row is part of the conflict: row.activate() and keep it. If the model is still infeasible, leave the row out for good.
  3. The rows left active at the end are the subsystem.


The tests use models with one conflict and with two unrelated ones. About ten lines.

write_iis Needs CPLEX, Gurobi or Xpress, and Works on the MILP


write_iis(model, file_name, solver=None), also in pyomo.contrib.iis, does not search itself: it hands the model to a commercial solver's persistent interface (CPLEX, Gurobi or Xpress), asks that solver for an IIS of the MILP, and writes it to a file in LP format:




In the course runner without a Gurobi license it finds none of the three and raises RuntimeError: Could not find a solver to use, supported solvers are ['cplex', 'gurobi', 'xpress'], which is what write_iis_demo.py prints there. With a Gurobi license mounted into the runner (lesson Licenses: size-limited, academic, and cloud/container (WLS) sets one up), the same program prints the rows of the file Gurobi wrote for the $2,600k cut: once(P017), spend_cap(2030) and spend_cap(2031), three rows and no bounds. That is smaller than the MIS above, and it is the MILP's conflict: P017 alone spends 5,310 in one year, more than 2,600, so P010 is not needed. (Gurobi's file writes the names with parentheses.)

find_infeasible_constraints Checks a Plan Without a Solver


A different question: the planners hand you a plan and ask which rules it breaks under the cut. That needs no solver at all. Fix every variable at the plan's value and evaluate each row; pyomo.util.infeasible.find_infeasible_constraints does the second step, yielding each violated row with its body's value:






The plan that was optimal under the full budgets spends $8,560k in 2030 and $7,720k in 2031; under the $4,000k cut those two rows break and every other row still holds. Its companion log_infeasible_constraints writes the same findings to a logger instead. This is also how to test a planner's hand-made plan against the model before arguing about it.


Summary


  • Piecewise writes y below, above or equal to f(x), and the side must match the push. "UB" on a cost the objective pushes down paid $0 for 40 months whose curve cost is $1,150k. Bound x to the breakpoints: unbounded, Piecewise raises; bounded past them, it only warns, and its rows cap x.
  • A convex cost bounded from below needs no binaries. Pyomo checks convexity and writes one row per segment; force_pw=True turns that off.
  • SOS2, the default, fails with HiGHS as soon as Pyomo writes it. It ran on a convex curve only because no SOS2 was written; name a binary form such as "INC", "CC" or "DCC".
  • pyomo.contrib.piecewise separates the function from its formulation. A PiecewiseLinearFunction plus a transformation, including a GDP route; no convexity shortcut.
  • The MIS tool explains an infeasible model with any solver, on its LP relaxation. Four rows and 43 bounds on the $2,600k cut; on the $4,000k cut, a conflict only integrality causes, it reports the model feasible.
  • write_iis needs CPLEX, Gurobi or Xpress, and works on the MILP. With Gurobi, three rows.
  • find_infeasible_constraints checks a plan without a solver. Fix the plan, list the rows it breaks.

Design Note: Name the Formulation and the Solver's Limits in the Code


Two failures in this lesson only appear when the data changes: SOS2 works until a curve turns concave, and the MIS tool explains until the conflict depends on integrality. Neither is a bug in your rows. Write the choice down where the next reader sees it: pass pw_repn explicitly with a comment on why, and have the infeasibility report say which model it explained (LP relaxation or MILP) and which tool it used.
