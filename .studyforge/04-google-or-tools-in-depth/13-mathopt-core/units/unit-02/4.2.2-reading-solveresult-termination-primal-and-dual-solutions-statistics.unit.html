  16. Reading SolveResult

GridCap's nightly planning job solves the selection model and writes the plan to a report. One night the budgets arrive truncated to $100k a year, and the job crashes with a ValueError from objective_value(). Another night a time limit stops the solve early, and the job reports the plan as optimal when it was not. Both bugs are the same bug: the job read the result without first asking what kind of result it was.

mathopt.solve returns a SolveResult, and what it holds depends on how the solve ended. This lesson reads every part of it, in the order a program should: the termination, then the plan and its bounds, then the dual information an LP adds, then the statistics. Its programs solve the GridCap selection model with HiGHS (and GLOP for the LPs) through MathOpt in OR-Tools 9.15. GridCap is a fictional portfolio.

The Model

Every program here solves GridCap small's project-selection model from lesson Model, variables, constraints, objective, either as the MILP or as its LP relaxation (each $x_i$ allowed anywhere in $[0, 1]$).

Sets. $P$ the candidate projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$.

Parameters. $v_i$, the NPV of project $i$ at its earliest start ($k); $c_{i,y}$, what it spends in year $y$ ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k).

Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ (MILP), or $0 \le x_i \le 1$ (LP relaxation).

Read the Termination First: Its Reason Says What the Rest Holds

A SolveResult has five main parts: termination (how the solve ended), solutions (the plans found, best first), primal_rays and dual_rays (certificates for unbounded and infeasible models), and solve_stats (what the search did). SCIP, PDLP and OSQP also attach output of their own. The OR-Tools samples all read termination before anything else, and so does every program here:

termination is a small record with five fields:

  • reason is a TerminationReason, the one field every program must check. The table below lists all nine.
  • limit says which limit stopped the solve. It is set only when reason is FEASIBLE or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND (both mean "a limit stopped me"), and None otherwise.
  • detail is free text from the solver: here HiGHS's own status name. It is for logs, not for code: each solver words it differently.
  • problem_status is the solver's claim about the model itself, separate from what it found: is the primal problem (your model) feasible, and is the dual problem feasible? Each is a FeasibilityStatus: FEASIBLE, INFEASIBLE or UNDETERMINED. Both FEASIBLE, as here, means the model has plans and a finite best value.
  • objective_bounds holds the two numbers of lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap: the primal bound, the value of the best plan found, and the dual bound, a value no plan can beat. Equal bounds, as here, are a proof of optimality.

The nine reasons, from MathOpt's TerminationReason docstring, grouped by what they leave you:

reasonMeaningA plan to read?
OPTIMALa plan proven best, within the solver's tolerances (including a gap tolerance, lesson Stopping on gap, time, nodes or solution count)always
FEASIBLEa limit stopped the solve after it found a plan; limit says whichalways
NO_SOLUTION_FOUNDa limit stopped the solve before it found any plannever
INFEASIBLEproven: no plan satisfies every rownever
UNBOUNDEDproven: plans exist whose objective is as good as you likesometimes (see below)
INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDEDone of the two, and the solver cannot say whichnever
IMPRECISEsolved, but some tolerance was missedmaybe, slightly off
NUMERICAL_ERRORthe solver hit an unrecoverable numerical problemnever
OTHER_ERRORsome other error stopped the solvernever

The limit values, from the Limit docstring: ITERATION, TIME, NODE, SOLUTION, MEMORY, OBJECTIVE, NORM, INTERRUPTED, SLOW_PROGRESS, OTHER, and UNDETERMINED when the solver does not say which limit it reached.

The Plan Is the First Solution, and objective_value Raises When There Is None

The plan the solver found is the primal solution: a value for every variable. Read it through objective_value() and variable_values():

variable_values takes one variable (and returns a float), a list of variables (a list), or nothing (a dict from every variable to its value). best_objective_bound() is the dual bound under another name. Both objective_value() and variable_values() read result.solutions[0], the best plan, and both raise ValueError when there is no feasible plan to read. has_primal_feasible_solution() says in advance whether they will. The SolveResult docstring promises it is True whenever the reason is OPTIMAL or FEASIBLE.

So what does each ending leave? The program solves six variants, from the GridCap model with its budgets cut to $100k a year to an LP whose variable x has no upper bound, and prints the same fields for each:

Read it row by row:

  • FEASIBLE came with a plan worth $20,594k and the limit that stopped the search, SOLUTION (stop at the first plan found). The dual bound, $23,669k, says the best plan might be worth up to $3,075k more. Report this plan with its gap (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap), never as "the optimum".
  • NO_SOLUTION_FOUND stopped after one simplex iteration with nothing to show: no plan, and objective_value() raises. The limit field still says why, so the job can raise the limit and retry.
  • INFEASIBLE is a proof, not a failure: the must-do projects cost more than $100k in some year. There is nothing to read, and the fix is in the data or the rules (section 9 finds which rows clash).
  • UNBOUNDED from HiGHS came with a feasible point whose value is 0.0, while the primal bound is $+\infty$. That plan is only a witness that plans exist; it is not a "best" anything. So a program must not treat "has a plan" as "has a useful plan": the reason decides.
  • INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED is the same model on GLOP, which proved there is no finite optimum but not which of the two causes it is. As in lesson Model, variables, constraints, objective, the usual cause is a missing bound.

The bounds follow one convention, for a maximization: the primal bound is the value of the best plan found, so $-\infty$ when there is none; the dual bound is the best value any plan could have, so $+\infty$ when nothing is proven, and $-\infty$ when the model is proven infeasible (no plan can have any value). For a minimization every sign flips. primal_bound() and dual_bound() never raise, which makes them the safe numbers to log for every ending.

A Handler Turns the Reason Into What the Caller Should Do

Put together, a program's first lines after a solve are a decision on termination.reason, and the three answers a caller needs are: is there a plan, can it be called optimal, and if not, why not.

reasonPlan?What the caller does
OPTIMALyesuse it
FEASIBLEyesuse it, report the gap and the limit
NO_SOLUTION_FOUNDnoreport the limit; raise it or retry
INFEASIBLEnoreport a data or rule conflict
UNBOUNDED, INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDEDno usable planreport a modeling error (a missing bound)
IMPRECISE, NUMERICAL_ERROR, OTHER_ERRORnoreport a solver failure, with detail

Write it as one function with one branch per row, and never as if reason == OPTIMAL: ... else: crash: the else is where the nightly job's two bugs lived.

  1. Coding practice

    Tell the caller what a solve means

    • each termination reason maps to one action for the caller, with no catch-all branch
    • objective_value and variable_values read the first solution and raise when there is none, while primal_bound and dual_bound never raise
    • termination holds the reason, the limit, the solver's detail, the problem status and the objective bounds

Problem statement

A hospital's staffing service solves a nurse-rostering model every evening and hands the answer to three callers: a dashboard, a nightly report and an alerting job. Each caller only needs to know what to do with the result. Write verdict(result) in verdict.py, which reads a mathopt.SolveResult and returns a Verdict with four fields:

termination.reasonactionvaluegaplimit
OPTIMAL"use"the objective value0.0None
FEASIBLE"use-with-gap"the objective value$\lvert \text{dual bound} - \text{primal bound} \rvert$the limit's name, such as "SOLUTION"
NO_SOLUTION_FOUND"retry"NoneNonethe limit's name
INFEASIBLE"conflict"NoneNoneNone
UNBOUNDED, INFEASIBLE_OR_UNBOUNDED"model-error"NoneNoneNone
IMPRECISE, NUMERICAL_ERROR, OTHER_ERROR"solver-error"NoneNoneNone

An UNBOUNDED result may carry a feasible point; it is still a model error, and its value is None. A limit's name is result.termination.limit.name (a Limit such as Limit.SOLUTION has the name "SOLUTION"). The tests build their results by solving small models of their own, and a few by constructing a SolveResult directly.

Dual Values, Reduced Costs and a Basis Come Only From an LP Solve


The read above asked for one more thing, and got an error:






A dual solution prices the rows: the dual value of a row is how much the objective would improve per unit of extra right-hand side (lesson The dual LP and what its variables price). It exists for LPs, because an LP is convex; a MILP's best value jumps when the budget crosses a project's cost, so it has no such prices, and MathOpt returns none. The SolveResult docstring states the rule: dual solutions are given only for convex problems such as LPs, not for integer programs.


An LP solve with a simplex solver returns three extras. The program below adapts the OR-Tools sample advanced_linear_programming.py, which reads all three:






  • dual_values(rows): one more unit of c1's right-hand side (601 instead of 600) is worth about 0.6667 of objective, and c3's about 3.3333. c2 has slack, so its price is 0.
  • reduced_costs(variables): x2 is at its bound 0, and its reduced cost, $-2.6667$, is how much each unit of x2 forced into the plan would cost the objective (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver).
  • constraint_status and variable_status give the simplex basis: which rows are tight (AT_UPPER_BOUND) and which variables are BASIC (strictly between their bounds) or sit at a bound. Only simplex solvers return one, and it is what lets a solver restart quickly after a small change.


The sample checks has_dual_feasible_solution() and has_basis() before reading, because MathOpt does not require a solver to return either one even on OPTIMAL. Do the same. The same reading on GridCap small's LP relaxation prices each year's budget:






In the relaxation, one more $k of 2030 budget is worth about $1.38k of NPV, and 2029 and 2031 have money to spare. Those are LP prices: they describe the relaxed model, not the yes/no plan (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver says what they do and do not mean for a MILP). Lesson Switching solvers: HiGHS, SCIP, CP-SAT, GLOP, Gurobi shows which solvers return duals at all.

Problem statement


A dairy cooperative plans its weekly milk allocation with an LP: how many thousand litres go to cheese, butter and bottled milk, under limits on milk supply, plant hours and churn capacity, and a minimum for the bottled-milk contract. Its finance team asks one question after every run: which limits hold the plan back, and what would one more unit of each be worth?


Write binding_prices(result, rows, tol=1e-6) in prices.py. rows is a list of the model's mathopt.LinearConstraints.


  • When result has no dual feasible solution (a MILP, or a solve that failed), return None.
  • Otherwise return a dict from row name to the row's dual value, for every binding row in rows, and no other.


A row is binding when its activity, $\sum_j a_j x_j$ at the result's variable values, is within tol of one of its finite bounds: its upper bound, its lower bound, or the single value of an equality row. Compute the activity from the row's terms() and result.variable_values(). Do not use the basis (constraint_status): a first-order solver such as PDLP returns dual values without one.

Statistics Describe the Search, and the Solution List Can Hold More Than One Plan


The last part of the result says how much work the solve took:






solve_stats has node_count (branch-and-bound nodes, lesson Branching on a fractional variable), simplex_iterations, barrier_iterations, first_order_iterations (PDLP's steps) and solve_time, a timedelta measured around the solver call (not counting the model build). HiGHS solved GridCap small at the root node. Use these numbers to compare runs and to watch a job's health over time, but never assert on them in a test: solve_time changes on every run, and the counts describe one search, which a different solver, solver version or setting changes. Test the plan and its value instead.


result.solutions is a list, best first. Every solve so far read one plan from it, but a solver that keeps a solution pool can return several, when asked with the common parameter solution_pool_size:






SCIP returned the optimum and four runners-up it met during the search. They are not "the five best plans", only five plans it kept, but they answer a planner's favourite follow-up question ("what else was close?") at no extra cost. HiGHS was not asked: lesson Common vs. solver-specific parameters, and what each solver rejects shows it refuses solution_pool_size. Each entry of solutions has a primal_solution (with variable_values, objective_value and a feasibility_status), and for an LP a dual_solution and a basis.


Summary


  • Read the termination first. reason says what the rest of the result holds; limit says which limit stopped a FEASIBLE or NO_SOLUTION_FOUND solve; detail is the solver's own text.
  • The plan is solutions[0]. objective_value() and variable_values() raise ValueError when there is none; has_primal_feasible_solution() says whether there is. An UNBOUNDED result may carry a plan that means nothing.
  • The bounds are always there. primal_bound() and dual_bound() never raise; infinite values encode "nothing found" and "nothing proven".
  • A handler maps each reason to what the caller does, with no catch-all branch.
  • Dual values, reduced costs and a basis come only from an LP solve, and a program checks has_dual_feasible_solution() and has_basis() before reading them.
  • Statistics describe the search; time and some solvers' counts change between runs, so log them and never assert on them.

Design Note: A Result Is a Claim, Not a Proof You Have Checked


Every field of SolveResult is what the solver claims. MathOpt's own docstrings say so: the termination reasons are as reported by the solver, which is not required to return a certificate, and OPTIMAL means optimal within the solver's tolerances. For a planning job that means a cheap check after the solve is worth having: recompute each year's spend from the plan with plain Python (or evaluate_expression) and compare it with the budget. A plan that breaks a row by more than the tolerance is a bug to report, whatever the status said. Section 10 returns to tolerances, and section 12 turns such checks into tests.
