  17. Interval variables and NoOverlap

GridCap's vegetation program keeps trees away from power lines. A capital plan decides which year each vegetation project is funded; this module takes that year as given and decides which week each project is worked, and by which crew. Nothing here needs the year plan itself: lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, later in the course, builds it in depth. The question changes shape. A year plan asks "how much money does each year need?"; a season plan asks "is this crew already busy that week?". Two jobs that each fit the season can still collide, because one crew cannot be in two places at once.

This lesson starts with one crew and five circuit jobs from GridCap small, all in the Valley and Coast regions. (GridCap is a fictional utility; the season data are invented on top of it, see season.py below.) A circuit trim or clear takes this crew 2 to 4 weeks. In what order should the crew do them, and when does the season's work end? CP-SAT, the constraint solver of OR-Tools that lessons Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers to When CP-SAT beats MIP, and when it doesn't introduce, has a variable made for exactly this: the interval.

The season's jobs, as season.py derives them from the portfolio. The module uses all twelve; this lesson uses the five Valley and Coast circuits P003, P010, P011, P014 and P015:

The weeks column is the work's length: two weeks per crew-month GridCap gives the project. crews, due and weight matter from lesson Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows on.

An Interval Variable Ties a Start, a Size and an End Together

A schedule is made of time spans: "P014 is worked from week 3 to week 4". Weeks are numbered from 0, the season's first week. A span is written as the half-open range $[s, e)$: it starts at the beginning of week $s$ and ends at the beginning of week $e$, so it covers weeks $s, s+1, \dots, e-1$, and its size is $e - s$. A two-week job that starts at week 3 is $[3, 5)$: it occupies weeks 3 and 4, and the next job on the same crew can start at week 5.

In CP-SAT you build a span from two integer variables, its start and its end, and one size, and then tie them together into an interval variable:

HORIZON is the season's length, 30 weeks: the latest week any job may end. Every start and end needs such a bound, because an integer variable in CP-SAT always has a finite domain (lesson Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers); a job that ends after the horizon is simply not a value the variable can take.

new_interval_var(start, size, end, name) does two things. It creates the interval, the object the scheduling constraints below take as input, and it adds the constraint $\text{end} = \text{start} + \text{size}$ to the model. The size here is the constant 2; it could also be an integer variable when a job's length is a decision (lesson Element, table and allowed-assignment constraints does that).

When the size is a constant there is a shorter form that needs no end variable, and a fixed span (a holiday, say) is the same call with a constant start:

Without the interval nothing links end to start, so the solver sets end to its lower bound, 0. With it, the end follows the start by exactly two weeks. solve is the module's small helper (cpsat_run.py): it creates a cp_model.CpSolver, sets one search worker so that a printed schedule repeats from run to run (when two schedules are equally good, several parallel workers can return either; lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs covers reproducible runs), and returns the solver with the status name. NoOverlap Lets One Crew Do One Job at a Time

An interval alone says nothing about other jobs. The rule "one crew does one job at a time" is a statement about a whole set of intervals: no two of them may share a week. CP-SAT states it in one call, add_no_overlap(intervals). For two intervals $[s_a, e_a)$ and $[s_b, e_b)$ it means

(one of them ends before the other starts), and it means that for every pair in the list.

The crew's five jobs, as one model. Each job $j$ has a start $s_j$, an end $e_j$ and an interval of $d_j$ weeks; the makespan $C$ is the week the last job ends, and the objective is to make it as small as possible:

$J$ is the set of the five jobs, $d_j$ their weeks, and $H = 30$ the horizon. In code:

add_max_equality(target, expressions) sets the target equal to the largest of the expressions; it is how the job shop example on the OR-Tools site writes the makespan, and it is exact, not a bound. (fixed is empty for now; the next section fills it.) With nothing blocked, the crew works without a break:

Each line gives the first and last week a job occupies. Five jobs that add up to 14 weeks end at week 14: no idle week, which is the best one crew can do. Any order of the five jobs would also end at week 14; the makespan does not prefer one order over another, and that is a point lesson Scheduling vegetation crews across circuits comes back to.

What does add_no_overlap save you? Write the same rule by hand: for every pair of jobs, one Boolean variable says which comes first, and two enforced rows (lesson Native logic: AddBoolOr, AddImplication, OnlyEnforceIf: a row with only_enforce_if(b) holds only when the literal b is true) keep them apart:

pairwise_order.py writes that for every pair of the five jobs and the blocked weeks of the next section, and gets the same answer:

Twenty Booleans for five jobs and two blocked periods (seven intervals make 21 pairs, and the pair of two fixed periods needs none); the count grows with the square of the number of jobs. A MIP solver needs the same pairs, but it has no only_enforce_if, so it writes each pair as two big-M rows with a Boolean $y_{ab}$ (1 when $a$ comes first): $e_a \le s_b + M(1 - y_{ab})$ and $e_b \le s_a + M y_{ab}$. $M$ is a constant at least as large as the horizon, so the row whose Boolean term is $M$ holds whatever the starts are. Their LP relaxation is weak: with each Boolean at 1/2, both rows of a pair are nearly free. Lesson Deriving big-M from bounds, later in the course, derives such constants in depth. add_no_overlap is one constraint over the whole set, and CP-SAT reasons about the set at once (for example, that five jobs adding up to 14 weeks cannot all end before week 14) instead of pair by pair. That set-level reasoning is why scheduling models are usually written with intervals in CP-SAT.

Fixed Intervals Block Weeks the Crew Cannot Work

The crew is not free all season. It has a training week at week 8 and is held for storm standby in weeks 12 and 13. A blocked period is an interval too, a fixed one, and it goes into the same NoOverlap as the jobs: then no job can share a week with it. In the code above, blocked maps a name to (first week, number of weeks), and each entry becomes model.new_fixed_size_interval_var(first, weeks, name). The OR-Tools sample no_overlap_sample_sat.py does the same with weekends, and jobshop_with_maintenance_sat.py with a machine's maintenance window.

The work still takes 14 weeks, but now it ends at week 18. Three weeks are blocked, and week 11 is idle: between the training and the standby there are three free weeks (9, 10 and 11), and every job is 2 or 4 weeks long, so no set of jobs fills exactly three. Count it: 14 weeks of work, 3 blocked and 1 idle make 18. The solver did not need to be told that a job cannot be split around a block: an interval is one unbroken span, so a two-week job either fits in a gap or goes after it.

Mind the conversion from a planner's calendar to an interval. A planner says "standby in weeks 12 to 13"; the interval is $[12, 14)$, size 2. Writing size $13 - 12 = 1$ blocks week 12 only and lets a job run in week 13. Whenever a period is given by its first and last unit, the size is $\text{last} - \text{first} + 1$.

Problem statement

A pipeline company has one welding rig. It repairs segments one at a time, and some days it is booked elsewhere: a safety stand-down, a maintenance visit. Each booking is given as its first and last day, both included, so (4, 4) books day 4 only. Days are numbered from 0.

Write shortest_schedule(jobs, booked, horizon) in rig.py. jobs maps a segment to its days of work, booked lists the bookings, and the rig's work must end by day horizon. Return each segment's start day, chosen so that the last repair ends as early as possible.

The Job Shop Is One NoOverlap per Machine and One Precedence Row per Step


The OR-Tools guide The Job Shop Problem is the standard first scheduling model. Several jobs each pass through machines in a fixed order of steps; a machine works on one step at a time; a step, once started, runs to its end. The guide's data are three jobs on three machines, each step a pair (machine, processing time):




The model has two kinds of rule, and each is one line in the loop:


  • every machine gets a NoOverlap over the intervals of the steps that use it;
  • every job's steps run in order: step $k+1$ starts no earlier than step $k$ ends, a precedence row $s_{b,k+1} \ge e_{b,k}$.




The horizon is the sum of every step's time, as in the guide: if all steps ran one after another, the schedule would end there, so no optimal schedule ends later. The makespan is the largest end of each job's last step, through add_max_equality as before:



job1#2[7,11] is job 1's step 2 (counting from 0), in the span $[7, 11)$. The guide's optimal length is 11 too. Read machine 1: job 2 uses it first, then job 0 waits for its own step 0 on machine 0 to end at 5, then job 1's last step ends the schedule at 11.


Now move it onto GridCap, in working days. Vegetation work on a Valley circuit passes through three crews: a climbing crew cuts, a chipper crew clears the brush, an inspector signs the circuit off. The corridor widening P006 starts with the chipper crew's mower and then needs climbers for the edge trees. Each crew is a machine, each project a job:




The climbers are the bottleneck: they have 3 + 5 + 6 + 3 = 17 days of work, and whichever job they do last still needs at least one inspector day after it, so no schedule ends before day 18. This one does. In it the chipper's mower starts P006 on day 0 (P006 is the only job whose first step is not with the climbers) while the climbers start P018.


Only the data changed between the two runs; jobshop is the same function. That is the point of the pattern: one NoOverlap per resource, one precedence row per consecutive pair of steps.

Optional Intervals Let a Job Choose Its Crew


Back to weeks. Now there are two crews, A and B, and each job can go to either. Crew A has its training week at week 8; crew B is still finishing last season's work in weeks 0 to 3. Which crew does which job, and when?


A fixed interval cannot express "this job is on crew A's calendar or on crew B's". An optional interval can: it has a presence literal, a Boolean, and when the literal is false the interval is absent and every scheduling constraint ignores it. So give each job one optional interval per crew, all on the job's one start and end, with presence $x_{j,c}$ ("crew $c$ does job $j$"), and require exactly one presence per job:




Each crew's NoOverlap gets that crew's optional intervals and its own blocked weeks. A job done by crew A is present in crew A's list and absent from crew B's, so it uses crew A's weeks and none of crew B's. The CP-SAT Primer builds meeting rooms the same way (one optional interval per room, exactly one present), and warns against the shortcut of sharing one interval between rooms: an interval in two NoOverlap lists is present in both, so it would occupy both crews.



Two crews end the work at week 10 instead of 14. Crew A takes 8 weeks of work before its training week; crew B, free from week 4, takes 6. Nothing could end sooner. For the work to end by week 9, crew A could use only weeks 0 to 7 (a job cannot run through its training week, and nothing fits after it), 8 weeks, and crew B only weeks 4 to 8, 5 weeks: 13 weeks of room for 14 weeks of work.


two_crews.py also builds the shortcut the Primer warns about: one ordinary interval per job, placed in both crews' lists. CP-SAT accepts the model and proves its optimum, but the optimum is a different schedule: every job occupies both crews at once, so each job must also avoid both crews' blocked weeks.



Nineteen weeks: worse than the 18 one crew needed in the previous section, because the jobs now have to dodge crew B's weeks 0 to 3 as well as crew A's training week. A wrong model is still a valid model; only the schedule tells you.


A presence literal is an ordinary Boolean: it can appear in other constraints and in the objective. Lesson Element, table and allowed-assignment constraints ties one to a choice of contractor, and the job with no present interval at all, work that is simply not done this season, is the same idea with "exactly one" relaxed to "at most one".

Summary


  • An interval variable ties a start, a size and an end together. new_interval_var adds $e = s + d$; new_fixed_size_interval_var needs no end variable; a fixed span is the same call with a constant start. Every start and end has a finite horizon.
  • NoOverlap lets one crew do one job at a time. It is one constraint over a set of intervals, in place of a Boolean and two enforced rows for every pair.
  • Fixed intervals block weeks the crew cannot work. They go into the same NoOverlap; a period given by its first and last unit has size last − first + 1.
  • The job shop is one NoOverlap per machine and one precedence row per step. The makespan is the largest end of each job's last step, written with add_max_equality.
  • Optional intervals let a job choose its crew. One optional interval per crew on the job's start, a presence literal each, exactly one present, and one NoOverlap per crew.

Design Note: Intervals or Week-by-Week Booleans


There is another way to write a season: one Boolean per job and week, "job $j$ is worked in week $w$", with rows that make each job's weeks consecutive and a crew's Booleans sum to at most one per week. The CP-SAT Primer warns against reaching for intervals by reflex: when time comes in a few fixed slots (shifts, days of a short roster), slot Booleans are often the better model, and lesson Element, table and allowed-assignment constraints builds a roster that way. Intervals pay off when time is long or fine-grained: the size of an interval model does not grow with the number of weeks, while the slot model needs a variable for every job and week.
