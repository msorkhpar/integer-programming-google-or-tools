  17. Cumulative resources and windows

Lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap gave each crew its own calendar. GridCap's vegetation manager does not think that way: she has a pool of three crews for the season and twelve jobs, and some jobs need two crews at once. A corridor widening (P006, P012, P020, P035) runs a mower and a climbing crew side by side, so it takes two of the three crews for its whole length; a circuit job takes one. Her questions are about the pool: "in any week, do the jobs under way need more crews than I have?", "what if one crew is held back for storm duty in the stormy weeks?", "can my circuit clears fit into the weeks the grid operators will switch those circuits off?". (GridCap is fictional; so is this season, see season.py in lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap.)

Each question is a resource with a capacity, and CP-SAT states each in one constraint. The twelve jobs come from season.py:

crews is the job's demand: how many of the pool's crews it holds in every week it runs. The due weeks and weights are for lesson Scheduling vegetation crews across circuits; this lesson minimizes the makespan, the week the last job ends, as lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap did.

Cumulative Caps the Crews at Work in Every Week

NoOverlap says "at most one at a time". The pool needs "at most three crews' worth at a time", where each job counts its demand. That is the cumulative constraint. Given intervals $[s_j, s_j + d_j)$, demands $c_j$ and a capacity $K$, it requires that in every week $w$ the demands of the jobs running in that week add up to at most $K$:

The set under the sum depends on the starts, which are variables, so this is not a linear row you could hand to a MIP solver as written. CP-SAT takes it as one constraint, add_cumulative(intervals, demands, capacity), and reasons about it directly. season_model.build creates one fixed-size interval per job on a start variable and adds the pool:

The start's upper bound is horizon - j.weeks, so every job ends inside the 30-week season. The makespan objective is the one of lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap, written with the interval's end expression:

crew_pool.py first works out a bound by hand. The twelve jobs need 68 crew-weeks (weeks times demand, summed), and three crews supply 3 crew-weeks per week, so no schedule can end before week $\lceil 68 / 3 \rceil = 23$:

The last line is the load profile: the crews at work in each of the 30 weeks, one digit per week. It never exceeds 3, which is the cumulative constraint at work, and it is full for the first 20 weeks. The optimum is 24, one week above the hand bound, and OPTIMAL means CP-SAT proved that 23 is impossible. The hand bound only counts crew-weeks; it knows nothing about which jobs can share a week, so a real schedule can need more.

The late notes show what the makespan does not care about: P006 is due at week 6 and finishes at week 20. Lesson Scheduling vegetation crews across circuits changes the objective.

NoOverlap Is Cumulative With Capacity 1

A single crew is a pool of size 1 in which every job has demand 1, so add_cumulative(intervals, [1] * n, 1) must say exactly what add_no_overlap(intervals) says. nooverlap_is_cumulative.py solves the one-crew schedule of lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap (five jobs, the training week and the storm standby) both ways:

The same 18 weeks. Use add_no_overlap when the capacity really is 1: it says what you mean, and CP-SAT has reasoning made for that case. Use add_cumulative when a resource holds more than one job, or when jobs take different amounts of it.

A Capacity That Drops Is a Fixed Interval Holding the Missing Crews


From week 12 to week 15 the regional operations center holds one crew back for storm duty, so the pool has 2 crews in those 4 weeks and 3 the rest of the season. add_cumulative takes one capacity. How do you make it change over time?


Add a fixed interval for the period, with the demand of the crews that are away. It occupies part of the capacity just as a job would, and the jobs share what is left:



Here standby = {12: (4, 1)} (STANDBY in season_model.py): from week 12, for 4 weeks, 1 crew away. The capacity stays at 3 (the most crews the season ever has), and a fixed interval of demand $3 - a_w$ is added wherever only $a_w$ crews are available. The OR-Tools sample cumulative_variable_profile_sample_sat.py builds a whole day's capacity profile this way, with one fixed interval per period, each as high as the gap between that period's capacity and the peak.



Weeks 12 to 15 now have 2 crews at work, and the profile never goes above the second line. The makespan is still 24. Count the crew-weeks: 24 weeks of 3 crews are 72, the work takes 68 and the standby 4, so this schedule leaves no crew idle before week 24, where the first one had 4 idle crew-weeks (weeks 20 to 23 at 2 crews). The schedule is a different one, and its late jobs differ: a rule that does not move the objective can still move the plan.


A capacity that rises over time works the same way: set the capacity to the highest level and hold the difference with fixed intervals in the weeks before it rises.

The model, with $J$ the jobs, $d_j$ and $g_j$ their days and gangs, $a_t$ the gangs on day $t$, $H$ the horizon and $G = \max_t a_t$:



The (gangs) rows are one cumulative constraint of capacity $G$ in which the $G - a_t$ missing gangs are held by fixed intervals. An Outage Window Is a Domain on the Start


A circuit clear (P003, P008, P010, P015, P018) removes trees close to energized conductors, so the circuit must be switched off (an outage) while the crew works. The grid operators allow outages only in certain weeks of each region, OUTAGE_WINDOWS in season.py: the Valley, for example, can be switched in weeks $[2, 12)$ and $[18, 28)$. A clear must lie entirely inside one window.


For a job of $d$ weeks and a window $[a, b)$, the starts that keep the job inside are $a, a+1, \dots, b - d$. The job's allowed starts are the union of those ranges over its region's windows: a set of integers with holes. CP-SAT lets a variable take its values from exactly such a set, a domain, built with cp_model.Domain.from_intervals and used through new_int_var_from_domain:




Domain.from_intervals takes closed ranges, both ends included, so $[a, b - d]$ here is the list [a, b - j.weeks]. A window shorter than the job is skipped (b - j.weeks >= a). The five clears get these domains:



P008 may start in weeks 0 to 2 or 14 to 18: a six-week clear starting at week 2 occupies $[2, 8)$, weeks 2 to 7, and its end, 8, equals the end of the first Ridge window $[0, 8)$, whose last week is 7. One starting at week 3 would also work week 8, which lies outside the window, when the circuit is back on.


No constraint was added: the windows live in the variable's domain, and a start in a hole is not a value the solver can even try. The OR-Tools sample scheduling_with_calendar_sample_sat.py does the same for a task that cannot start during a lunch break, with Domain.from_intervals([(8, 12), (14, 15)]).


One Outage per Region Is a NoOverlap Over That Region's Clears


The operators add one more rule: a region switches off one circuit at a time, so two clears in the same region must not overlap. That is a second resource, of capacity 1, over a subset of the same intervals:



An interval can sit in as many scheduling constraints as you like: each clear is in the crew pool's cumulative and in its region's NoOverlap, and the schedule must satisfy both. (A region with one clear needs no NoOverlap, hence len(clears) > 1.) outages.py solves the season with the standby and the windows, first without and then with the one-outage rule, and prints the clears:



Every clear sits inside a window of its region in both runs. With windows only, P010 and P018 both switch Valley circuits off in weeks 2 to 5; with the rule, the Valley's three clears run one after another. They need 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 weeks and the first Valley window is 10 weeks long, so one of them has to wait for the second window, $[18, 28)$: P010 does. The makespan is 24 in all four runs of this lesson. The rules cost no time at the end of the season, but they move jobs, and a moved job can be a late one; lesson Scheduling vegetation crews across circuits prices that.


Why a domain for the windows and not optional intervals, one per window with exactly one present, as lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap chose crews? Both are correct. The domain is smaller (one variable, no Booleans). Optional intervals are worth their size when the choice of window must appear in other rules or in the objective: a different crew rate in the late window, for example.

Problem statement


A railway closes a line to trains only during possessions, agreed stretches of days, and track work on that line must lie wholly inside one possession. A line also takes one work gang at a time; gangs on different lines work independently. Days are numbered from 0.


Write possession_plan(jobs, possessions) in possessions.py. jobs maps a job to (line, days); possessions maps a line to its possessions, each (first_day, last_day) with both days included. Return each job's start day, chosen so that the last job ends as early as possible. Every instance in the tests has a plan.


The model, with $J$ the jobs, $\ell(j)$ and $d_j$ each job's line and days, $P_\ell$ the possessions of line $\ell$, and $L$ the lines. A job fits in possession $(f, l)$ when it starts on a day from $f$ to $l - d_j + 1$:



Build each start's domain with cp_model.Domain.from_intervals, whose ranges include both ends. The tests check the plan you return and how early it ends. About twenty lines.

The Model


The season model season_model.build writes with every option on, and the makespan objective of this lesson. This lesson added the pool, the standby and the outage rules.


Sets. $J$ the jobs; $O \subseteq J$ the circuit clears, which need an outage; $R$ the regions, with $r(j)$ the region of job $j$; $\mathcal{W}_r$ the outage windows of region $r$, each a span $[a, b)$ of weeks.


Parameters. $d_j$, the weeks of job $j$; $c_j$, the crews it holds while it runs; $K = 3$, the crews in the pool; $H = 30$, the season's weeks; the standby, $n = 1$ crew away for the 4 weeks $[12, 16)$.


Variables.



Objective.



Constraints. Each job's interval is $[s_j, s_j + d_j)$, a fixed-size interval on $s_j$.



$[12 \le w < 16]$ is 1 in the standby weeks and 0 otherwise: the standby's fixed interval.

Summary


  • Cumulative caps the crews at work in every week. add_cumulative(intervals, demands, capacity): in every unit of time, the demands of the running intervals add up to at most the capacity.
  • NoOverlap is cumulative with capacity 1 and every demand 1; use NoOverlap when that is what you mean.
  • A capacity that drops is a fixed interval holding the missing crews. Set the capacity to the peak and add a fixed interval with demand peak − available in each period below it.
  • An outage window is a domain on the start. Domain.from_intervals with closed ranges $[a, b - d]$, one per window, and new_int_var_from_domain.
  • One outage per region is a NoOverlap over that region's clears. One interval can belong to several resource constraints, and the schedule meets them all.

Design Note: Domains Are Data, So Check Them as Data


An outage window that is one week too short, or a domain built with $b$ where $b - d$ was meant, does not make the model fail loudly. It makes some starts impossible, and the solver answers with a worse schedule or, if no start is left, INFEASIBLE with no hint of why. Print each domain next to its windows, as outages.py does, and test start_domain on a job whose length equals its window (exactly one start) and on one longer than every window (an empty domain). Lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility shows how CP-SAT can name the rule that made a model infeasible.
