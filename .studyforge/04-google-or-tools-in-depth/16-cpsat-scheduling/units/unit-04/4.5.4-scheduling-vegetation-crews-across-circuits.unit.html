  17. Scheduling vegetation crews

This lesson puts the module together into the schedule GridCap's vegetation manager actually needs. Twelve jobs across four regions, a pool of three crews with one held back for storm duty in weeks 12 to 15, circuit clears that must sit inside their region's outage windows, one outage per region at a time: all of lesson Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows's rules at once. And now a due week for every job, with a weight that says how much it matters.

Why due weeks? Trees keep growing during the season. A circuit whose conductors are already close to the canopy (GridCap's asset-health model gives it a short remaining useful life) carries a higher chance of a tree contact, an outage or a fire for every week its trim waits. season.py turns that into two numbers per job (GridCap and these rules are fictional):

  • the due week $\delta_j$: week 6 if the asset's remaining useful life is 2 years or less, week 12 if it is 15 or less, week 20 otherwise;
  • the weight $w_j$: the asset's consequence of failure divided by 100, read as $k of risk carried for every week the job finishes after its due week.

The makespan of lessons Interval variables and NoOverlap and Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows is blind to all of this. The manager's question is "which schedule carries the least risk?", and the answer is a different objective: the risk-weighted lateness, the sum over jobs of weight times weeks late.

Due Weeks as Hard Rules Make the Season Infeasible

The first idea is to make every due week a rule, $s_j + d_j \le \delta_j$:

INFEASIBLE means CP-SAT proved that no schedule meets every rule. Here you can see why by hand. Five jobs are due at week 6: P004 (6 weeks, 1 crew), P006 (4 weeks, 2 crews), P008 (6 weeks, 1 crew), P011 (4 weeks, 1 crew) and P012 (6 weeks, 2 crews). They need $6 + 8 + 6 + 4 + 12 = 36$ crew-weeks before week 6, and three crews supply 18. No ordering can fix that. A due week that cannot always be met is not a rule but a wish, and a wish belongs in the objective, where it can be traded against other wishes.

Lateness Is max(0, end − due), and Its Weight Is the Risk Carried

The lateness of job $j$ is how many weeks it finishes after its due week, and 0 if it finishes on time or early:

The floor at 0 matters: finishing early earns nothing. Without it, a job that ends three weeks early would count $-3$ and pay for another job's lateness, which is not how risk works. CP-SAT writes the max directly with add_max_equality, as the makespan was written, and the objective weights each lateness by its job's risk:

add_max_equality makes $L_j$ equal to the max, so the value you read back is the lateness itself. A MIP writes the same objective with two rows, $L_j \ge 0$ and $L_j \ge s_j + d_j - \delta_j$, and lets the minimization push $L_j$ down onto the larger of the two; that also works here, because a smaller $L_j$ always lowers the objective. The equality is simply exact whatever the objective.

season_lateness.py builds the full season (every rule of lesson Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows) with this objective:

Three jobs are late: P012 by 6 weeks at $22k a week, P018 by 4 at $30k, and P006 by 18 at $6k. $132 + 120 + 108 = 360$, the objective. Compare the two late corridors due at week 6. P006, with the lowest weight of the season ($6k a week), is the job left to the very end of the season, 18 weeks late. P012, at $22k, starts at week 6: in weeks 0 to 5 the three crews are on P004, P008 and P011 (also due at week 6) and P015, which leaves no week with the two crews P012 needs. OPTIMAL says that no schedule keeping every rule carries less than $360k, so any other way of sharing out the lateness costs at least as much.

Problem statement

A machine workshop has a pool of mechanics. Each repair needs some mechanics for some days, has a due day, and costs a penalty for every day it finishes after its due day (a customer's machine standing idle). Finishing early earns nothing. Days are numbered from 0, and a job that starts on day s and takes d days finishes at s + d.

The Shortest Season Is Not the Safest One


The same rules with the makespan objective, and the risk of the schedule it returns, computed afterwards from its starts:






Both schedules end at week 24, and the makespan objective cannot tell them apart. Their risk differs almost threefold: $1,038k against $360k. Lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap noted that, for one crew, any order of the jobs gave the same makespan; with due weeks the order is the whole question, and only an objective that sees the due weeks can answer it.


This is a general lesson about objectives, not about scheduling. An objective is a statement of what the planner values, and the solver optimizes exactly that and nothing else. Two optimal schedules for two objectives can share every rule and differ in everything the planner cares about. When a schedule looks wrong, ask first whether the objective says what was meant. When the planners care about both, rank them: first the least risk, then, among schedules with that risk, the shortest season. That is a prioritized objective, which lesson Prioritized objectives with add_auxiliary_objective builds in MathOpt with add_auxiliary_objective. Lesson Hierarchical objectives with tolerances, later in the course, treats such hierarchies of objectives in depth.


A Checker Written From the Rules Proves the Schedule


How do you know the model says what the planners said? Write the rules a second time, in plain Python, as a function that takes a schedule and lists every rule it breaks, and run it on what the solver returns. The checker shares no code with the model: it reads the same data, and nothing else.



(The function goes on with the one-outage-per-region rule, pair by pair.) It is slow and obvious on purpose: a loop over weeks, a sum per week, a test per rule, each written the way the planner says it. season_lateness.py already printed its verdict on the least-risk schedule, rules broken: none. A checker earns its keep when it can fail, so check_demo.py also runs it on two hand edits of the solver's schedule:



Each edit breaks rules the risk alone would never show: the risk is the same $360k, since neither job is late. In a test suite (the section 12 lesson Unit-testing one constraint at a time builds one), the checker runs on every schedule the model returns, for every instance, so a model change that quietly drops a rule fails the build instead of reaching a planner.

Problem statement


A museum plans the installation of its new exhibitions. Each job needs some installers for some days; some jobs, the noisy or dusty ones, must happen while their gallery is closed to visitors. The museum has a fixed team of installers. Days are numbered from 0, and a job that starts on day s and takes d days occupies days s to s + d - 1 and ends at s + d.


Write broken_rules(jobs, start, installers, horizon) in checker.py, without a solver: it checks a plan someone else made. jobs maps a job to (days, installers_needed, windows), where windows is a list of closures (a, b), each the half-open span of days $[a, b)$ (open from the start of day a to the start of day b), or None when the job can happen any time. start maps each job to its start day. Named Crews Come From Handing Out Free Crews in Start Order


The cumulative constraint promises that no week needs more crews than there are. It does not say which crew does which job, and a crew foreman needs a name on the work order. Can every pool schedule be turned into named crews? Yes, and with a simple rule: go through the jobs (and the standby) in order of start week, and give each the crews that are free at its start.



Why does it never run short? Think of a job that needs $k$ crews as $k$ copies of the same interval. At the moment a job starts, the crews still busy are exactly those on jobs that started earlier and have not ended, and the cumulative constraint says those, plus this job's demand, fit in the pool. So enough crews are free. (This is the classic result that intervals can be coloured with as many colours as the most of them that overlap at one time.) Note what makes it work: the jobs are handed out in start order. In any other order a crew could be booked for a later job first and be missing for an earlier one.



The corridor jobs appear on two crews each (P012 on B and C, P020 on A and B), every crew's jobs are in sequence without overlap, and crew A takes the storm standby. The assert in the loop never fired. A model with named crews from the start (one optional interval per job and crew, as in lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap) would give the same answer with many more variables; when crews are interchangeable, schedule the pool and name the crews afterwards.

The Model


The season model season_lateness.py solves, in one place. Lesson Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows built the rules; this lesson added the due weeks, the lateness variables and the objective.


Sets. $J$ the jobs; $O \subseteq J$ the circuit clears; $R$ the regions, $r(j)$ the region of job $j$; $\mathcal{W}_r$ the outage windows of region $r$, each a span $[a, b)$.


Parameters. $d_j$, the weeks of job $j$; $c_j$, the crews it holds; $\delta_j$, its due week; $w_j$, its weight in $k per week late; $K = 3$ crews; $H = 30$ weeks; the standby, $n = 1$ crew away in weeks $[12, 16)$.

Summary


  • Due weeks as hard rules make the season infeasible. Five jobs due at week 6 need 36 crew-weeks against 18; a target that cannot always be met belongs in the objective.
  • Lateness is max(0, end − due), and its weight is the risk carried. add_max_equality with 0 in the list; minimize the weighted sum. The least-risk schedule carries $360k.
  • The shortest season is not the safest one. Both objectives give week 24; the makespan schedule carries $1,038k of risk. An objective optimizes what it says and nothing else.
  • A checker written from the rules proves the schedule. Plain Python, no shared code with the model, run on every schedule; test that it can fail.
  • Named crews come from handing out free crews in start order. The cumulative constraint guarantees the hand-out never runs short.

Design Note: Risk Weights Are a Model Too


The weights here are a crude proxy: consequence of failure over 100, flat for every week late. A real program would make the risk grow with the weeks (a tree grows into the line faster in midsummer) and with the circuit's condition. Two things carry over whatever the weights are. First, the objective is linear in the lateness, so a job's weight is exactly the price of one week's delay of that job, and a planner can read it that way. Second, the weights are data: keep them in the data set, next to the due weeks, where the asset-health team can review them, rather than in the model code. When they change, the schedule changes, and the checker still proves it keeps every rule.
