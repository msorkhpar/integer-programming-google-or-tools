  15. Optional visits, penalties, and pickup and delivery

Two of the Valley's four inspection crews are called away to storm repairs, and the two that remain cannot inspect all 18 substations in one shift. The eight urgent sites (five years or less of predicted life) must still be done today; the ten routine ones can wait for next week, but the planner would rather skip the ones whose failure would cost least. Lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP) showed what a plain routing model does when the work does not fit: it finds nothing, and says only that it timed out. This lesson makes the routine visits optional, at a price, so a plan always exists and the price decides what gets skipped.

The same afternoon, a second request arrives: the portable transformer test set was left at substation A157, and the crew inspecting A230 needs it. Some crew must collect it at A157 and carry it to A230. That is a pickup and delivery pair, the second half of this lesson. The drive times are fictional, as everywhere in this module.

A Disjunction With a Penalty Makes a Visit Optional

The routing library's tool for "this visit may be skipped" is the disjunction: a set of indices of which at most a given number (by default one) are visited. With a penalty, each index of the set that is left out adds the penalty to the objective. A disjunction holding a single index is exactly "visit this site, or pay to skip it". The OR-Tools dropped-visits guide makes all 16 of its locations optional this way:

Three rules come from AddDisjunction's docstring:

  • The list holds indices, not nodes, so convert with NodeToIndex.
  • The depot, and every vehicle's start and end, cannot be in a disjunction. The loop starts at node 1 for that reason.
  • A site with no disjunction is mandatory. That is how the Valley model keeps the urgent sites: it gives a disjunction to the routine sites only.

The docstring adds a fourth rule: a negative penalty makes the disjunction mandatory. The penalty must be positive for the visit to be optional; a negative one forces the visit, so AddDisjunction([index], -1) keeps the site just as leaving it out of every disjunction does. That is handy when the penalties come from data and a must-visit site is marked with a negative number. negative_penalty.py runs the two-crew Valley day of the next section twice, with A183's penalty at 10,000 and at -1:

With a penalty of 10,000, A183 is among the seven dropped sites; with -1 it is on crew 1's route, and seven other routine sites are dropped instead.

The guide's data has demands that add up to 70 for four vehicles of capacity 15 (60 in all), so at least 10 units of demand must be dropped:

The objective is the distance plus 1,000 per dropped node. The guide's page shows a different answer, nodes 6 and 15 dropped for 7,936. Its program stops the search after one second; this copy stops it after 100 solutions. Both are plans the model accepts, from searches that ran for different lengths, and how a search is stopped is lesson First-solution strategies, local search and search limits's subject.

The Penalty Decides What Gets Dropped

The penalty is the price of skipping a visit, in the objective's units (here meters or drive minutes). The solver drops a site whenever dropping it saves more than its penalty, and that one fact sets the rule for choosing penalties. The dropped-visits guide states it: a penalty larger than the sum of all arc costs means dropping a visit can never pay for itself, so the solver drops only what it must to make the plan feasible. A smaller penalty lets it drop a visit just because it is out of the way.

valley_drops.py runs the Valley day four ways, with vans of 12 kits:

Read the runs as business policies:

  • Penalty 10,000 for every routine site is above the 8,334-minute sum of all arcs, so no drop pays for itself in driving. With two crews, all eight urgent sites are kept (they have no disjunction), and seven routine ones are dropped. With equal penalties, the solver has no reason to prefer one routine site over another, so which seven go is decided by geography: it kept A064, A096 and A305.
  • Penalty proportional to the consequence of failure (cof) says what the planner means: skipping a transformer whose failure costs $3.5M is worse than skipping one that costs $0.2M. Seven sites are still dropped, but now A183 (cof 2,978) is kept and A064 (cof 523) is dropped.
  • A penalty that is too small (cof // 100, 2 to 35 minutes) breaks the plan in the other direction. With three crews and a penalty of 10,000, the solver visits all 18 sites in 283 drive minutes, so three crews can do the whole day. With cof // 100 it drops A064, A281 and A302 anyway, for penalties of 5, 22 and 2 minutes (29 in all), because the plan without them drives 215 minutes: 68 minutes saved for 29 paid. Those three transformers go uninspected to save fuel.

A penalty is a trade-off rate between two things the objective adds up. Put both in the same units, check that the largest saving a drop could bring cannot exceed the penalty of a site you must not lose, and when nothing may be skipped, leave the site out of every disjunction.

A Dropped Visit Is an Index That Points to Itself

A dropped site is on no route, and the routing model encodes that by making it its own successor: $\text{next}_i = i$. So you find dropped visits by testing every index that is neither a start nor an end, as the guide's printer does:

The start test matters: a start index is never a visit, even when its vehicle is unused (then it points to its end). The ends lie past routing.Size() (lesson The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP's index table), so the loop never reaches them; the IsEnd test only makes the intent plain.

The penalty a plan paid is the sum of its dropped sites' penalties, and the objective minus that sum is the driving. valley_drops.py prints the split on every line ("drive 229 + penalties 833"), and a planner should get the same split: a total of 1,062 means little until you know how much of it is fuel and how much is risk left in the field.

Problem statement

A grocery delivers online orders with two vans that carry 8 crates each, and today's orders add up to more than both vans hold. Prepaid orders must go out today; the others may wait until tomorrow, at a cost the shop sets per order (a discount voucher, a lost customer). optional.py builds the routes and the van capacity. Write two functions:

  • make_optional(routing, manager, penalties): each node in penalties may be skipped at penalties[node]; every other order stays required.
  • skipped(routing, manager, solution): the nodes on no route, in increasing order, so the shop can send the vouchers.

In the lesson's notation, with $O$ the optional orders, $\pi_a$ their penalties and $\text{active}_i = 1$ when a route visits index $i$:

Before you code, say which call makes one order optional, what it takes (a node or an index), and which indices skipped must leave out of its test.

The tests price hand-built plans and read a solve, so any correct formulation passes. About six lines.

Pickup and Delivery Is a Pair, a Same-Vehicle Row and an Order Row


The test set must be collected at A157 and taken to A230. The pickup and the delivery are two visits with two rules between them: the same crew does both, and it reaches the pickup first. The OR-Tools pickup-and-delivery guide writes each rule as one constraint, plus a call that tells the search the two visits belong together:




  • routing.solver() is the constraint-programming solver under the routing model, and Add posts a constraint on its variables directly. This is how you write a rule that no dimension or disjunction expresses.
  • routing.VehicleVar(i) is the vehicle that visits index $i$; equal vehicle variables put both stops on one route.
  • The order row compares cumuls of a dimension that only grows along a route (distance driven), so "smaller cumul" means "earlier on the route". A time dimension works the same way.
  • AddPickupAndDelivery(pickup, delivery) declares the pair. Its docstring says it helps the search, especially local search, which then moves both stops together.

The Model


The Valley model with optional visits that valley_model.build makes when it gets a penalty, and the pickup-and-delivery rows of valley_test_set.py. Lessons The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP to Time windows and service times (VRPTW) gave routes, Kits and Time; this lesson added the disjunctions and the pair. It is a vehicle routing model, searched by the routing library's heuristics.


Sets. $N$ the nodes, node 0 the yard and 1 to 18 the sites; $U \subseteq N$ the urgent sites; $R = N \setminus (U \cup \{0\})$ the routine sites; $V$ the crews; $I$ the indices; $P$ the pickup-delivery pairs $(p, d)$ (one pair, A157 to A230, in valley_test_set.py).


Parameters. As in lesson Time windows and service times (VRPTW) ($c_{ab}$, $\text{service}_a$, $[a_i, b_i]$, $H$, $W$, $k_a$, $K$), plus $\pi_i > 0$, the penalty for skipping routine site $i$.


Variables. As in lesson Time windows and service times (VRPTW), plus $\text{active}_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for each site index: 1 if a route visits it. A site with $\text{active}_i = 0$ has $\text{next}_i = i$.

Summary


  • A disjunction with a penalty makes a visit optional. AddDisjunction([index], penalty) per optional site; a site with no disjunction, or with a negative penalty, stays mandatory; the depot can never be in one.
  • The penalty decides what gets dropped. The solver drops a site when the saving exceeds the penalty. Above the sum of all arc costs, it drops only what it must; proportional to value, it drops the least valuable; too small, it drops sites it could have visited.
  • A dropped visit is an index that points to itself. Test solution.Value(routing.NextVar(i)) == i for every index that is neither a start nor an end, and report penalties apart from driving.
  • Pickup and delivery is a pair, a same-vehicle row and an order row. In OR-Tools 9.15, AddPickupAndDelivery alone accepted a pair split across two vehicles; the same-vehicle row refuses it.

Design Note: Penalties Turn "Infeasible" Into "Here Is What It Costs"


A model with hard visits answers a short fleet with nothing (lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP)). A model with penalties always has a plan (drop everything is one), and it answers a different, better question: "with the crews I have, what is the least painful set of visits to postpone?" The price of that answer is that the penalties are now part of the model, and a planner must agree to them. Write them in business terms (here, a share of the consequence of failure), show the dropped list next to the routes, and treat "seven sites postponed" as a result to explain, not a detail.
