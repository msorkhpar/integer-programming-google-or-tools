  16. Time windows and service times

The lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP) plan routed three crews by drive minutes and kits. Then the Valley's field supervisor reads it and asks two questions. First: where is the inspection itself? Each one takes 30 to 60 minutes on site (longer for older transformers), and the plan behaves as if the crews never stop. Second: some substations can only be entered at certain hours. Five need a switching outage that operations books for the morning (crews must arrive between 08:00 and 11:00), four need one booked for the afternoon (arrive between 12:00 and 15:00), and the shift runs from 08:00 to 16:00.

A VRP in which each visit must start inside a given interval is a VRP with time windows (VRPTW). The routing library models it with the tool of lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP), a dimension, this time one whose cumul is the clock. With inspection time counted, the planner gives the day four crews, each with a smaller van that carries 9 kits. All times are minutes after 08:00, and the drive times are fictional.

Time Is a Dimension Whose Transit Is Service Plus Travel

Make the clock a dimension: the cumul at a stop is the time the crew arrives there. Lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP)'s rule says the cumul at the next stop is this stop's cumul plus the transit (plus slack). So the transit of the arc from stop $a$ to stop $b$ must be everything that happens between arriving at $a$ and arriving at $b$: the inspection at $a$, then the drive.

The OR-Tools VRPTW guide's example has no service time, so its callback returns the travel time only. The Valley callback adds the inspection at the stop being left:

The time callback feeds the Time dimension only. The arc cost (the objective) stays the drive minutes: inspection time is the same whatever the order, so adding it to the objective would change nothing but the number.

What happens if you forget the service time? valley_windows.py solves the day both ways, then replays the travel-only plan with the real inspection times:

All four crews are printed; only crew 1's replay misses a window. The model thinks crew 1 inspects seven substations and is back by 10:21. Driven with real inspections, it is back at 14:21 and arrives at A064 and A302 after their morning windows have closed, so two outages booked for nothing. The model was solved correctly; it was the wrong model.

A Time Window Is a Range on the Arrival Cumul

A window says "arrive between $a_i$ and $b_i$", and the arrival time is the cumul, so the window is a bound on one variable: time_dimension.CumulVar(index).SetRange(a, b). The VRPTW guide's example:

Two details carry over to every time-window model:

  • NodeToIndex turns your node into the solver's index before you take its cumul. The loop skips the depot, because the depot is not one index but a start and an end per vehicle; its window goes on each vehicle's start index instead.
  • The capacity is the horizon. No cumul may exceed it, the ends included, so a capacity of 30 means every vehicle is back by minute 30. The Valley model uses the shift: capacity 480, back by 16:00.

The Valley model sets each site's window the same way, and leaves the yard's start and end to the capacity:

A window that cannot be met makes the model infeasible, like a capacity that is too small. The search can then only report that it found no routes (lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP)); it never proves why, so check windows against drive times from the yard before you blame the solver.

Slack Is Waiting, and the Start Time Is Left Free

A crew that arrives at A230 at 10:00 when its window opens at 12:00 must wait two hours. In the dimension rule, that wait is the slack: cumul at the next stop = cumul + transit + slack. The second argument of AddDimension bounds it; the guide allows 30 minutes, the Valley model 120 (MAX_WAIT). A slack bound of 0 forbids waiting anywhere on the route.

The fourth argument is False in both models. For distance and load (lesson Vehicles, capacities and dimensions (CVRP)) the start was fixed at 0; for time it is left free, so the solver may also choose when each crew leaves, between the yard window's bounds. A crew that would only wait at its first stop can leave later instead.

The guide then calls AddVariableMinimizedByFinalizer on every start and end cumul. The routing search fixes the successors first and the dimension values last, in what its docstring calls the solution finalizer; this call asks the finalizer to pick the smallest feasible value for those variables, so each crew leaves and returns as early as its route allows:

The Valley plan with inspection time counted:

Crew 1 waits 166 minutes, more than the 120 that MAX_WAIT allows at one stop. That is not a contradiction: the bound is per stop. A087 opens at 08:00, but its arrival range starts at 08:56. The crew inspects A087 for 60 minutes and drives 4 to A230, which opens at 12:00; arriving at A087 before 08:56 would mean waiting more than 120 minutes there. So the crew waits 46 minutes at the yard (slack at its start) and 120 at A087. The program computes the waits from the arrival times ($\text{cumul}_{\text{next}} - \text{cumul} - \text{transit}$) rather than reading the slack variables.

Problem statement

A lift-maintenance company sends one engineer a day to several office buildings. Each building lets the engineer in only during an access window, each visit takes some minutes of work, the engineer may wait outside a building for up to max_wait minutes, and the shift ends at shift_end minutes after the start. windows.py builds the route priced in travel minutes; write add_time(routing, manager, travel, service, windows, shift_end, max_wait) so the model allows exactly the plans that keep these rules. windows[node] = (opens, closes) is the arrival window of a building; the depot has none, and the engineer may leave it whenever suits.

Each Stop Gets an Arrival Range, Not One Time


The plan prints two times per stop, such as A087 08:56-10:10. A time dimension's cumul is not fixed by the routes: any arrival between solution.Min(cumul) and solution.Max(cumul) keeps the whole route feasible, given the windows and waits around it. The VRPTW guide calls this range the solution window: it lies inside the stop's own window, and when its two ends differ the crew has room to wait there. The guide prints it as Time(min,max):






Two numbers, two meanings. The objective, 71, is the arc cost: the sum of travel times. The total time of all routes, 82 minutes, is the sum of each vehicle's arrival back at the depot, which also counts waiting. Vehicle 1 shows where waiting enters: it can reach node 1 four minutes after node 7, but node 1's window opens at 7, so a vehicle that left node 7 at minute 2 waits.


For a planner, the earliest time is the schedule, and the range is the room the crew has if something runs late. At A087 08:56-10:10 the crew can arrive anywhere in a 74-minute range and still make A230 at 12:00; at A136 12:00-12:00 it has none.


Where do the two ends of A087's range come from? Both neighbours of the stop limit it, and arrival_range.py reads each limit from the solved plan:






Seen from A230 alone, any arrival at A087 up to 10:56 still reaches A230 at 12:00 (10:56 plus 64 minutes), and none before 08:56, which would mean waiting more than 120 minutes at A087. But the finalizer fixed crew 1's departure at 08:00, the minimum of its start cumul, and the slack at the yard is bounded by MAX_WAIT like any other: leaving at 08:00 and driving 10 minutes, the crew reaches A087 at 10:10 at the latest. The range is the overlap of the two sides, 08:56 to 10:10, so its upper end comes from the yard, not from A230.


A Soft Upper Bound Charges for Lateness Instead of Forbidding It


A window is hard: the model refuses a plan that breaks it. Some limits are preferences. The supervisor would like every crew back by 14:30 so the afternoon paperwork gets done, but a crew back at 15:00 is not a disaster. A soft upper bound on a cumul adds a cost for each unit above the bound instead of forbidding it. Its docstring states it exactly: cost 0 while the cumul is at most the bound, and coefficient × (cumul − bound) above it.



valley_model.py puts it on each crew's end, the return to the yard, with 5 drive-minutes of cost for each minute after 14:30:



The first plan has crew 3 back at 15:38, 68 minutes after 14:30. With the soft bound, every crew is back by 14:17, and no lateness is charged, so the objective, 261, is all driving. Look at that number again: 261 drive minutes is less than the first plan's 287, even though the second model has an extra cost and no extra freedom. So the first plan was not the best the first model could do; it was where that search stopped. The extra cost pushed the search down a different path that happened to end lower. That is what a heuristic search does, and lesson First-solution strategies, local search and search limits shows how to search harder and what you can and cannot conclude.


Soft bounds work on any cumul: a site's arrival (a preferred visit time) or a load. A soft lower bound exists too (SetCumulVarSoftLowerBound; its docstring suggests it for earliness costs). Choose the coefficient in the objective's units: here 5 means "one minute late is as bad as five minutes of driving".

Summary


  • Time is a dimension whose transit is service plus travel. The cumul is the arrival time; the callback adds the inspection at the stop being left, then the drive. Forget the service time and the plan is wrong, not merely worse.
  • A time window is a range on the arrival cumul: CumulVar(NodeToIndex(node)).SetRange(a, b). The dimension's capacity is the horizon, so it also bounds the return to the depot.
  • Slack is waiting, and the start time is left free. The slack bound limits waiting at each stop; fix_start_cumul_to_zero=False lets crews leave later; the finalizer picks the earliest times.
  • Each stop gets an arrival range, not one time: solution.Min and solution.Max of its cumul. The objective counts travel; route times also count waiting.
  • A soft upper bound charges for lateness instead of forbidding it: coefficient × minutes over the bound, added to the objective.

Design Note: Time Windows in a MIP and in the Routing Library


A MIP states a time window with continuous arrival times and one big-M row per arc: "if the vehicle drives from $i$ to $j$, then $t_j \ge t_i + \text{service}_i + c_{ij}$", relaxed by a large constant $M$ when it does not, so that the row holds whatever the two times are. Those rows are many, one per arc, and the larger $M$ is, the less the LP relaxation, where the arc's 0/1 variable may be fractional, says about the arrival times. Two lessons later in the course treat this in depth: lesson Deriving big-M from bounds derives such constants from bounds, and lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics shows what loose ones do to the LP bound. The routing library needs none of them: the Time dimension computes arrival times along each route as the search builds it, and a move that breaks a window is refused before it is ever priced. The resource-constraints guide shows how far the same idea goes: a depot with two loading docks becomes an interval per vehicle at its start and end cumul, under a cumulative limit of 2, the same kind of constraint lesson Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows builds in CP-SAT.
