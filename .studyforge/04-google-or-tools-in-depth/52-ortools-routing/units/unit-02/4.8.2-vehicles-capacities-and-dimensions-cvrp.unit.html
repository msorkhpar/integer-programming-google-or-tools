  15. Vehicles, dimensions and capacities

Lesson The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP sent one crew to the eight urgent Valley sites. The full list has 18 substations: the eight urgent ones and ten routine inspections. That is too much for one crew, so three crews share the day, and two limits appear. Each inspection uses one to three oil-sample kits (older transformers need more samples), and a crew truck carries 12. And the union agreement caps a crew at 150 minutes behind the wheel. The planner wants each site assigned to a crew, an order for each crew, and both limits kept.

Several vehicles that share the visits make a vehicle routing problem (VRP); with a load limit per vehicle it is a capacitated VRP (CVRP). This lesson adds both, and the one idea the routing library uses for every quantity that builds up along a route: the dimension. The drive times are fictional, as in lesson The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP.

One Index Manager Serves the Whole Fleet

More vehicles change one argument: the second argument of RoutingIndexManager is the number of vehicles, and the manager creates a start and an end index for each (lesson The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP's index table showed two vehicles). Nothing else changes: one transit callback and one arc cost cover every vehicle, and the solver decides which vehicle visits each site.

Each vehicle's route is read the same way as the TSP's, starting from routing.Start(vehicle). A vehicle with nothing to do drives from its start straight to its end; routing.IsVehicleUsed( solution, vehicle) is false for it, and the guides' printers skip it.

Fewer total kilometers is not always what a planner wants, though. The OR-Tools VRP guide sends four vehicles to 16 locations and wants the longest of the four routes to be as short as possible, so that no driver gets a far longer day than the others. It gets that with a dimension, the next section's subject. Here is its result, from this module's adapted copy:

The objective is not the distance: it is 100 times the longest route plus the total. Where the 100 comes from is the next section.

A Dimension Accumulates a Quantity Along Each Route

A dimension is a quantity that builds up as a vehicle drives its route: distance driven, time on the clock, load in the truck. For every index $i$ it has three variables:

  • the cumul $\text{cumul}_i$, the quantity's value when the vehicle arrives at $i$ (dimension.CumulVar(i));
  • the transit $\text{transit}_i$, what the arc from $i$ to its successor adds, given by a callback;
  • the slack $\text{slack}_i \ge 0$, an extra amount the vehicle may add at $i$ (waiting time, in lesson Time windows and service times (VRPTW)).

They are tied by one rule, written in the docstring of AddDimension:

routing.AddDimension(callback_handle, slack_max, capacity, fix_start_cumul_to_zero, name) creates one. slack_max bounds each slack, capacity bounds every cumul ($0 \le \text{cumul}_i \le \text{capacity}$), the flag fixes the cumul at each vehicle's start to 0, and name is how you get it back with routing.GetDimensionOrDie(name). In the VRP guide's code:

The same callback that prices the arcs now feeds the dimension, so a vehicle's cumul at its end is the distance it drove, and the capacity 3000 caps every route at 3000 m. A dimension is a constraint by default; it changes the objective only if you ask. SetGlobalSpanCostCoefficient(100) asks: it adds 100 times the global span, the largest cumul at any vehicle's end minus the smallest cumul at any vehicle's start. Every start is 0 here, so the span is the longest route, and the objective becomes 100 × longest + total, the 177,500 above.

On the Valley day the same two calls cap each crew at 150 driving minutes and, in a second run, weigh the longest drive by 100:

The program walks crew 0's route in the second run and prints each stop's cumul next to the transit of the arc leaving it. Each cumul is the previous one plus the previous transit (the slack is 0):

Where the dimension changes the plan is the comparison of the two runs:

With only the total in the objective, crew 0 drives 107 minutes and crew 2 drives 52. With the span weighted, the longest drive falls to 95 minutes and the total rises from 234 to 95 + 95 + 66 = 256 minutes; the objective is 100 × 95 + 256 = 9,756. Which plan is better is a business question: fewer kilometers, or a fairer day. The coefficient is how you answer it.

Read the cumul at the route's end index to get the route's total: CumulVar(index) after the walk loop, where index is the end. That is how both printers get "drive 95 min".

A Capacity Is a Dimension Whose Transit Is the Demand


A truck's load is a dimension too. Its transit at a stop is the stop's demand (here, the kits the inspection uses), whatever stop comes next, so the callback takes one index, not two. The guide calls it a unary callback and registers it with RegisterUnaryTransitCallback. The capacity can differ by vehicle, so the guide uses AddDimensionWithVehicleCapacity, which takes a list with one capacity per vehicle:




Slack is 0 because a truck cannot gain load from nowhere, and the start is fixed at 0 because it leaves empty. The guide's four vehicles each carry 15, and its 16 demands add up to exactly 60:



Read the loads closely. Vehicle 0 arrives at node 7 with Load(0), and node 7's demand of 8 only shows at node 3. The cumul is the value on arrival: the transit of a stop is added on the arc that leaves it. So the cumul at a stop is everything picked up before it, and the cumul at the end is the route's whole load, 15 for every vehicle here. The guide's own printer adds each stop's demand itself and shows the load after the stop; both readings are right once you know which one you are printing. The total, 6,208 m, is the one the CVRP guide prints. (This copy stops its search after 100 solutions instead of the guide's one second, so the result is the same on every run; lesson First-solution strategies, local search and search limits explains why.)


The Valley model adds a "Kits" dimension the same way, with a callback that returns a site's kits:




Every crew in the runs above ends with 12 kits or fewer. A model can hold as many dimensions as it has quantities (distance, time, kits, weight), each under its own name. The CVRP guide's advice for several kinds of load is one callback and one dimension per kind, each with a unique name: AddDimension returns False and creates nothing when the name is already taken.

Problem statement


A brewery delivers to pubs with two trucks. Each pub takes some kegs and some kilograms of bottled stock, and each truck has two limits: how many kegs fit on it and how much weight it may carry. The Search Does Not Prove That a Fleet Is Too Small


The Valley sites need 33 kits. With two crews the trucks carry 24, so no plan exists. A MIP solver would prove the model infeasible and say so (lesson Reading SolveResult: termination, primal and dual solutions, statistics reads MathOpt's termination reasons). The routing search is a heuristic, and a heuristic that finds nothing has not proved there is nothing to find:






The solve returns None, and routing.status() says why it stopped: ROUTING_FAIL_TIMEOUT, "time limit reached before finding a solution", not "proven infeasible". Two habits follow:


  • Check the arithmetic before you solve. Total demand against total capacity is one line of Python, and it gives the planner a reason ("2 crews carry 24 kits, the day needs 33") that the solver cannot.
  • Always set a limit when routes might not exist. The CVRP guide warns that an unsolvable problem can keep the search running for a long time. Lesson Optional visits with penalties, and pickup and delivery shows the other remedy: make some visits optional, so a plan always exists.


The Model


The capacitated Valley model valley_crews.py builds, in one place. Lesson The routing model: index manager, transit callbacks and the TSP gave the successor variables and routes; this lesson added the vehicles, the Drive and Kits dimensions and the span term. It is a vehicle routing model; the routing library searches it with heuristics.


Sets. $N$ the nodes, node 0 the yard and 1 to 18 the Valley sites; $V$ the crews, $|V| = 3$; $I$ the solver's indices: one per site, a start $s_v$ and an end $e_v$ per crew.


Parameters. $c_{ab}$ drive minutes from node $a$ to $b$ (integers, fictional); $k_a$ the kits site $a$ uses ($k_0 = 0$); $K_v = 12$ the kits crew $v$'s truck carries; $D = 150$ the most minutes a crew may drive; $\beta$ the span coefficient (0 or 100).


Variables. $\text{next}_i \in I$ for each non-end index (the successor); $\text{vehicle}_i \in V$; $0 \le \text{drive}_i \le D$ and $0 \le \text{load}_i \le K_{\text{vehicle}_i}$ for every index, the cumuls of the Drive and Kits dimensions, all integer.

Summary


  • One index manager serves the whole fleet. The vehicle count is the manager's second argument; the solver assigns sites to vehicles, and an unused vehicle drives start to end.
  • A dimension accumulates a quantity along each route: cumul at the next stop = cumul + transit + slack, with every cumul at most the capacity. A dimension is a constraint; a span coefficient also puts it in the objective.
  • A capacity is a dimension whose transit is the demand, from a unary callback, with one capacity per vehicle. The cumul is the load on arrival.
  • The search does not prove that a fleet is too small. It returns None with a failure status; check total demand against total capacity yourself, and set a limit.

Design Note: Dimensions Are the Routing Library's Linear Rows


In a MIP, every limit is a row. In the routing library almost every limit is a dimension: one callback for how the quantity changes on an arc, one capacity for how high it may go, and a slack for how much it may rise on its own. Distance, time, load, crew hours and money all fit the same shape. When a new rule arrives, the first question is: "what quantity builds up along the route, and what bounds it?" If the answer fits, the rule is a dimension, and the solver's moves already know how to respect it.
