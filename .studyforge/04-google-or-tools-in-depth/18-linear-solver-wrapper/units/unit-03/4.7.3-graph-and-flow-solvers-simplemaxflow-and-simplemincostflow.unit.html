  15. Graph and flow solvers

Lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over shipped GridCap's spare transformers from two depots, through a hub or direct, to four regions, as an LP: one flow variable per route, one balance row per place. Two questions from stores come back to the same network. Before winter: how many spares can the network deliver at most, and which routes are the bottleneck? And every week: ship this week's spares at the least cost. Neither question needs an LP solver. OR-Tools has a graph library with solvers built for exactly these two problems, the maximum flow and the minimum-cost flow, that work on whole numbers and take the network itself as input, not a model.

This lesson teaches the two solvers the way the OR-Tools pages "Maximum Flows" and "Minimum Cost Flows" do, on their own examples, and then puts lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over's network through them. The answer must match the LP's $299k. GridCap, its depots and its network are fictional. Four rules, one per heading.

SimpleMaxFlow Finds the Most a Network Can Carry From a Source to a Sink

A network (lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over) is a set of nodes joined by arcs: one-way links, each with a capacity, the most it can carry. The maximum flow problem picks two nodes, a source $s$ and a sink $t$, and asks for the largest amount that can travel from $s$ to $t$. Every arc must stay within its capacity, and every other node must pass on exactly what it receives. As an LP, with $f_a$ the flow on arc $a$ and $v$ the amount delivered:

SimpleMaxFlow solves it without your writing any of these rows. You give it the arcs, and it returns the flows. The OR-Tools example, from Taha's textbook (adapted from simple_max_flow_program.py):

How to read the API:

  • Nodes are integers 0, 1, 2, ... and are created by mentioning them in an arc. There are no names. A program with named places keeps its own name-to-number table.
  • Arcs are given as three parallel arrays: start nodes, end nodes and capacities, where entry $k$ of each describes arc $k$. add_arcs_with_capacity adds them all and returns their arc indices. add_arc_with_capacity(tail, head, capacity) adds one arc and returns its index.
  • solve(source, sink) returns a status. Check it against smf.OPTIMAL before reading anything else; the other statuses report bad input or a possible integer overflow.
  • Results are read by arc index: optimal_flow() is the total, flow(arc) or flows(arcs) is the flow on arcs, and tail(arc), head(arc) and capacity(arc) describe an arc.

Every arc out of node 0 is full: 20, 30 and 10 add up to 60. No plan can send more than those three arcs carry, so 60 is the most possible. That reasoning is the max-flow min-cut theorem. A cut splits the nodes into a source side (holding $s$) and a sink side (holding $t$). Its capacity is the total capacity of the arcs that go from the source side to the sink side. Every unit of flow has to cross the cut, so every cut's capacity bounds the flow, and the theorem says the smallest cut's capacity equals the maximum flow. The solver hands back a minimum cut:

The source side is $\{0\}$, and its arcs out carry $20 + 30 + 10 = 60$, the max flow. (Nodes 2 and 3 are on neither list: get_sink_side_min_cut returns the nodes that can still reach the sink through unused capacity, which describes a second minimum cut, and the two answers need not cover every node.) The cut is the useful part of the answer: it names the arcs to widen if you want more flow. Widening any other arc changes nothing.

Many Sources and Many Sinks Become One Pair With a Super Source and a Super Sink

GridCap's question has two depots and four regions, not one source and one sink. The standard trick: add a super source with an arc to each depot, of capacity equal to that depot's shelf, and a super sink with an arc from each region, of capacity equal to its demand. A flow from the super source to the super sink is then a shipment that respects every shelf and fills no region beyond its need.

NODE maps each place's name to its number, in the order depots, hub, regions (it is in spares_network.py, with lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over's data). The program also lists the arcs that cross the minimum cut, the bottleneck, and tries the network with the Northgate–Central route closed:

With every route open, the network can deliver all 70 spares the regions need. The minimum cut is the four arcs into the super sink: the bottleneck is the demand itself, so the routes are wide enough.

With Northgate–Central closed, at most 60 can arrive, and the cut explains the shortfall in three terms. Southport can send no more than its 35. Northgate, cut off from the hub, can reach only Valley directly (10) and Ridge (whose need of 15 is the limit). $35 + 10 + 15 = 60$. Northgate still holds 20 spares that nothing can carry. To get the last 10 delivered, stores must widen one of those cut arcs, for example raise Northgate–Valley's capacity, or reopen the hub route. A bigger Southport–Central route would not help, because it is not in the cut.

Problem statement

A generator rental firm keeps portable generators in yards and sends them to towns after a storm. Before the season it asks how many generators its road routes could deliver at most.

Write most_delivered(stock, needs, routes) in generators.py, using SimpleMaxFlow. stock maps each yard to the generators it holds, needs maps each town to the most it can take, and routes maps (from, to) pairs of place names to the most generators that route carries. A route may pass through a place that is neither a yard nor a town. Return the largest number that can arrive at the towns in total.

SimpleMinCostFlow Ships Supplies to Demands at Least Cost, and the Supplies Must Balance


The minimum-cost flow adds a cost per unit to each arc, and a supply $b_n$ to each node: positive at a node that sends units out, negative at a node that takes them in (a demand), 0 at a node that only passes them on. It asks for the cheapest flow that meets every supply exactly:



Add up every balance row and each arc's flow appears once with $+1$ (at its tail) and once with $-1$ (at its head), so the left sides sum to 0. The supplies must therefore add up to 0, or no flow can satisfy the rows. The OR-Tools example (adapted from simple_min_cost_flow_program.py, from Bradley, Hax and Magnanti's Applied Mathematical Programming) has one source of 20 and two demands of 5 and 15:






The calls mirror SimpleMaxFlow: arcs as parallel arrays, now with a unit cost too (add_arcs_with_capacity_and_unit_cost), and supplies set per node (set_node_supply(node, supply), or set_nodes_supplies for many). solve() takes no source or sink: the supplies say where flow starts and ends. Check node 3: in $4 + 12 = 16$, out $11$, net $-5$, its demand.

The fix is to say where the other 10 spares go. They stay on the shelf. So add a shelf node that demands the surplus, $b = -10$, with a zero-cost arc from each depot to it, each as wide as that depot's stock:






$299k, and the same routes lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over's LP chose. The program solves that LP again in MathOpt for the check. The shelf node is the min-cost-flow form of the LP's "$\le$ supply" row: the depot's slack has become an arc.


The data must be whole numbers. Capacities, costs and supplies are integers in this API, and a fractional cost is refused before anything is solved:



When a cost has cents or tenths, scale every cost by the same power of ten (here, to $100s) and divide the optimal cost by it afterwards. Scaling every cost by the same positive factor leaves the best flow unchanged. In return for integer data you get an integer answer: every flow is a whole number, which lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over showed an LP of this shape also achieves at its corners.


When Demand Cannot Be Met, solve_max_flow_with_min_cost Ships What It Can


solve() needs balanced supplies and a network able to carry them. Close Northgate–Central and raise Valley's demand by 10, so that supply (80) and demand (80) balance but the routes cannot carry it, and solve() reports the plain truth:



solve_max_flow_with_min_cost() answers a different, often more useful question: of all flows that deliver as much as possible (from the positive-supply nodes to the negative ones, within each node's supply), which is cheapest? Here it ships 60 of the 80 wanted, at $259k: the extra 10 for Valley cannot get past the same cut the max flow found. maximum_flow() reports how much was shipped. Compare it with the total demand to learn whether everyone got their spares.

The Model


The two problems the solvers take, as lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over would write them. The solvers build these rows themselves from the arcs and supplies.


Maximum flow (SimpleMaxFlow). Sets. $N$, the nodes; $A \subseteq N \times N$, the arcs; a source $s \in N$ and a sink $t \in N$. Parameters. $u_a$, the capacity of arc $a$ (whole number). Variables. $0 \le f_a \le u_a$ for $a \in A$; $v \ge 0$, the amount delivered.



For GridCap's question, $N$ adds a super source $s$ with arcs $(s, d)$ of capacity $\text{shelf}_d$ to each depot $d$, and a super sink $t$ with arcs $(r, t)$ of capacity $\text{need}_r$ from each region $r$.


Minimum-cost flow (SimpleMinCostFlow.solve). Sets. $N$, $A$ as above. Parameters. $u_a$, capacity; $c_a$, cost per unit ($k), whole numbers; $b_n$, supply of node $n$ (positive: sends; negative: demands), with $\sum_n b_n = 0$. Variables. $0 \le f_a \le u_a$ for $a \in A$.



For GridCap's shipment: $b$ is +45 at Northgate, +35 at Southport, 0 at Central, minus each region's demand, and $-10$ at the shelf node, whose arcs from the depots cost 0.


Summary


  • SimpleMaxFlow finds the most a network can carry from a source to a sink. Nodes are integers, arcs come in parallel arrays, solve(s, t) returns a status, and the minimum cut names the arcs that limit the flow: 60 on the OR-Tools example, all of them leaving node 0.
  • Many sources and sinks become one pair with a super source (arcs as wide as each shelf) and a super sink (arcs as wide as each demand). GridCap's network delivers all 70 spares, and 60 with Northgate–Central closed.
  • SimpleMinCostFlow ships supplies to demands at least cost, and the supplies must balance. A surplus is UNBALANCED until a shelf node takes it; then the cost is $299k, the LP's answer. Data must be whole numbers.
  • When demand cannot be met, solve_max_flow_with_min_cost ships what it can, at least cost. Check maximum_flow() against the demand.

Design Note: Name the Nodes Once, Next to the Data


The graph solvers know nodes only as numbers, and an off-by-one in a node table ships spares to the wrong region with no error. Keep one table from names to numbers next to the data (spares_network.py has NAMES and NODE), build every arc through it, and translate every answer back through it. Extra nodes, such as a super source, a sink or a shelf, get numbers after the named ones, so adding them never renumbers a real place.
