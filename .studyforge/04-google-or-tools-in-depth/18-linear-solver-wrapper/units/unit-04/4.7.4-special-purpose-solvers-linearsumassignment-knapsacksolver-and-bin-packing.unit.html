  17. Special-purpose solvers

Three requests reach GridCap's work planners in one week. Each of the eight crews must lead one of the eight projects of the tiny portfolio this season: which crew leads which project, at the least cost? A contract crew works one 12-month season: which projects give it the most value? And the distribution and vegetation work must be hired out in whole seasons: how few contract crews can do it all? These are an assignment, a knapsack and a bin packing, three model families that a MIP can also state, as this lesson does to check each answer. Lessons 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack and Multiple knapsack and generalized assignment, later in the course, model the knapsack and assignment families as MIPs in depth.

OR-Tools has a dedicated solver for the first two, SimpleLinearSumAssignment and KnapsackSolver. Each accepts only its own problem shape, works on whole numbers, and needs no model at all. This lesson teaches both the way the OR-Tools pages do, checks each against a MIP on GridCap, and shows where each one stops: the side rule that sends you back to a min-cost flow or a MIP. Bin packing has no dedicated solver in OR-Tools, so it is a MIP, and a simple bound sometimes makes the MIP unnecessary. GridCap, its crews, rates and seasons are fictional. Five rules, one per heading.

LinearSumAssignment Matches Each Worker to One Task at Least Cost

The assignment problem: $n$ workers, $n$ tasks, and a cost $c_{w,t}$ for worker $w$ to do task $t$. Each worker does exactly one task, each task is done by exactly one worker, and the total cost is as small as possible. As a MIP, with $x_{w,t} = 1$ when worker $w$ does task $t$:

SimpleLinearSumAssignment, in ortools.graph.python.linear_sum_assignment, solves exactly this problem and nothing else. You give it one arc per allowed (worker, task) pair with its cost; workers are the left nodes and tasks the right nodes, each numbered from 0. The OR-Tools example, adapted from assignment_linear_sum_assignment.py:

The meshgrid lines only turn the 4 × 4 table into three flat arrays (row number, column number, cost), one entry per cell. add_arc_with_cost(worker, task, cost) adds one arc at a time, which is what the GridCap program below does. After solve():

  • the status is OPTIMAL, INFEASIBLE (no way to give every worker a task through the arcs given), or POSSIBLE_OVERFLOW (costs so large that the integer arithmetic could overflow);
  • optimal_cost() is the total;
  • right_mate(w) is the task worker w got, and assignment_cost(w) what it costs;
  • num_nodes() is the number of left nodes, the workers.

Costs are integers, as for the flow solvers in lesson Graph and flow solvers: SimpleMaxFlow and SimpleMinCostFlow. Scale cents or decimals to whole units first:

Now GridCap. Two crews are based in each region: a line crew, qualified for every program, at $24k per crew-month, and a field crew, not qualified for transmission, at $18k. Leading a project costs the crew's rate times the project's crew-months, plus travel when the project is in another region. A field crew on a transmission project is not allowed, so that pair gets no arc, the same rule a MIP follows by giving a pair that is not allowed no variable at all (lesson Multiple knapsack and generalized assignment, later in the course, uses that rule throughout):

Three solvers, one answer: the dedicated solver, the MIP of the model above solved by HiGHS over the 52 allowed pairs, and that MIP's LP relaxation, solved by GLOP with every $x$ between 0 and 1. The relaxation came back whole with no branching. That is the property lesson LP patterns: blending, flows, multi-period carry-over described: the assignment rows have the structure of a flow network (each variable in exactly one worker row and one task row), so with whole-number data the LP has a whole optimal corner. It is also why a solver built for this one problem exists. Checking it against the MIP, as here, is the test to keep whenever you replace a MIP with a special-purpose solver.

Two crews travel, and the data says why. Coast has one project, P003, a transmission project that no field crew may lead, so Coast's field crew has nothing at home and must work away. Valley has three projects for its two crews, so one crew must come to Valley. The solver sent Ridge line to P002 in Valley and Coast field to P001 in Ridge, the cheapest way to settle both moves for all eight crews at once.

The Table Must Be Square: Pad It With Dummy Tasks

The OR-Tools assignment example with a MIP (assignment_mip.py) has five workers and four tasks: one worker stays idle. SimpleLinearSumAssignment needs every left node matched, and it has no task for the fifth worker:

The fix is a dummy task, "no task", that every worker can take at cost 0. It squares the table to 5 × 5, and whoever gets it is the idle worker:

The MIP of the OR-Tools page writes the same rule directly, with "at most one task" rows for workers and "exactly one worker" rows for tasks, and agrees: 265. With more tasks than workers, pad the other way: dummy workers, at cost 0 (when a task may stay undone) or at a large cost (when leaving it undone should be a last resort).

A Side Rule Moves Assignment to a Min-Cost Flow or a MIP


The OR-Tools page "Assignment with Teams of Workers" adds one rule: six workers in two teams, four tasks, and each team takes at most two tasks. SimpleLinearSumAssignment has no place for that rule. Without it, the cheapest assignment gives team B three tasks:



The rule fits a min-cost flow (lesson Graph and flow solvers: SimpleMaxFlow and SimpleMinCostFlow), as the OR-Tools sample balance_min_flow.py builds it. A source sends one unit per task. It reaches the workers only through a team node whose arc from the source has capacity 2, so no team can pass on more than two units. Each worker passes at most one unit (arc of capacity 1), along a cost arc to a task, and each task passes one unit to the sink:






250, the cost the OR-Tools page reports, and the same as the MIP with one extra row per team, $\sum_{w \in \text{team}} \sum_t x_{w,t} \le 2$. The rule cost $250 - 235 = 15$.


Where does each tool stop?


Rule added to the assignmentTool
none; square table (pad with dummies if not)SimpleLinearSumAssignment
capacities on groups of workers or tasks (teams, regions)SimpleMinCostFlow with a node per group
a worker takes several tasks whose sizes add up against its capacityMIP: a generalized assignment (lesson Multiple knapsack and generalized assignment, later)
pairs of workers that must work together, or logical rulesMIP or CP-SAT


The sizes row is where the flow structure breaks. In a flow, every unit that passes a worker counts the same against its capacity; a task of size 10 and a task of size 3 cannot weigh differently. With sizes, the model is a generalized assignment: each task goes to exactly one worker, and the sizes of a worker's tasks must fit its capacity. Its LP relaxation may split a task between workers: one task of size 10 and two crews of capacity 6 have no whole assignment, yet the LP can put half of the task on each crew (5 of 6 used on each). So the MIP needs branching. Lesson Multiple knapsack and generalized assignment, later in the course, treats the generalized assignment in depth. The OR-Tools sample assignment_task_sizes_mip.py solves the sizes variant as a MIP, and the page "Assignment with Allowed Groups" solves the pairs variant with a MIP and with CP-SAT.


KnapsackSolver Picks Yes/No Items Under Capacity Rows, and Its Type Must Fit the Rows


Lesson A knapsack branch-and-bound in 116 lines used KnapsackSolver as a cross-check on GridCap, and lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack, later in the course, does so again. Here is its API in full. The 0-1 knapsack: items with values $v_i$ and weights $w_{r,i}$, each taken or not ($x_i \in \{0, 1\}$), maximizing total value under one or more capacity rows $\sum_i w_{r,i}\, x_i \le C_r$. The OR-Tools example, adapted from knapsack.py, has 50 items and one row:




  • KnapsackSolver(solver_type, name) picks the algorithm with a SolverType and gives the solver a name.
  • init(values, weights, capacities) takes a list of values, a list of weight lists, one per row, and a list of capacities, one per row. With one row, weights is still a list holding one list: [[7, 0, 30, ...]]. All three must be integers; the page says so, and GridCap's capex and values are already whole $k.
  • solve() returns the best total value, and best_solution_contains(i) says whether item i is in the best set.



7534, as the OR-Tools page reports. The program then runs the same data through every solver type, once with the example's one row and once with a second row added ("at most 30 items"). Each run is in a child process, for a reason the table makes plain:



Every type that ran agrees, with one row (7534) and with two (7513). But four types handle only one row, and the brute-force type, in its message's words, "less than 30" items. Five runs stopped, and each left one message. The 64-items type's message names the brute-force solver: the two types report a second row with the same text, so read the run's label, not the message, to know which type stopped. Given data they cannot handle, they do not raise a Python exception: a failed internal check stops the whole process (exit code −6 is the abort signal), and the messages above went to standard error. A try/except cannot catch that. So pick the type from the data: a MULTIDIMENSION type whenever there may be more than one row, and never the brute-force type for real data.

Bin Packing Minimizes the Number of Bins, and a Lower Bound Can Prove a Heuristic Optimal


Bin packing: items of sizes $s_i$, bins of capacity $C$; put every item in some bin, using as few bins as possible. OR-Tools has no dedicated bin-packing solver; its guide solves it as a MIP, in pywraplp with SCIP. Here is that model ported to MathOpt (lesson Migrating a pywraplp model to MathOpt), with $x_{i,b} = 1$ when item $i$ goes in bin $b$ and $y_b = 1$ when bin $b$ is used:



Three rows to read:


  • once: every item goes in exactly one bin.
  • cap does two jobs. Its right side is $C\, y_b$: a used bin holds at most $C$, and an unused bin ($y_b = 0$) holds nothing. Without $y_b$ on the right, items could sit in bins the objective does not count.
  • order is not in the OR-Tools guide's model. The bins are identical, so any packing can be relabelled in many ways: many packings that differ only in which identical bin got which label. That is called symmetry, and lesson Symmetry and how to break it, later in the course, treats it in depth. "Use bin $b$ only if bin $b-1$ is used" keeps one labelling of each choice of used bins, which removes some of the copies a search would otherwise explore. It changes no optimal count.


How many bins are offered? The guide offers one bin per item, always enough. The program does that for the guide's data:



Four bins, as the guide reports, though its packing differs: several packings tie. Two other numbers sit beside the MIP. The lower bound $\lceil \sum_i s_i / C \rceil$ is the total size divided by one bin's capacity, rounded up: $\lceil 370 / 100 \rceil = 4$. No packing can use fewer bins. First fit decreasing (FFD) is the classic heuristic: sort the items largest first, and put each into the first bin with room, opening a new bin when none has room.



When the heuristic's count equals the lower bound, the heuristic is proved optimal, and no MIP is needed. When they differ, either one may be off, and the MIP settles it. A small case where FFD loses:



FFD packs the two 3s together (6 of 7) and then has no room to place the 2s well. The MIP pairs each 3 with two 2s.


Now GridCap: 26 distribution and vegetation projects of the small portfolio, each a bundle of crew-months one crew does whole, packed into 12-month contract seasons.



74 crew-months need at least $\lceil 74 / 12 \rceil = 7$ seasons, and FFD finds 7, so 7 contract crews it is, proved by the bound alone. The MIP agrees, with a different packing: FFD fills six seasons and leaves the seventh nearly empty, while the MIP's packing happens to spread the load. Both count 7, which is all the objective asks. If planners also want the loads even, that is a new objective, and only the MIP can take it.

The Model


The three models of this lesson, in full.


Assignment (SimpleLinearSumAssignment; the MIP of crew_to_site.py). Sets. $W$, the crews; $T$, the projects, with $|W| = |T|$; $E \subseteq W \times T$, the allowed pairs (a field crew may not lead a transmission project). Parameters. $c_{w,t}$, the cost ($k) for crew $w$ to lead project $t$: rate times crew-months, plus travel. Variables. $x_{w,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $(w, t) \in E$.



With teams (teams_flow.py), add $\sum_{w \in g} \sum_t x_{w,t} \le 2$ for each team $g$ (team$_g$); with more workers than tasks, the worker rows become $\le 1$.


Knapsack (KnapsackSolver; season_knapsack.py). Sets. $I$, the distribution and vegetation projects. Parameters. $v_i$, NPV ($k); $m_i$, crew-months; $S = 12$, the season. Variables. $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$.



Bin packing (bin_packing.py). Sets. $I$, the items; $B = \{0, \dots, K-1\}$, the bins offered. Parameters. $s_i$, item size; $C$, bin capacity. Variables. $x_{i,b} \in \{0, 1\}$ (item $i$ in bin $b$); $y_b \in \{0, 1\}$ (bin $b$ used).



Summary


  • LinearSumAssignment matches each worker to one task at least cost. Integer costs, one arc per allowed pair, right_mate for the answer. On GridCap's crews it matched the MIP and the LP relaxation: $752k.
  • The table must be square: pad it with dummy tasks. Five workers and four tasks is INFEASIBLE until a cost-0 "no task" column is added; then 265, as the MIP finds.
  • A side rule moves assignment to a min-cost flow or a MIP. Team caps fit a flow with a node per team (250); task sizes need a MIP, a generalized assignment.
  • KnapsackSolver picks yes/no items under capacity rows, and its type must fit the rows. Integer data, weights as one list per row; one-row types stop the process on two rows.
  • Bin packing minimizes the number of bins, and a lower bound can prove a heuristic optimal. $\lceil \sum s_i / C \rceil$ against first fit decreasing; GridCap's work needs 7 crews, proved by the bound.

Design Note: Keep the MIP Next to the Special-Purpose Solver


A special-purpose solver is a promise about the problem's shape: square, integer, one row, no side rules. Requirements change, and the shape changes with them. Keep the MIP of the same problem in the code base with a test that compares the two on a few instances, as crew_to_site.py and season_knapsack.py do. When a new rule arrives that the dedicated solver cannot state, the MIP is already there, already trusted, and the switch is a one-line change at the call site.
