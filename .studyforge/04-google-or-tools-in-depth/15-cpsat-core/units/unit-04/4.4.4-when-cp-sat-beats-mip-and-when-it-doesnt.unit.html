  16. Choosing between CP-SAT and a MIP solver

GridCap now has two solvers that can take its selection model: HiGHS through MathOpt, which proves optimality with LP bounds (lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap), and CP-SAT, which searches over integers with propagation and clause learning (lesson Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers). Lesson The problem zoo: LP, MILP, CP, NLP and how to pick said CP-SAT "shines on scheduling, sequencing and dense logic" and promised a measurement. This lesson makes it, on three GridCap-style models chosen because they pull in different directions: the one-period selection, a vegetation crew's trimming order, and the ten-year selection of GridCap medium.

A comparison between solvers is easy to get wrong, so the measurements follow three rules. Each solver gets a deterministic work limit instead of a time limit (HiGHS a number of branch-and-bound nodes; CP-SAT one search worker, or a deterministic portfolio, and a limit in deterministic time, a work measure the solver keeps "as close as possible to a second"), so a run gives the same answer every time and on every machine; each run is repeated once to show it. The programs print statuses, values and bounds, never wall-clock times, which change from machine to machine and run to run. And the conclusion is about these models: the lesson ends with how to measure your own.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio, generated for this course; the trimming jobs below are a seeded random draw in its style.

The Model

Three models, two of them from earlier lessons.

The one-period selection is lesson Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers's model, unchanged.

The crew's trimming order. One vegetation crew trims $n$ circuits, one at a time.

  • Sets. $J = \{1, \dots, n\}$, the circuits.
  • Parameters. $d_j$, the days circuit $j$ takes; $u_j$, its due day; $w_j$, its risk weight (how much a day's lateness costs); $H = \sum_j d_j$, the horizon in days.
  • Variables. $s_j \in \{0, \dots, H\}$, the day trimming starts; $\ell_j \in [0, H]$, its lateness in days; $y_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}$ for each pair $j < k$, 1 if $j$ is trimmed before $k$.
  • Objective. $\min \sum_{j \in J} w_j\, \ell_j$, the risk-weighted lateness.
  • Constraints.

Each pair is a disjunction: one circuit or the other goes first. For HiGHS, each implication is a big-M row: $s_j + d_j \le s_k + H(1 - y_{jk})$ and $s_k + d_k \le s_j + H\, y_{jk}$. A big $M$ has to be at least the most the row could be broken by when it is switched off, which comes from bounds in the data. Here the crew never needs to wait, so every circuit can be finished by day $H$; then $s_j + d_j - s_k \le H$, and $M = H$ is enough to switch either row off. (Lesson Deriving big-M from bounds, in section 6, derives such bounds, and lesson Disjunctions: big-M vs. hull treats disjunctions, in depth.) For CP-SAT it is the row with only_enforce_if(y) or only_enforce_if(~y) (lesson Native logic: AddBoolOr, AddImplication, OnlyEnforceIf). In CP-SAT, $\ell_j$ is an integer from 0 to $H$; it is a whole number at the optimum anyway, because every $d_j$ and $u_j$ is.

The ten-year selection is the multi-period timing model of lesson Indicator constraints on GridCap medium: start-time binaries $x_{i,t}$, 1 if project $i$ starts in year $t$, for each project and allowed start year, at most one start per project and exactly one for a must-do, at most one project per alternative group, children starting at least their lag after their parents, and a budget row per year, maximizing NPV by start year. Lesson Indicator constraints states it in full (without that lesson's reserve rows), and lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, in section 6, builds it step by step; it has no logic beyond what those rows say.

Disjunctions and Sequencing Favor CP-SAT


The crew's trimming order, for 8, 10 and 12 circuits. HiGHS gets 10,000 nodes; CP-SAT one worker and 10 units of deterministic time. The two models differ only in how each disjunction is written:








With 8 circuits both prove the optimum. With 10, HiGHS finds the optimal order (133) but after 10,000 nodes its bound is still 0: it cannot rule out a plan with no lateness at all. With 12 it has not found the best order (176 against 167) and its bound is 3. CP-SAT proves every optimum within its limit. Both runs repeat exactly.


The bound tells you why. In the LP relaxation of the big-M model, $y_{jk}$ may be $0.5$, and then both rows read $s_j + d_j \le s_k + H/2$ and $s_k + d_k \le s_j + H/2$: with $H$ as large as all the trimming days together, both hold with every circuit starting on day 0. The relaxation says "no lateness is possible", so its bound is near 0 and branching has to fix many $y$ before the bound rises. This is the weak relaxation a big-M row gives, at its worst (lesson What a loose M does to the LP bound and to numerics, in section 6, measures what a loose $M$ does to the LP bound): the rule is nothing but disjunctions. CP-SAT does not rely on that relaxation. Once a few orders are decided, propagation narrows every start day's range, and each dead end teaches it a clause that rules out a whole family of orders.


So reach for CP-SAT when the model is mostly logic and disjunctions: sequencing, scheduling with "this or that first", many if-then rules, all-different assignments. Lessons Interval variables and NoOverlap and Cumulative resources: crews and outage windows go further with CP-SAT's interval variables and add_no_overlap, built for exactly this crew problem.

Large Models With Strong LP Bounds Favor a MIP Solver


The ten-year selection of GridCap medium is the other kind of model: hundreds of yes/no decisions tied together by a few budget rows: a multi-dimensional knapsack (yes/no items under several capacity rows) with timing (lesson 0-1 and multi-dimensional knapsack, in section 6, treats knapsacks in depth). Its LP relaxation is close to the integer optimum, which is exactly what branch-and-bound needs. The model is written once, and build(portfolio, api) creates it in either API:




HiGHS gets 2,000 nodes. CP-SAT gets 10 units of deterministic time twice: once with one worker, and once with 8 workers running an interleaved portfolio, a parameter (interleave_search) that sat_parameters.proto describes as deterministic whatever the number of workers (lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs):



HiGHS ends within 0.05% of its bound. A single CP-SAT worker ends 3% away, with both a worse plan and a weaker bound. The 8-worker portfolio closes most of that, to 0.12%, but HiGHS on its single search is still closer. The limits are in different units (nodes, deterministic seconds), so this is not a race with a winner; it is the shape of the result that matters. On a large model whose LP relaxation is strong, an LP-based MIP solver gets its bound from the relaxation directly, while CP-SAT has to earn it, and does so mainly through its LP-based workers (the Primer's chapter on the search describes them), which is why one worker does much worse than a portfolio.


Also part of "when it doesn't": the free LP bound. HiGHS's best bound comes from LP relaxations at every node, and it reports duals for an LP (lesson Shadow prices, reduced costs and ranging from a solver). CP-SAT reports a bound, but no duals, no reduced costs, and no LP relaxation you can inspect.


Continuous Amounts Need MathOpt or Scaling


The third limit is the type of variable. CP-SAT's model has no continuous variable at all; its variable makers are



(intervals aside, lesson Interval variables and NoOverlap). A model with a genuinely continuous quantity, an amount of money borrowed, a flow, an LP's dual, belongs in MathOpt. MathOpt can still hand such a model to CP-SAT: lesson Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers's credit line, turned into any amount from $0k to $2,000k at 0.6 $k of cost per $k borrowed, solved by both:






Same answer. The log line shows how: before searching, CP-SAT's MathOpt backend turned the model into a pure integer one, scaling the continuous variable and the float coefficient. Here that is harmless, because the best amount is a whole number of $k anyway. On a model where continuous quantities carry the structure (a network flow, a blend), that conversion is approximate by nature, and an LP-based solver handles the continuous part directly; keep such models on HiGHS, SCIP or Gurobi, and use CP-SAT where the decisions are discrete.


Measure on Your Own Model Before You Choose


The three results give a rule of thumb, and no more:

When the model is in between, measure, and measure fairly:


  1. Write the model once and build it for each solver, as multi_period.py does, so you compare solvers rather than two different models.
  2. Use deterministic limits (a node limit, CP-SAT's deterministic time with one worker or an interleaved portfolio) or no limit at all, and repeat each run to confirm it repeats.
  3. Compare statuses, values and bounds, on several instances of the size you will run in production; a single instance can mislead.
  4. Check the plans against each other: if two solvers prove different optima, one of the models is wrong.


Lesson Honest benchmarking: instance sets, seeds, time-to-gap, shifted geometric mean, in section 5, turns this into a benchmark harness with seeds and time-to-gap; this lesson's job is to know which way to lean before you start.


Summary


  • On a plain selection both prove the same optimum. For GridCap's selection, choose by modeling style; CP-SAT's logic constraints read like the rules.
  • Disjunctions and sequencing favor CP-SAT. A big-M disjunction's LP bound is near 0; CP-SAT proves optima that HiGHS cannot within the same kind of limit.
  • Large models with strong LP bounds favor a MIP solver. On GridCap medium's ten-year selection, HiGHS ends closest to proven; one CP-SAT worker is far behind, a portfolio much closer.
  • Continuous amounts need MathOpt or scaling. CP-SAT has no continuous variable; its MathOpt backend scales such a model to integers.
  • Measure on your own model before you choose. One model, deterministic limits, statuses and bounds, several instances.

Design Note: Keep the Model Portable Until You Have Measured


multi_period.py builds one model for both APIs from a dozen lines. That is cheap insurance: the choice of solver is a measurement, not a belief, and the measurement changes when the data grows or a rule is added. Keep the model in a form that can reach both (MathOpt does this for MIP solvers and CP-SAT alike, lesson Switching solvers: HiGHS, SCIP, CP-SAT, GLOP, Gurobi) until the numbers on your own instances settle the choice; then use the chosen solver's own strengths, such as CP-SAT's only_enforce_if or HiGHS's duals, deliberately.
