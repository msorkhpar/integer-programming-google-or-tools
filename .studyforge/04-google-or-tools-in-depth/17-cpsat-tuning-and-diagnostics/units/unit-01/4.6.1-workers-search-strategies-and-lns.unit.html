GridCap's planners keep a multi-period capital plan: each project starts in at most one year of the plan, and the money and crew-months it needs in each year of its work must fit that year's budget and crews. They have moved that plan to CP-SAT, OR-Tools' constraint solver for integer models (lessons Integer and Boolean variables, linear constraints over integers to When CP-SAT beats MIP, and when it doesn't teach its modeling API). The Model below states it in full, so this module needs nothing else; lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, later in the course, treats start-year models in depth. On the small portfolio (40 projects) CP-SAT proves the best plan almost at once. On the medium portfolio (400 projects, ten years, 887 start variables) it does not: stopped after a fixed amount of work, it reports a plan and a proof that no plan is more than 4.31% better, and the planners want that gap smaller without waiting longer. This lesson covers the three settings that change how CP-SAT spends its work: how many workers search at once, which search strategy a worker follows, and large neighborhood search (LNS), which improves a plan by re-solving part of it.

GridCap is a fictional utility's portfolio; none of its numbers are real.

The Model

Every program in this module solves the same model, built by cpsat_plan.build. It has one Boolean per project and allowed start year; each project starts at most once; every year's spending must fit that year's budget; and each program (transmission, distribution, vegetation) may use at most its own crew-months in a year, a hard row with no contractor to hire. Two lessons later in the course treat these pieces in depth: lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows the start-year variables and their spending rows, and lesson Deriving big-M from bounds crew rows that a hired contractor can switch off. CP-SAT works on integers only; GridCap's money is in whole $k and its crew needs in whole crew-months, so every coefficient below is already an integer.

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the plan's years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years project $i$ may use; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups, each a set of projects; $C \subseteq P$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$ years; $G$ the programs (transmission, distribution, vegetation).

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$, the NPV of project $i$ started in year $t$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, what project $i$ spends in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget of year $y$ ($k), multiplied by a scenario factor $s$ (1 unless a lesson says otherwise); $m_i$, the crew-months project $i$ needs in each year it is worked on; $K_{g,y}$, the crew-months program $g$ has in year $y$; $d_i$, the years of work of project $i$.

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for every $i \in P$ and $t \in T_i$: project $i$ starts in year $t$. In code, x[i, t], made with model.new_bool_var.

CP-SAT Runs a Portfolio of Subsolvers, One per Worker

A worker is a thread: one line of computation that the operating system can run on its own processor core. solver.parameters.num_workers sets how many CP-SAT starts. The parameters proto (the file that documents every CP-SAT setting, sat_parameters.proto) gives its meaning: 0, the default, means "use every core", and 1 means no parallelism.

What the extra workers do is the first thing to learn, because it is not what most people guess. A MIP solver that runs in parallel usually splits one search tree between its threads. CP-SAT does not split anything: it runs a portfolio, a set of different subsolvers (complete searches with different settings, plus heuristics), all working on the whole model at once and sharing what they find. Each new plan any of them finds becomes everyone's incumbent (the best plan known so far), and each better bound any of them proves becomes everyone's bound.

The program below solves GridCap medium twice with the same budget of work, first with one worker and then with eight. The work budget is max_deterministic_time, a limit in CP-SAT's own unit of work rather than in seconds; lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs explains it, and for now read "3.0" as "the same amount of search effort per worker".

Read the first line with lesson Best bound, incumbent, absolute and relative gap's terms. FEASIBLE is the status CP-SAT gives when it has a plan but has not proved it optimal (a limit stopped it). The bound is a number no plan can beat: CP-SAT has proved that every plan is worth at most $224,292k. The gap is how far the bound sits above the plan, as a share of the plan: $(224{,}292 - 215{,}024) / 215{,}024 = 4.31\%$. The true optimum lies somewhere in that range.

The eight-worker run closes the gap below 1%. Its exact plan, bound and gap change from one run to the next (lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs shows why), so the program prints only the test that came out the same in every run we made. The four lists under it come from CP-SAT's log (lesson Reading the CP-SAT log shows how to capture it) and they are the portfolio itself:

  • Full problem subsolvers each run a complete search that can prove optimality. The proto's subsolvers entry describes them: default_lp, no_lp and max_lp differ in how much of the model they copy into an LP relaxation (linearization level 1, 0 and 2); reduced_costs steers the search with LP information; quick_restart restarts often; core raises the bound the way MaxSAT solvers do (the proto's optimize_with_core).
  • First solution subsolvers only try to find some plan fast. fj is a feasibility jump heuristic, which the OR-Tools troubleshooting page lists among the 8-worker portfolio.
  • Interleaved subsolvers share the remaining threads, taking turns in short slices. Most of their names end in _lns: they are the large neighborhood searches of the last section of this lesson.
  • Helper subsolvers do bookkeeping, such as sharing solutions between the others.

With one worker there is room for one subsolver only. The log of a one-worker run says so: 1 full problem subsolver: [main] (lesson Reading the CP-SAT log). That is why the one-worker bound is so much weaker: max_lp, which builds the strongest LP relaxation, and core never run.

How many workers to use is a measured choice. The troubleshooting page recommends at least 8 for parallel search and adds subsolvers at 16, 24 and 32 workers. More workers than cores just makes them take turns on the same cores, so a server running several solves at once should give each solve fewer workers, not all of them. And whatever you choose, set it explicitly: the default of 0 means "every core of whatever machine this runs on", so a laptop and a 64-core server run different portfolios.

A Search Strategy Decides Which Variable to Fix Next and to Which Value

Inside one subsolver, a CP-SAT search is a sequence of decisions: pick a variable that is not fixed yet, give it a value, propagate what follows (for example, a project that just took the last of a year's budget forces every other start in that year to 0), and repeat. When a decision leads to a contradiction, the solver learns why and backs up. Two choices shape the search: which variable to decide next, and which value to try first. Together they are the search strategy.

solver.parameters.search_branching picks the strategy family. The proto lists, among others:

ValueWhat it does
AUTOMATIC_SEARCHthe default: the solver's own heuristics
FIXED_SEARCHfollow the order you give with add_decision_strategy
LP_SEARCHdecide using the LP relaxation's reduced costs
PSEUDO_COST_SEARCHdecide by how much past decisions moved the objective bound
PORTFOLIO_SEARCHa simple portfolio, the one LNS workers use

You give an order with model.add_decision_strategy(variables, variable_selection, value_selection). With cp_model.CHOOSE_FIRST the solver decides the first unfixed variable of your list; with cp_model.SELECT_MAX_VALUE it tries the largest value first, which for a start variable means "start it". Four things in this table carry over to other models:

  • A strategy changes the plans, hardly the bound. The final plans range from $203,721k to $218,230k, while every bound sits between $224,291k and $224,303k. The bound comes from propagation and the LP relaxation, which every strategy shares.
  • Your own order is not automatically better. The greedy NPV order found the worst final plan: deciding the highest-NPV starts first is not the same as finding the most valuable combination of starts, and the default heuristics did better here.
  • add_decision_strategy alone changes nothing on one worker. The last line is the same as the first, number for number: under AUTOMATIC_SEARCH this run did not follow the order. FIXED_SEARCH is what makes the solver follow it. (With several workers, the proto says a fixed subsolver joins the portfolio when a strategy is given, so the order is tried alongside the others.)
  • Measure before you choose. LP_SEARCH was best on this model with this budget, and PSEUDO_COST_SEARCH behaved exactly like the default. Neither result transfers to another model without running it.

The CP-SAT Primer gives the same advice from its own experiments: a hand-written strategy mostly helps a weak model, and the defaults learn good decisions by themselves. In practice, the portfolio of the last section already runs six full searches side by side at 8 workers, reduced_costs (which steers with LP information) among them, which is one more reason to give CP-SAT workers before giving it a strategy. The proto also lists a pseudo_costs subsolver, but it is not among the six this 8-worker run printed.

LNS Frees Part of a Good Plan and Lets CP-SAT Rebuild It

Large neighborhood search (LNS) improves a plan you already have. Each step:

  1. Destroy: choose a neighborhood, a part of the problem to re-plan (here, the projects of two of GridCap's four regions).
  2. Fix the rest: every variable outside the neighborhood keeps its value in the current plan, with a constraint $x_{i,t} = \bar x_{i,t}$, where $\bar x$ is the current plan.
  3. Repair: solve the much smaller problem that is left, with a short work limit.
  4. Accept the result if it is better than the current plan, and repeat with another neighborhood.

Because each step searches a whole sub-model instead of trying one change at a time, one step looks at a huge number of nearby plans at once. Fixing variables to constants is what makes the sub-model small: every fixed variable is one decision fewer to search. Hinting the current plan (add_hint, lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility) tells the step where to start; lesson Hints, assumptions and SufficientAssumptionsForInfeasibility shows a hinted solve whose first plan is the hint itself.

Summary


  • CP-SAT runs a portfolio of subsolvers, one per worker. More workers add different searches, including the ones that raise the bound; set num_workers explicitly.
  • A search strategy decides which variable to fix next and to which value. add_decision_strategy needs search_branching = cp_model.FIXED_SEARCH to be followed on one worker, and a strategy changes the plans found far more than the bound.
  • LNS frees part of a good plan and lets CP-SAT rebuild it. Fix everything outside the neighborhood, hint the current plan, solve briefly, keep improvements.

Design Note: Why a Portfolio and Not a Split Tree


A MIP solver's tree search splits naturally: each thread takes open nodes. CP-SAT's search learns clauses (short facts like "these three starts cannot all be 1") from every conflict, and those facts are valid everywhere, so a single search that never splits gets stronger as it goes. Running different complete searches side by side, each learning in its own way and sharing solutions and bounds, keeps that strength while using more cores. The price is that adding workers does not divide the time by the number of workers, and that a run's result depends on which worker got there first (lesson Deterministic time, seeds and reproducible runs).
