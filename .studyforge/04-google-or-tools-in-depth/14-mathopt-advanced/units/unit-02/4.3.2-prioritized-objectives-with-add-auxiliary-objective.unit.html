  16. Prioritized objectives

GridCap's plan maximizes NPV, and finance asks a second question: among the plans with the best NPV, which one spends the least money? Then a sharper one: if we gave up at most 1% of NPV, how much less could we spend? Neither question has one objective. Each has two, in a strict order: NPV matters first, and spend only breaks ties, or near-ties, between plans that are equally good on NPV. This is a lexicographic (or hierarchical) objective, ordered the way a dictionary orders words: the first letter decides, and the second only matters when the first ties.

MathOpt can store such an objective in the model with add_auxiliary_objective. This lesson shows the API, which solvers in OR-Tools 9.15 accept it (in the runner, none), how to read the refusal, and how to get the same answer anyway by solving one objective at a time. Section 8 (lessons Hierarchical objectives with tolerances–Three implementations: auxiliary objectives in MathOpt, setObjectiveN in gurobipy, and a manual two-pass solve) treats lexicographic planning in depth; this lesson is about the MathOpt API. GridCap is a fictional utility.

The Model

The multi-period timing model of lesson Indicator constraints, with its objective replaced by two prioritized ones. Its decisions are start-time variables: $x_{i,t}= 1$ if project $i$ starts in year $t$ of its window $T_i$; a project starts at most once and spends its cost profile from its start year on. (Lesson Start-time variables x(i,t) and timing windows, in section 6, builds this model step by step.)

Sets. $P$ the projects; $Y$ the years; $T_i \subseteq Y$ the start years of project $i$; $M \subseteq P$ the must-do projects; $\mathcal{G}$ the alternative groups; $C$ the child projects, with parent $p(c)$ and lag $L_c$.

Parameters. $\text{npv}_{i,t}$ ($k); $c_{i,k}$, project $i$'s spend in its $k$-th year of work ($k); $B_y$, the budget ($k); $\varepsilon \ge 0$, the share of the best NPV the plan may give up (0 or 1% in this lesson).

Variables. $x_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ for $i \in P$, $t \in T_i$.

With $\text{spend}_y = \sum_{i \in P} \sum_{t \in T_i,\, t \le y} c_{i,\,y-t}\, x_{i,t}$:

Constraints.

Each Objective Has a Priority, and the Lowest Number Goes First

A MathOpt model always has one primary objective, model.objective, set by model.maximize or model.minimize. Any number of auxiliary objectives can be added next to it, each with an expression, a sense (maximize or minimize) and an integer priority:

CallAdds
model.add_auxiliary_objective(priority=p, expr=e, is_maximize=False, name="")an objective, minimized unless is_maximize=True
model.add_maximization_objective(e, priority=p, name="")the same, maximized
model.add_minimization_objective(e, priority=p, name="")the same, minimized

The objectives are optimized from the lowest priority number to the highest. The primary objective has priority 0 unless you change it with model.objective.priority = ..., so an auxiliary objective with priority 1 comes second. The number is an order, not a weight: priority 1 does not mean "one unit of importance", and priorities 1 and 100 rank the same way. The Objective docstring adds that a model with one objective ignores its priority, and that an auxiliary objective must be linear.

OR-Tools' own example of the API, hierarchical_objectives.py, adapted here, is a model with many optimal solutions and a second objective that picks one:

With $z = 1$ the row leaves $x + y \le 0.5$, and every split of that 0.5 between $x$ and $y$ gives the primary objective its best value, 2.5. The auxiliary objective says which split to return: as much $y$ as possible. Like constraints, an AuxiliaryObjective has an id, numbered from 0 in creation order, and its priority, is_maximize and name read back. Unlike indicator constraints, an objective can be edited: priority and is_maximize have setters, and model.delete_auxiliary_objective(aux) removes one.

In OR-Tools 9.15, Add a Second Auxiliary Objective Term by Term

The sample has one auxiliary objective. A model with two, written the obvious way, loses the first one's terms when the second is added:

After the second call, overtime_cost has no terms at all, and the model a solver would receive (export_model(), read back by variable id) has lost them too. Nothing raised. The cause is in OR-Tools' objectives.py: add_auxiliary_objective(expr=...) first calls the new objective's clear(), and in 9.15 AuxiliaryObjective.clear() clears the coefficients of every auxiliary objective, not only its own. The primary objective is not affected.

The way around it is to create each auxiliary objective without an expression, which skips clear(), and set its terms one at a time with set_linear_coefficient, which touches only that objective. in_order.add_auxiliary does that:

mathopt.LinearExpression(expr) flattens any linear expression into a dict of terms (terms) and a constant (offset). With one auxiliary objective, as in the rest of this lesson, the shortcut methods are safe; with two or more, add them term by term, and read them back to check.

A Tolerance Lets a Later Objective Buy Something From an Earlier One


With no tolerance, the second objective only breaks exact ties in the first. In a MIP like GridCap's, exact ties on NPV are rare, so the second objective would rarely change anything. The 1% question needs a degradation tolerance: how far the first objective may fall from its best value to make room for the second.


MathOpt sets it per objective, in ModelSolveParameters, the model-specific half of a solve's settings (lesson Solution hints and ModelSolveParameters covers its other fields). The ObjectiveParameters docstring in model_parameters.py defines it. A hierarchical solver takes the objectives in priority order; for each one $f^k$ it finds the best value $\Gamma^k$ subject to every constraint, and from then on treats a plan as acceptable for $f^k$ only if $f^k(x)$ is within tolerance of $\Gamma^k$:



When both tolerances are set, meeting either one is enough, so the allowed loss is the larger of the two. With neither set, the loss allowed is 0. The program asks for 1% on NPV:



objective_parameters is a dict keyed by the objective objects themselves, model.objective for the primary one and an AuxiliaryObjective for the others. ObjectiveParameters also takes objective_degradation_absolute_tolerance and a per-objective time_limit.


No Solver in the Runner Takes Auxiliary Objectives


The same sample model, sent to each MIP solver in the runner:






HiGHS, SCIP and CP-SAT refuse the model, each with a clear reason once it is read from __context__ (lesson Indicator constraints). The OR-Tools sample itself solves with Gurobi, a commercial solver that needs a license, and the Gurobi line is different: a RuntimeError with nothing on __context__, so refusal falls back to the error's own text. "bad_function_call" is a C++ error saying a function was called through an empty pointer: MathOpt reached for the Gurobi library and found none it could use. Section 5 covers Gurobi and its licenses; until then, no lesson needs it.


So in this course's runner, a model with an auxiliary objective cannot be solved by any solver as it stands. The API is still worth knowing: the model stores the priorities in one place, and on a machine with a supported Gurobi the same model solves unchanged. The sample reads the second objective's value with result.solutions[0].primal_solution.auxiliary_objective_values[aux].


Solve in Order: One Objective at a Time, Each Kept Within Its Tolerance


The docstring's definition is also a recipe any MIP solver can follow. For each objective in priority order: optimize it; read the best value $\Gamma$; add a row keeping that objective within its allowed loss of $\Gamma$; go on to the next. For a maximized objective the row is $f(x) \ge \Gamma - \text{loss}$, for a minimized one $f(x) \le \Gamma + \text{loss}$. in_order.py does exactly that, on a copy of the model so the caller's model is not changed:






Three details carry the method:


  • The copy. MathOpt models have no clone method in OR-Tools 9.15. export_model() writes the model as a protocol buffer (MathOpt's serialized form of a model), and Model.from_model_proto reads it back as a new model whose variables keep the same ids, so work.get_variable(t.variable.id) finds each original variable's twin.
  • The auxiliary objectives are deleted from the copy, or the solver would refuse it as above. Each objective is rebuilt as the copy's single objective from its linear_terms() and offset.
  • The sign of the kept row follows the sense: a maximized objective may fall by the loss, a minimized one may rise by it, and abs(best) keeps a relative tolerance right when the best value is negative.


On the sample, with the tie broken both ways:



HiGHS alone happened to return the split with all of the 0.5 on $y$. That was its choice among equally good answers, and nothing in the model asked for it. Solved in order, the auxiliary objective decides: maximize $y$ and it is 0.5, minimize it and it is 0, while the primary value stays 2.5 both times. Those are the values the OR-Tools sample states for Gurobi's solve: primary 2.5, secondary 0.5.


On GridCap, with NPV first and total spend second:




Read the three rows in turn:


  • Exact. The plan has the best NPV, 24,260, and of all plans with that NPV it spends the least, 41,630.
  • 1% tolerance. NPV may fall to $24{,}260 \times 0.99 = 24{,}017.4$. The plan found gives up $229k of NPV (0.94%) and spends $1,190k less, with one project fewer. That trade is the answer to finance's sharper question, and a planner can now decide whether it is worth taking.
  • Swapped priorities. With the NPV objective's priority set to 2, spend comes first. The least spend is the must-do projects alone, 4 projects and 7,630, and NPV only picks among plans that spend exactly that. Priorities are not weights: whatever comes first wins outright.

Summary


  • Each objective has a priority, and the lowest number goes first. The primary objective has priority 0; add_maximization_objective(expr, priority=1) adds a second one.
  • In OR-Tools 9.15, add a second auxiliary objective term by term. Adding one with an expression erases the terms of every auxiliary objective already in the model.
  • A tolerance lets a later objective buy something from an earlier one. ModelSolveParameters(objective_parameters={obj: ObjectiveParameters(...)}); with both tolerances set, the larger allowed loss applies.
  • No solver in the runner takes auxiliary objectives. HiGHS, SCIP and CP-SAT refuse them, with the reason on __context__; Gurobi cannot be loaded in the runner.
  • Solve in order: one objective at a time, each kept within its tolerance. On GridCap a 1% NPV tolerance bought $1,190k less spend for $229k of NPV.

Design Note: Store the Priorities in the Model, Solve Them Where You Can


npv_then_spend.py builds one model that says everything about the question, objectives and tolerances included, and leaves how to solve it to one function. On a solver that takes auxiliary objectives, that function is one mathopt.solve call; in the runner it is solve_in_order. The model code does not change between the two. Keep the question in the model and the workaround in the solve, and the workaround can be deleted the day it is no longer needed.
